=== place/varanasi
title: Varanasi
type: place
date: c. 800 BCE
themes: religion, art, kinship
src: 21fd9ea4aaac
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Varanasi, also called Benares or Kashi, is a city on the Ganges in Uttar Pradesh, India, continuously settled since about the 8th century BCE and the most important Hindu pilgrimage and cremation center.
--- fact 1
Varanasi lies on the left bank of the Ganges in Uttar Pradesh, and excavations at Rajghat show settlement from about the 8th century BCE.
--- fact 2
Hindu tradition holds that dying at Varanasi and being cremated beside the Ganges gives release from the cycle of rebirth, so families bring their dead from across India to cremation sites such as Manikarnika, and more than 80 bathing and cremation steps (ghats) line the river.
--- fact 3
The Buddha is traditionally said to have given his first sermon at Sarnath, near Varanasi, in about the 5th century BCE; Sarnath has an Ashokan pillar and Gupta-period sculpture.
--- fact 4
Varanasi has been a center of Sanskrit learning, and Banaras Hindu University was founded in 1916; the city is also known for silk weaving.
--- fact 5
The Kashi Vishwanath temple was destroyed and rebuilt several times, and the present temple dates from 1780; the adjacent Gyanvapi Mosque and the history of the site are the subject of continuing legal disputes.
--- fact 6
Varanasi had about 1.2 million people at the 2011 census.
--- context
Varanasi grew up on the [[place/ganges-river|Ganges River]] in the world of the [[event/vedic-period|Vedic Period]], was linked with the Buddhist state of the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]] and the [[culture/gupta-empire|Gupta Empire]] through Sarnath, and later came under the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]]. It sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Varanasi is a place where the grief-rite is the local institution: bodies from far away are brought to the riverbank because a meaning-engine teaches that death there brings release, and a supporting economy of priests, wood sellers and boatmen has formed around the practice. A Buddhist site, Hindu temples and a mosque within a few kilometers show several meaning-engines using one stretch of river, and the continuing dispute over one temple and mosque pair shows claim-lines still being drawn around old buildings. The reading would weaken if the cremation trade proves to be a small part of the city's economy compared with its other trades.
--- question 1
How large was Varanasi in the 1st millennium BCE, and how did it compare with other Ganges cities?
--- question 2
How many cremations take place at Varanasi each year?

=== place/venice
title: Venice
type: place
date: c. 500 CE
themes: trade, war, art
src: d4c9b88ad794
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Venice is a city on islands in a lagoon at the head of the Adriatic Sea in northeastern Italy, settled from about the 5th to 6th century CE and, as an independent republic until 1797, a leading maritime and trading power of the Mediterranean.
--- fact 1
Tradition dates Venice's founding on lagoon islands to 25 March 421, but no surviving records deal with it directly; the date_start of 500 is a round placeholder for settlement in the 5th to 6th centuries.
--- fact 2
Nominally under Byzantine authority, Venice became self-governing under elected doges, dated by tradition from 697 and by historians from the early 8th century; about 810 the ducal seat moved to the Rialto.
--- fact 3
In 1204 a Crusade fleet supplied by Venice under Doge Enrico Dandolo sacked Constantinople, and Venice took a large share of the Byzantine lands, including Crete; wars with the Ottoman Empire later cost it Negroponte (1470), Cyprus (1570 to 1573) and Crete (1669).
--- fact 4
Venice opened a plague hospital on the island of Santa Maria di Nazareth in 1423, regarded as the first permanent lazaretto, and a second, Lazzaretto Nuovo, in 1468, and its isolation of arriving people and goods became an early model of quarantine.
--- fact 5
In 1516 the Republic required its Jewish residents to live in an enclosed district called the Ghetto, whose name later spread to other cities.
--- fact 6
The Great Council voted to end the Republic on 12 May 1797, as French forces approached; Austria received Venice under the Treaty of Campo Formio, and it joined the Kingdom of Italy in 1866.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Venice and its Lagoon in 1987; the historic center's population fell from about 175,000 in the 1950s to under 50,000, and the MOSE flood barriers were first raised in October 2020.
--- context
Venice was a partner and then a rival of [[place/constantinople|Constantinople]], took part in the [[event/crusades|Crusades]], and lost ground to the Ottomans after the [[event/fall-of-constantinople|Fall of Constantinople]] in 1453. Its lazaretto system followed the [[event/black-death|Black Death]], its painting and printing belong to the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]], and the Republic ended during the [[event/napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars]]. Venice sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Venice, a state with almost no land of its own, made its story-glue from the sea: elected doges, councils that limited any one family, and a yearly ceremony in which the doge symbolically wed the Adriatic. A port lives on strangers and cargo, and the administration that built the lazaretto also enclosed the Jewish district in 1516, so a system built to manage risk also confined people, which the Observer records as a harm to those held. The reading would weaken if Venice's institutions differed little from those of other Italian maritime republics such as Genoa.
--- question 1
How many people died in Venice in the plague outbreaks of 1348 and 1575 to 1577?
--- question 2
Can the lagoon city stay habitable and populated as sea level rises and the MOSE barriers age?

=== place/xingu-garden-cities
title: Xingu Garden Cities
type: place
date: c. 1200 – 1600
themes: kinship, technology, trade
src: a93d36c2cbff
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Xingu garden cities are pre-Columbian settlements in the upper Xingu region of Brazil, dating from about 1200 to 1600 CE, whose plazas, roads, and ditches were mapped by the anthropologist Michael Heckenberger with the Kuikuro people.
--- fact 1
Researchers documented about 28 towns, villages, and hamlets, which may have supported up to about 50,000 people.
--- fact 2
Larger towns had ditches about 3 meters deep and 10 meters wide, plazas up to 150 meters across, and roads aligned with the summer solstice.
--- fact 3
Surrounding fields grew manioc, and earthen dams and ponds supported fish farming.
--- fact 4
Population estimates are debated, and the descendants of the builders still live in the region.
--- context
See [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]]. The settlements declined after 1500, as epidemics followed the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the research was done together with the Kuikuro, whose descendants still live in the region. Living communities and archaeologists can inform each other.
--- question 1
How many people lived in the Xingu network at its height?

=== place/yellow-river
title: Yellow River
type: place
date: c. 6000 BCE
themes: earth-systems, war, technology
src: cb94e64b3762
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Yellow River (Huang He) is a river of northern China, about 5,460 kilometers long, whose loess-laden floodplain was a center of early farming and of the Shang and Zhou states, and whose floods and course changes have shaped Chinese history.
--- fact 1
The Yellow River rises in the Bayan Har Mountains of Qinghai and flows through northern China to the Bohai Sea.
--- fact 2
Farming communities growing millet are documented in the Yellow River basin from about the 7th to 6th millennia BCE, and the Shang and Zhou states arose in its middle and lower reaches.
--- fact 3
The Yellow River carries a heavy load of loess sediment from the Loess Plateau, which has raised its bed above the surrounding plain in the lower reaches and made dikes necessary; records list many breaches and several major changes of course.
--- fact 4
From 1194 until 1855 the river mostly ran south of the Shandong Peninsula into the Yellow Sea, until a breach in 1855 returned it to a northern course.
--- fact 5
A flood in 1887 is estimated to have killed between about 900,000 and 2 million people.
--- fact 6
In 1938, during the Second Sino-Japanese War, Nationalist forces breached dikes at Huayuankou to slow the Japanese advance; several hundred thousand people died, with published estimates up to about 900,000, and millions were displaced.
--- context
The Yellow River is linked to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in northern China, to the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]] and the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] as states that organized flood control, and to the [[event/second-sino-japanese-war|Second Sino-Japanese War]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a river that floods without warning helped tie authority to water control, and the traditional story of Yu the Great, who is said to have tamed the floods and founded the first dynasty, is a story-glue joining rule to engineering. The 1938 breach shows the same river used as sanctioned harm by a state against an invading army, with civilians as its main victims. The image of the Yellow River as the sole cradle of Chinese civilization is itself a story, since sites on the Yangtze and elsewhere also show early farming and state formation. The hypothesis that flood control drove state formation is contested, and specialists could weaken this reading by showing that other factors mattered more.
--- question 1
How large was the death toll of the 1938 flood, and how many people were displaced?
--- question 2
How much did flood control contribute to early state formation compared with warfare and trade?

observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Al-Andalus was the Muslim-ruled part of the Iberian Peninsula from 711 to 1492, a region of Muslim, Christian, and Jewish communities that contributed to science, architecture, and literature.

Muslim forces crossed from North Africa into Iberia in 711.

Al-Andalus grew from the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]] and was a channel for the knowledge of the [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]] into Latin Europe. --- observer
The Observer notes that the same year saw the fall of Granada, the expulsion of Jews from Spain, and the departure of Columbus. A period of pluralism ended as another began that would reshape the planet.

=== culture/ayutthaya-kingdom
title: Ayutthaya Kingdom
type: culture
date: 1351 – 1767
themes: trade, war, religion
src: ad35add7b6f8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Ayutthaya Kingdom was a Tai state in central Thailand that lasted from 1351 to 1767, grew into a major trading power that hosted merchants from Asia and Europe, and ended when Burmese armies destroyed its capital.
--- fact 1
The kingdom was founded in 1351 by King Ramathibodi I, also known as U Thong. In foreign accounts it was called Siam, while its own people called themselves Tai and the kingdom Krung Tai.
--- fact 2
It became a major regional power and a trading centre where European merchants and Asian traders met. King Narai the Great began diplomatic relations with France during the reign of Louis XIV.
--- fact 3
The capital fell to Burmese forces on 7 April 1767 after a siege of fourteen months, and the city was almost completely destroyed. The siege belonged to the Burmese-Siamese War of 1765 to 1767, in which King Hsinbyushin of the Konbaung dynasty sent armies against Ayutthaya.
--- fact 4
The kingdom's lifespan is usually given as 417 years. A new capital was established at Thonburi afterwards.
--- context
Ayutthaya grew in the region formerly dominated by the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]], and its long rivalry with Burma continued from the era of the [[culture/pagan-kingdom|Pagan Kingdom]]. Its port trade links it to [[culture/malacca-sultanate|Malacca Sultanate]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a city that knew itself by one name and was recorded abroad by another, which shows how a people's own label and an outsider's may diverge for centuries. The city's destruction, after fourteen months of siege, is recorded as harm to its inhabitants as well as to its monuments.
--- question 1
How many people lived in Ayutthaya at its height, and how many died or were carried off in 1767?
--- question 2
How much of Ayutthaya's wealth came from the trade in the port compared with the surrounding rice lands?

=== culture/botai-culture
title: Botai Culture
type: culture
date: c. 3700 – 3100 BCE
themes: technology, kinship, trade
src: ffcc2687c0ec
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Botai culture was a society of hunter-herders in northern Kazakhstan, from roughly 3700 to 3100 BCE, who used horses for meat, milk, and probably riding.
--- fact 1
Pottery residues show that Botai people used mare's milk by about 3500 BCE, and horse bones show signs of management.
--- fact 2
A 2018 study in Science found that Botai horses were not ancestors of modern domestic horses.
--- fact 3
Instead, they were ancestors of Przewalski's horses, which are therefore feral descendants of early domesticated horses and not truly wild.
--- fact 4
Modern domestic horses derive from a different lineage.
--- context
The Botai case is central to debates about [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] and the [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. See [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the first known horse keepers were not the ancestors of the horses that later carried armies. The first domestication seems to have been a dead end.
--- question 1
Where and when did the ancestors of modern domestic horses first come under human control?

The Casarabe culture was a society of the Llanos de Mojos savanna in the Bolivian Amazon, active from about 500 to 1400 CE, whose monumental settlements were revealed by lidar in 2022.

=== culture/champa
title: Champa
type: culture
date: c. 192 CE – 1832
themes: religion, art, trade, war
src: c98553fb0ad6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Champa was a group of Cham states along the coast of central and southern Vietnam, dated from 192 CE, that built Hindu temples at My Son, traded across the South China Sea, and was gradually conquered by Vietnam until its last remnant was annexed in 1832.
--- fact 1
Champa is usually dated from 192 CE, when the Cham clans of central and southern Vietnam were unified into a single state. The kingdom later broke into separate principalities.
--- fact 2
Hinduism, probably brought by Indian merchants, was adopted early and mixed with local traditions and with Buddhist, Khmer, and Chinese influences. By the end of the 10th century Muslim traders had brought Islam.
--- fact 3
My Son is a group of Shaiva Hindu temples in central Vietnam, built between the 4th and 13th centuries by Champa's kings.
--- fact 4
Vijaya was the capital from 938 until 1471, when the Vietnamese ruler Le Thanh Tong (Lê Thánh Tông) took it and annexed most of the country. One account gives 40,000 Chams killed and 30,000 taken prisoner; these numbers are not independently confirmed.
--- fact 5
The remaining principality of Panduranga lasted until 1832, when Emperor Minh Mang began the final conquest and annexed the last of Champa.
--- context
Champa was a neighbour and rival of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] and its temples at [[place/angkor|Angkor]], and took part in the sea trade of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a people who took a meaning-engine from across the sea and made it local, and who were absorbed over centuries by a neighbour to the north. The loss of 1471 is recorded as harm: a capital taken, people killed or carried off, and a state reduced to a remnant that lasted another three and a half centuries.
--- question 1
How reliable are the reported casualty figures for the fall of Vijaya?
--- question 2
How did Cham communities that survive today (Hindu, Muslim, Buddhist) keep their identity after annexation?

--- summary

--- context
The Chinchorro's practice is the earliest known artificial mummification, thousands of years before the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]]. See [[observer-note/why-the-species-buries-its-dead|Why the Species Buries Its Dead]] and [[region/andes|Andes]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a culture of coastal foragers invested in preserving the dead, including children, before farming or cities. The grief-rite is older than the state.
--- question 1
Why did the Chinchorro begin to preserve their dead?

=== culture/ghana-empire
title: Ghana Empire
type: culture
date: c. 800 CE – 1240
themes: trade, war
src: 84c6bd9a1a00
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Ghana Empire, called Wagadu by its own people, was a Soninke state in the western Sahel whose wealth rested on the trans-Saharan trade in gold, salt, and ivory. Its exact extent, its end, and the role of outside conquest are debated.
--- fact 1
The empire lay in what is now southeastern Mauritania, western Mali, and eastern Senegal. Most scholars accept an origin in the 8th or 9th century.
--- fact 2
The Soninke people formed its foundation. Its wealth was linked to the growth of regular trans-Saharan trade in gold, salt, and ivory, which supported larger towns and territorial expansion to control trade routes.
--- fact 3
The capital is believed to have been at Koumbi Saleh, on the edge of the Sahara. The description left by al-Bakri in 1067 or 1068 says the capital consisted of two towns joined by continuous habitations.
--- fact 4
Some accounts say the capital was sacked about 1076 by the Almoravids of North Africa, perhaps in retaliation for Ghana's attempt to control trade held by Saharan commercial centres.
--- fact 5
The empire declined in the 12th century, when competing trade routes opened further east and a prolonged dry spell hurt agriculture. Tradition says the city was destroyed by Sundiata in 1240 and what remained was absorbed into the Mali Empire.
--- context
Ghana was the first of the large Sahelian states that the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]] and later the [[culture/songhai-empire|Songhai Empire]] followed. Its story is tied to the spread of Islam through the Sahara; see [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]].
--- observer
The Observer notes an exchange-web whose wealth was carried on paths and rainfall, and which, on these accounts, came apart through shifting routes and drought more than through a single blow. A network that looks like a state is only as fixed as the roads and climate under it.
--- question 1
Did the Almoravids actually conquer Ghana, or is that a later simplification of a slower decline?
--- question 2
How much of what is known about the capital comes from outside observers, and how much from excavation?

--- fact 1
Around 1396 the Bulala forced Mai Umar Idrismi to abandon Njimi and move the Kanembu people to Bornu, on the western side of Lake Chad. Over time intermarriage between the Kanembu and the peoples of Bornu produced a new people and language, the Kanuri.

The empire peaked under Mai Idris Alooma (about 1571 to 1603), remembered for military skill, administrative reforms, and Islamic piety. --- observer
The Observer notes a state that survived an exile by moving its centre of gravity, and whose people fused with those who had displaced them into a new people. Where the political story-glue failed at one capital, the kin-lattice held it together long enough to build another.

=== culture/kingdom-of-benin

type: culture
date: c. 1200 – 1897
themes: art, war, trade
src: 9e7e4a77d0eb
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Kingdom of Benin was a West African forest state of the Edo people, ruled by an oba from Benin City, that flourished from about the 13th century until British forces conquered it in 1897.
--- fact 1
The kingdom flourished from the 13th to the 19th century in the forest region of West Africa, in what is now southern Nigeria. In tradition, the Edo invited Prince Oranmiyan of Ife to rule in the 13th century, and his son Eweka became the first oba.
--- fact 2
Under Oba Ewuare the Great (reigned about 1440 to 1480), the kingdom expanded and became highly organized, with guilds of craftsmen. He built up the army, rebuilt the royal compound, and gave Benin City moats and great walls.
--- fact 3
In late December 1896 a party of nine British colonial officials led by James Robert Phillips set out for Benin City. On 4 January 1897 Edo chiefs ambushed and killed them near the village of Ugbine.
--- fact 4
A British force of about 1,200 men under Sir Harry Rawson carried out the Benin Expedition from 9 to 18 February 1897. It occupied the capital after sustained resistance, burned parts of the city and palace, and removed large quantities of sacred and royal art.
--- fact 5
The looted works, known as the Benin Bronzes, were sold to defray the costs of the expedition. Oba Ovonramwen was captured and exiled to Calabar, and the kingdom was incorporated into British Nigeria.
--- context
The conquest of Benin belongs to the wider [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]]. The kingdom was also a contemporary of [[culture/kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]] and traded with Europeans during the period of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]].
--- observer
The Observer records a state's sacred and royal art taken by force and sold to pay for the force that took it. Where such objects are held is itself a claim about who may tell the story they carry, and the continuing argument over their display shows how long a seizure can outlast the event.

--- question 2
