=== person/sergei-eisenstein
title: Sergei Eisenstein
type: person
date: 1898 – 1948
themes: art, technology
src: 55ba2699e597
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sergei Eisenstein (1898 to 1948) was a Soviet film director and theorist, born in Riga, who argued that meaning came from the collision between shots rather than any single image. His Odessa Steps massacre sequence in *Battleship Potemkin* (1925) remains one of cinema's most studied passages.
--- fact 1
Eisenstein was born in Riga in 1898, son of a civil engineer, and trained in engineering and architecture before turning to the arts.
--- fact 2
The Russian Revolution pushed him into theatre; he joined Meyerhold's Proletkult Theatre before moving into film in the early 1920s.
--- fact 3
His second feature, *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), made for the twentieth anniversary of the 1905 Revolution, brought him international fame.
--- fact 4
In *Strike* (1925) he pioneered "intellectual montage," cutting colliding images together and favoring crowds and non-professional "types" over trained stars.
--- fact 5
Invited to Hollywood then Mexico in the early 1930s, both projects went unfinished, and his 1937 Soviet film *Bezhin Meadow* was suppressed and destroyed.
--- fact 6
He regained official favor with *Alexander Nevsky* (1938), scored by Sergei Prokofiev, extending his editing style to sound and mass combat.
--- fact 7
He spent his final years on *Ivan the Terrible*; Part I won a Stalin Prize, but Stalin banned Part II, and Eisenstein died of a heart attack in Moscow in 1948 just after finishing it.
--- fact 8
His writings, including *Film Form* and *The Film Sense*, became foundational film-studies texts, and he is ranked among cinema's most important directors and theorists.
--- context
Eisenstein worked in the Soviet Union, within [[region/europe|Europe]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]; the [[event/russian-revolution|Russian Revolution]] redirected him from engineering into theatre and film, and his breakthrough film commemorated the revolutionary struggle before it. His staged battles and massacres connect him to [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer finds a subject who treated the edit itself as an argument: meaning, he held, was made not inside a single image but in the collision between two, an engineered story-glue built into a film's machinery. His preference for crowds and "type"-cast non-professionals over trained performers made the collective, not the individual, the vehicle for a revolutionary meaning-engine. The staged violence recurring through his films, a factory massacre, a staircase shooting, a battle on ice, reads as a fascination with sanctioned harm as spectacle. That the same state whose myths he helped build also destroyed one film and banned another shows how uneasily a master of persuasion sat within the system he served.
--- question 1
Since the negative of *Bezhin Meadow* (1937) was destroyed after its suppression, what the finished film actually contained is now unknown.

=== person/sesshu-toyo
title: Sesshū Tōyō
type: person
date: 1420 – 1506
themes: art, religion
src: 75b2ac5937a7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sesshū Tōyō (1420 to 1506) was a Japanese Zen monk and painter who studied Chinese ink painting in Ming China and made it a Japanese art. He is regarded as the greatest master of Japanese ink painting.
--- fact 1
Sesshū was born in 1420 in Bitchū Province (present-day Okayama Prefecture) and entered a Zen temple as a boy. He studied painting at Shōkoku-ji in Kyoto with the monk-painter Tenshō Shūbun.
--- fact 2
About the 1460s he took the name Sesshū ("snow boat") and settled in Yamaguchi under the patronage of the Ōuchi family.
--- fact 3
In 1467 he joined a trade mission to Ming China and spent about two to three years there (sources differ), travelling to Beijing and receiving an honorary title at the Tiantong monastery near Ningbo.
--- fact 4
He returned to Japan about 1469, as the Ōnin War was tearing Kyoto apart, and worked in the provinces from his studio, the Unkoku-an, in Yamaguchi. He is credited with designing several temple gardens and died in 1506.
--- fact 5
He painted in suibokuga, black ink and water on paper or silk, including the loose haboku ("broken ink") manner. Key works include Haboku Landscape (1495), Huike Offering His Arm to Bodhidharma (1496) and View of Ama-no-Hashidate (about 1501 to 1506).
--- fact 6
Six of his works are designated National Treasures of Japan, more than any other painter.
--- fact 7
The Unkoku school continued his studio, and Hasegawa Tōhaku styled himself Sesshū's fifth-generation heir. In 1956 the World Peace Council named him one of its "great cultural figures".
--- context
Sesshū's travel to the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] linked his work to [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] beyond Japan during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a tool-lineage of brush and ink that crossed the sea as a Chinese import in Zen monasteries and became a Japanese possession, carried by a monk on a trade mission, an exchange-web moving skill as well as goods. Later schools claiming descent from him, and a painter who styled himself fifth-generation heir, show ancestor-weight running the other way: a lineage can be asserted after the fact. Which of the works attributed to him are his own is not settled.
--- question 1
Which of the works attributed to Sesshū are by his own hand?

=== person/shen-zhou
title: Shen Zhou
type: person
date: 1427 – 1509
themes: art
src: da4b8c94a50d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Shen Zhou (1427 to 1509) was a scholar-painter of Ming-dynasty China who founded the Wu School in Suzhou. He never held office, and his landscapes combine painting, poetry and calligraphy as a record of friendship and quiet life.
--- fact 1
Shen Zhou was born in 1427 near Suzhou in the Jiangnan region, into a wealthy family of scholars, poets and collectors. His grandfather and father were painters, and the family avoided government service.
--- fact 2
He never sat the civil service examinations. When local officials recommended him for office he declined, saying he had to care for his widowed mother, who lived to almost 100.
--- fact 3
His home became a meeting place for Suzhou's intellectual elite, and many paintings were made as gifts for friends, marking birthdays, farewells or shared outings.
--- fact 4
He painted "in the manner of" the four great Yuan masters, Huang Gongwang, Wu Zhen, Ni Zan and Wang Meng. Almost every painting carries his own poem in his calligraphy.
--- fact 5
He was one of the first Chinese painters to make albums of real local places around Suzhou. Key works include Lofty Mount Lu (1467), Night Vigil (1492) and Poet on a Mountaintop (about 1500).
--- fact 6
Through his pupil Wen Zhengming the Wu School dominated Chinese painting in the 16th century. With Wen Zhengming, Tang Yin and Qiu Ying he is counted among the "Four Masters of the Ming Dynasty".
--- fact 7
According to one story, when forgers sold fake Shen Zhou paintings, he sometimes signed them to help them earn a living. He died in 1509, aged 82.
--- fact 8
Later theorists such as Dong Qichang placed him in the "Southern School" of literati painters, whom they judged superior to professional court artists.
--- context
Shen Zhou lived under the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter who refused office and made pictures for friends, so that art served as a currency of obligation and affection rather than of sale. His habit of painting in the manner of older masters treats the past as a conversation, an explicit form of ancestor-weight. The later ranking of scholar-painters above court artists came from theorists such as Dong Qichang, so the hierarchy is itself a later judgement, and the anecdotes of his generosity are the kind that gather around admired figures.
--- question 1
How many surviving works attributed to Shen Zhou are authentic, given that forgeries circulated in his lifetime?

--- fact 1

--- observer
The Observer notes that a movement that rejected the idea of a permanent self attracted millions of followers over centuries. The tradition treats craving as the root of suffering, an analysis that has parallels in modern psychology.

=== person/sitting-bull
title: Sitting Bull
type: person
date: c. 1831 – 1890
themes: war, religion, kinship
src: f19193b60afc
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sitting Bull (Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake, born between 1831 and 1837, died 1890) was a Hunkpapa Lakota leader and holy man who resisted United States expansion onto Lakota lands and was in the camp at the Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876. Native police killed him in 1890.
--- fact 1
Sitting Bull was born sometime between 1831 and 1837, traditionally on the Grand River in present-day South Dakota (a 2007 family oral tradition places his birth on the Yellowstone River instead), and became a leading Hunkpapa Lakota war leader and holy man.
--- fact 2
The Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868) guaranteed the Black Hills to the Lakota. After gold was found there in 1874 the United States sought to take the land, and war followed in 1876.
--- fact 3
On 25 and 26 June 1876 Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho warriors destroyed the force of Lt. Col. George Custer at the Little Bighorn. Sitting Bull was in the large camp; accounts describe his role as spiritual leader and organizer rather than field commander.
--- fact 4
After the battle he led his followers to Canada in 1877, and he surrendered at Fort Buford in July 1881.
--- fact 5
On 15 December 1890, at Standing Rock, Native police sent to arrest him amid tension over the Ghost Dance killed him in a struggle. The Wounded Knee massacre followed on 29 December.
--- context
Sitting Bull's Lakota faced the same expansion of the United States that drove earlier removals of eastern nations; see [[event/indian-removal-and-the-trail-of-tears|Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]]. He lived in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Sitting Bull is remembered as a war leader though the accounts place him as a spiritual leader at the Little Bighorn, a case of the record following the battle's fame rather than the man's role. His killing by Native police employed by the agency he opposed shows how a state can turn a community's own members into instruments of its claim-line. The reading would weaken if fuller Lakota accounts gave him a more central military role.
--- question 1
What do Lakota oral accounts say about his role at the Little Bighorn?

=== person/sofonisba-anguissola
title: Sofonisba Anguissola
type: person
date: c. 1532 – 1625
themes: art, kinship
src: a0881edb0304
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sofonisba Anguissola (about 1532 to 1625) was an Italian portrait painter from Cremona, one of the first women in Europe to build a professional career as a painter. She spent about 14 years at the Spanish court and won international fame.
--- fact 1
Sofonisba was born about 1532 in Cremona, the eldest of seven children of Amilcare Anguissola, a minor nobleman. He gave all his daughters a humanist education, and several became painters. She studied with Bernardino Campi and Bernardino Gatti.
--- fact 2
As a noblewoman she could not study anatomy from the nude or take large public commissions, so she made portraiture her field.
--- fact 3
Her father sent her drawings to Michelangelo, who reportedly challenged her to draw a crying boy. She answered with Boy Bitten by a Crayfish, a drawing that circulated widely.
--- fact 4
In 1559 she was invited to Madrid to serve Queen Elisabeth of Valois, wife of Philip II, as lady-in-waiting and painting teacher. She painted the royal court for about 14 years, and the king arranged her marriage to a Sicilian nobleman, Fabrizio de Moncada.
--- fact 5
After his death she married a Genoese sea captain and lived in Genoa and Palermo. In 1624 Anthony van Dyck visited her in Palermo and recorded her advice. She died there in 1625, in her early nineties.
--- fact 6
Key works include The Chess Game (1555), Self-Portrait at the Easel (about 1556) and Portrait of Philip II of Spain (1565).
--- fact 7
Many of her court works were later credited to male painters such as Sánchez Coello, and scholars have restored many to her name. The Prado's 2019 exhibition paired her with Lavinia Fontana.
--- context
Sofonisba worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]] and the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. [[person/michelangelo|Michelangelo]] encouraged her, and her success opened the way for later women painters such as [[person/artemisia-gentileschi|Artemisia Gentileschi]]. Some scholars link her Boy Bitten by a Crayfish to [[person/caravaggio|Caravaggio]]'s Boy Bitten by a Lizard. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the limits placed on her, no study from the nude and no large public commissions, shaped the genre she made her own, so that a social rule produced a new kind of picture. Her career also depended on a father who promoted her and a court that hired her, a case of the kin-lattice acting as an agency. That many court works were credited to male painters shows how attribution follows expectation. Restoring them is a correction that is still under way.
--- question 1
Which court portraits now credited to other painters are by Anguissola?

=== person/sor-juana-ines-de-la-cruz
title: Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz
type: person
date: c. 1648 – 1695
themes: art, language, religion
src: 8c846cfeaf2c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz (born 1648 or 1651, died 1695) was a nun, poet and playwright in colonial Mexico City. She is regarded as one of the leading Spanish-language writers of the seventeenth century and an early defender of women's right to study.
--- fact 1
Juana Ramírez de Asbaje was born at San Miguel Nepantla, near Mexico City. Baptismal records point to 1648 or 1651, and the year is debated. She died on 17 April 1695.
--- fact 2
Largely self-taught, she served at the viceroy's court in her teens and in 1669 entered the Hieronymite convent of San Jerónimo.
--- fact 3
She wrote lyric poems, plays such as Los empeños de una casa (1683) and the long poem Primero sueño. Her first collection, Inundación castálida, was printed in Madrid in 1689.
--- fact 4
In 1690 the Bishop of Puebla published her critique of a sermon by António Vieira as the Carta atenagórica and, writing as "Sor Filotea," urged her toward religious study. She answered in 1691 with the Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz, defending women's right to learn.
--- fact 5
In 1693 and 1694 she stopped writing and signed penitential documents. Whether she sold her library or had it taken, and how far the choice was hers, is debated.
--- fact 6
She died in the plague of 1695 while nursing other sisters.
--- context
Sor Juana lived in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], in a Mexico City built over [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] and ruled by Spain after the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the convent gave her a room, books and time at the price of vows. The meaning-engine that sheltered her writing later restrained it. A specialist could argue that the silence of 1693 was her own choice, or that the surviving documents are formulaic, which would weaken a reading of her as simply silenced.
--- question 1
Was her final silence chosen, forced, or both?
--- question 2
Which accounts of her library's fate rest on documents?

=== person/sukarno
title: Sukarno
type: person
date: 1901 – 1970
themes: war, religion
src: 678f835e905a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sukarno (1901 to 1970) was the leader of Indonesia's independence movement and its first president. He proclaimed independence in 1945, ruled until 1967, and was removed from power after the killings of 1965 and 1966.
--- fact 1
Sukarno was born on 6 June 1901 in Surabaya, in the Dutch East Indies, and died on 21 June 1970 in Jakarta, under house arrest.
--- fact 2
He founded the Indonesian National Party in 1927. The Dutch arrested him in 1929 and released him in 1931, then exiled him to Flores in 1934 and to Sumatra in 1938.
--- fact 3
He cooperated with the Japanese occupation from 1942 to 1945, which forced millions into labor and whose food requisitions contributed to a famine that killed over a million people in Java in 1944 and 1945.
--- fact 4
On 1 June 1945 he presented Pancasila, five founding principles for the state, and on 17 August 1945 he proclaimed independence, becoming president the next day.
--- fact 5
He hosted the Bandung Conference of Asian and African states in 1955 and introduced "Guided Democracy" in 1959, ending parliamentary rule.
--- fact 6
After the 30 September Movement of 1965, an army-led campaign killed between 500,000 and over a million people, mostly communists and alleged sympathizers. General Suharto took power in 1966 and 1967.
--- context
Sukarno worked in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], in the years of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] and the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a nation spread over thousands of islands was named and given five founding principles before its state existed, story-glue in its plainest form. He tried to hold nationalist, religious and communist camps under one roof, and the arrangement broke in 1965 in mass killing. A specialist could argue that army and foreign pressures made the collapse of Guided Democracy likely whatever he chose, which would weaken a reading that places the outcome in his leadership.
--- question 1
What role did he play, if any, in the events of 30 September 1965?
--- question 2
How much did his wartime cooperation with Japan cost, and how much did it gain?

His armies captured Belgrade in 1521 and Rhodes in 1522, then advanced deep into Central Europe, defeating and killing the King of Hungary at the Battle of Mohacs in 1526, though a first siege of Vienna in 1529 failed to take the city.
--- fact 2
Known within the empire as "Kanuni" (the Lawgiver), he issued a major legal code harmonizing Ottoman civil law with Islamic religious law across the empire's diverse territories.
--- fact 3
His navies under admirals such as Barbarossa Hayreddin Pasha contested Mediterranean control with Spain and its allies, and Ottoman forces also pushed into Persia and Mesopotamia, capturing Baghdad in 1534.

His reign saw a major flourishing of Ottoman architecture under the chief architect [[person/mimar-sinan|Mimar Sinan]], and of court poetry, painting, and decorative arts, centered on Istanbul.
--- context
Suleiman led the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] from [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], extending its reach across three continents. --- observer
The Observer notes that Suleiman's failed 1529 siege of Vienna, rather than his many successes, marked the practical limit of Ottoman expansion into Central Europe for the rest of his reign; the empire's reputation for irresistible advance rested on decades of victories that stopped, in the end, at a single unsuccessful siege.

=== person/sun-tzu
title: Sun Tzu
type: person
date: c. 544 – 496 BCE
themes: war, language
src: 71d51a082186
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sun Tzu (traditionally about 544 to 496 BCE) is the name attached to The Art of War, the most influential text on military strategy from ancient China, said to have served the state of Wu. Whether he was one historical person is disputed.
--- fact 1
Tradition places Sun Tzu, personal name Sun Wu, as a general who advised King Helü of Wu in the late 6th century BCE. The Art of War is organized in about 13 short chapters.
--- fact 2
The historian Sima Qian, writing centuries later, told a story of Sun Tzu proving his discipline by drilling the king's concubines, executing two who disobeyed. Scholars treat this as legend.
--- fact 3
His name appears in no text earlier than Sima Qian's history, and one theory holds "Sun Wu" was a made-up title, with the Art of War actually the work of his supposed descendant Sun Bin.
--- fact 4
In 1972 bamboo strips from a Han tomb at Yinqueshan included versions of both the Art of War and a separate text by Sun Bin, confirming the Art of War circulated early and that Sun Bin was a distinct author.
--- fact 5
The text has shaped military and business thought for over two thousand years, and is translated worldwide.
--- context
Sun Tzu's traditional dates place him in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] near the end of the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]], roughly contemporary with [[person/confucius|Confucius]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a text on organized, calculated sanctioned harm has outlived any certainty about who wrote it, carried forward in mark-code through copies made centuries after the events it claims. The 1972 tomb find settled that the text existed early, without settling who composed it. A specialist could argue "Sun Tzu" was a legendary attribution rather than a single author, which would weaken any biographical reading of the text's origin.
--- question 1
Was Sun Tzu a real individual, a legendary figure, or a name attached to a composite text?
--- question 2
How much of the Art of War as read today reflects its earliest version found at Yinqueshan?

=== person/sun-yat-sen
title: Sun Yat-sen
type: person
date: 1866 – 1925
themes: war, kinship
src: d2ba4101a58b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sun Yat-sen (1866 to 1925) was a Chinese revolutionary and physician who worked for decades to overthrow the Qing dynasty and became the first provisional president of the Republic of China in 1912. Both mainland China and Taiwan honor him as a founder.
--- fact 1
Sun Yat-sen was born on 12 November 1866 in Cuiheng, Guangdong, and died on 12 March 1925 in Beijing. He was educated in Hawaii and trained as a doctor in Hong Kong, graduating in 1892.
--- fact 2
He founded the Revive China Society in 1894, after an earlier reform petition was rejected, and organized several failed uprisings against the Qing from 1895 onward.
--- fact 3
In Tokyo in 1905 he founded the Tongmenghui, which grew to about a thousand members, and set out his Three Principles of the People: nationalism, democracy and people's livelihood.
--- fact 4
The Wuchang uprising of 10 October 1911, which he did not lead in person, began the revolution that ended the Qing dynasty. He returned from abroad and served as provisional president from 1 January 1912, resigning that March to Yuan Shikai.
--- fact 5
He rebuilt his Kuomintang party in 1919, and in 1923 allied it with the Soviet Union and the small Chinese Communist Party.
--- fact 6
In 1904 he obtained a certificate claiming birth in Hawaii in 1870, later renounced; his standard birth year and place are not in doubt.
--- context
Sun Yat-sen worked in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and the [[event/xinhai-revolution|Xinhai Revolution]] of 1911 that he had long prepared for ended the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that decades of failed uprisings built a network and an ideology, story-glue in the Three Principles, that outlasted any single attempt at sanctioned harm. He was abroad when the revolution he had long prepared for finally succeeded, and held power only briefly before yielding it. A specialist could argue his greatest effect was organizational and rhetorical rather than military, which would weaken a reading that credits him with the 1911 victory itself.
--- question 1
How much did his organizing directly cause the 1911 uprising, given he was not present for it?
--- question 2
What did his 1923 alliance with the Soviet Union and the Communists commit his party to?

=== person/sunni-ali
title: Sunni Ali
type: person
date: c. 1464 – 1492
themes: war, religion
src: ee39c99daa82
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sunni Ali (ruled about 1464 to 1492) was the ruler who turned Songhai from a small state on the Niger River into a West African empire, taking Timbuktu in 1468 as the Mali Empire declined. His birth year is unknown.
--- fact 1
Sunni Ali became ruler of Songhai about 1464, the fifteenth in the Sunni dynasty.
--- fact 2
He conquered Timbuktu in 1468 and, after a long siege, the trading city of Djenné, taking advantage of the weakening Mali Empire.
--- fact 3
He was nominally Muslim but tolerated older local religious practice, and this drew tension with Muslim scholars in Timbuktu, whose chronicles remember him harshly.
--- fact 4
He died in 1492; accounts differ on whether he drowned crossing a river or died another way.
--- fact 5
His son Sunni Baru succeeded him but was overthrown in 1493 by Askia Muhammad, a general who accused Sunni Baru of insufficient devotion to Islam and founded a new dynasty.
--- context
Sunni Ali built the [[culture/songhai-empire|Songhai Empire]] in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], seizing [[place/timbuktu|Timbuktu]] as the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]] weakened. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the harshest account of Sunni Ali comes from the Muslim scholars of the city he conquered, whose meaning-engine he did not fully adopt, so the record of his reign carries their grievance along with the facts. His death drew two different stories within a generation, a sign of how quickly detail slips even for a ruling conqueror. A specialist could argue that the Timbuktu chronicles' harsh portrait reflects the scholars' own resentment more than his actual governance, which would weaken a reading of him as simply irreligious.
--- question 1
How did Sunni Ali die, and why do the accounts differ?
--- question 2
How much of his reputation for irreligion reflects the political grievance of Timbuktu's scholars rather than his actual practice?

=== person/tamar-of-georgia
title: Tamar of Georgia
type: person
date: c. 1160 – 1213
themes: religion, war, kinship
src: b81af5f28dc8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tamar (about 1160 to 1213) ruled the Kingdom of Georgia from 1184 to 1213, presiding over its political and cultural high point, later called the Georgian Golden Age.
--- fact 1
She was crowned co-ruler with her father, George III, in 1178 to secure her succession, then became sole monarch on his death in 1184, ruling with the formal title "king of kings" rather than a feminine equivalent.
--- fact 2
Her armies won major victories against regional Muslim powers, including the Battle of Shamkor in 1195 and the Battle of Basian in 1202, expanding Georgian influence across much of the South Caucasus and into parts of Armenia and Anatolia.
--- fact 3
Her reign saw significant patronage of Georgian literature and architecture, including the era's most celebrated work, Shota Rustaveli's epic poem "The Knight in the Panther's Skin," traditionally dedicated to her.
--- fact 4
She was canonized as a saint by the Georgian Orthodox Church, and centuries of later Georgian tradition remember her reign as the kingdom's greatest period of security and cultural achievement.
--- context
Tamar ruled Georgia in the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] region during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], expanding Georgian power at the expense of neighboring Muslim states. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Tamar's title, "king" rather than "queen," suggests Georgian tradition saw sovereignty itself as inherently masculine even while accepting a woman as its holder, a distinction that separates the office from the person occupying it more sharply than modern titles usually do.
--- question 1
How much of Georgia's "Golden Age" reputation reflects Tamar's own governance versus favorable regional conditions, including the temporary weakness of neighboring powers?

=== person/teddy-roosevelt
title: Teddy Roosevelt
type: person
date: 1858 – 1919
themes: earth-systems, war, kinship
src: ae7b9d362ca2
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Theodore "Teddy" Roosevelt (1858 to 1919) was the 26th President of the United States, serving from 1901 to 1909, known for trust-busting antitrust policy, an assertive foreign policy, and a major expansion of federal land conservation.
--- fact 1
He became president in 1901 after the assassination of William McKinley, at 42 the youngest person to hold the office, and was elected in his own right in 1904.
--- fact 2
He pursued antitrust action against major corporate combinations, including a successful suit against the Northern Securities Company railroad trust in 1904, and mediated the 1902 coal strike partly by threatening to send in federal troops to run the mines.
--- fact 3
He established the United States Forest Service and used his executive authority to create national forests, wildlife refuges, and national monuments, protecting roughly 230 million acres of land during his presidency.
--- fact 4
After leaving office he attempted a comeback in the 1912 election on a progressive "Bull Moose" third-party ticket, splitting the Republican vote and helping elect Woodrow Wilson instead.
--- context
Theodore Roosevelt led the United States, in [[region/north-america|North America]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Roosevelt's conservation legacy, protecting land at a scale no predecessor had attempted, came from the same expansive view of executive power that also let him intervene directly in labor disputes and corporate affairs; the same instinct toward vigorous federal action that alarmed his political opponents also produced the national park and forest system many now take for granted.
--- question 1
How much of Roosevelt's conservation program would have been achievable through Congress alone, without his willingness to act through executive authority?

=== person/timur
title: Timur
type: person
date: 1336 – 1405
themes: war, art
src: 1e13ef26d898
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Timur (Tamerlane, traditionally dated 1336 to 1405) was a Central Asian conqueror who built the Timurid Empire and made Samarkand its centre.
--- fact 1
Timur's birth year is contested: 1336 is the traditional date, but many contemporary accounts imply the late 1320s. He was born near Kesh (now Shahrisabz, Uzbekistan).
--- fact 2
By about 1370 he had besieged his rival Amir Husayn at Balkh and, after Husayn's assassination, proclaimed himself sovereign of the Chagatai line of khans, with dominion over Transoxiana.
--- fact 3
He was not a descendant of Genghis Khan and did not take the title khan. He ruled as amir through a puppet Chaghatayid khan, Suyurghatmish, and took the title güregen (royal son-in-law) through his marriage to Saray Mulk Khanum, of the Chinggisid line.
--- fact 4
His armies campaigned in Persia, Iraq, the lands of the Golden Horde, India (Delhi was sacked in 1398), and Anatolia (he defeated the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402).
--- fact 5
Later chronicles describe massacres in cities he conquered. Their casualty figures are contested (see Open Questions).
--- fact 6
He died in February 1405 at Otrar (also called Farab), in present-day Kazakhstan, while preparing an invasion of Ming China. Sources differ on the day (17 to 19 February). He was buried in the Gur-e-Amir in Samarkand.
--- context
Timur founded the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]] and presented himself as a restorer of the Mongol empire of [[person/genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]]. He rebuilt [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]], where he deported artisans from conquered cities.
--- observer
The Observer notes a conqueror who could not claim descent from the earlier conqueror he admired, and so bought the link instead: a Genghisid wife, a puppet khan, and the title of son-in-law. Legitimacy borrowed through marriage rather than blood is still a form of ancestor-weight, and it suggests the pull of the earlier line was strong enough to be worth acquiring.
--- question 1
How reliable are the casualty figures in accounts of his campaigns? Chronicle figures for single cities run from tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand, and modern scholars estimate millions of deaths overall, but none of these numbers is well established.
--- question 2
When was he actually born: the late 1320s or 1336?

=== person/tokugawa-ieyasu
title: Tokugawa Ieyasu
type: person
date: 1543 – 1616
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: a52809f13ea0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543 to 1616) unified Japan after decades of civil war and founded the Tokugawa shogunate, a military government that ruled Japan for over two and a half centuries.
--- fact 1
He rose through alliance and conflict with the era's other major warlords, Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, before defeating a rival coalition at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600.
--- fact 2
He was appointed shogun by the emperor in 1603, then formally passed the title to his son Hidetada in 1605 while continuing to hold real power as retired shogun until his death.
--- fact 3
He destroyed the remaining Toyotomi family stronghold at the siege of Osaka Castle in 1615, removing the last major rival claim to national authority.
--- fact 4
His government issued regulations restricting Christianity and foreign trade that his successors expanded into the sakoku ("closed country") policy, limiting most foreign contact for over two centuries.
--- context
Tokugawa Ieyasu founded the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], ending the Sengoku period of civil war. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ieyasu's caution and willingness to wait out rivals rather than defeat them quickly are often credited for his success where more aggressive contemporaries failed; a well-known adage from the era compares Nobunaga, Hideyoshi and Ieyasu by how each supposedly answered a caged bird that would not sing, with Ieyasu simply waiting for it to sing on its own.
--- question 1
How deliberate was the shift toward isolationism under his immediate successors, versus a gradual response to specific incidents involving European traders and missionaries?

=== person/tomyris
title: Tomyris
type: person
date: c. 530 BCE
themes: war, kinship
src: 2bc9aeaf32a3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tomyris was a queen of the Massagetae, a nomadic people east of the Caspian Sea, who according to the Greek historian Herodotus defeated and killed Cyrus the Great of Persia in 530 BCE. She is known only from Herodotus, and other ancient accounts of Cyrus's death differ.
--- fact 1
Tomyris appears only in Book 1 of Herodotus's Histories, written about a century after the events. Her dates are unknown; the date given here is that of the battle in his account.
--- fact 2
Herodotus says she ruled the Massagetae, who lived east of the Caspian Sea near the Araxes River, and rejected Cyrus's offer of marriage, which he saw as a way to gain her kingdom.
--- fact 3
In his account the Persians captured her son Spargapises by trickery, and he killed himself in captivity. Tomyris then defeated the Persian army in battle.
--- fact 4
Herodotus says she had Cyrus's body found and his head put in a wineskin filled with blood.
--- fact 5
Other ancient accounts differ: Xenophon has Cyrus die peacefully in his palace, and Ctesias has him die of a wound in a war on the eastern frontier that the Persians won.
--- fact 6
Herodotus compares the Massagetae's customs with those of the Scythians.
--- context
Tomyris's story concerns the death of [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], founder of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], on the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]] frontier during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Tomyris survives only through an outsider's account that gives a steppe queen a speech and a grim revenge, a story-glue for Greek readers about the limits of a great king. That the tale turns on a mother's lost son shows how the kin-lattice gave it meaning. The reading would weaken if steppe or archaeological evidence showed a different ruler, or if the Persian defeat proved to be invented.
--- question 1
What do archaeology and steppe sources add about the Massagetae?

=== person/toussaint-louverture
title: Toussaint Louverture
type: person
date: c. 1743 – 1803
themes: war, kinship
src: aa3e7c810dfb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Toussaint Louverture was a formerly enslaved man who became the principal military and political leader of the Haitian Revolution, and died in a French prison in 1803.
--- fact 1
He was born into slavery on Saint-Domingue around 1743 (20 May 1743 in one account) and was freed in 1776 or 1777, depending on the source, before the revolution began in 1791.
--- fact 2
He began as a lieutenant in the 1791 uprising and at first allied with the Spanish of neighboring Santo Domingo. In 1794 he switched to the French Republic, which had abolished slavery, and in 1798 he negotiated the withdrawal of British forces under a treaty with General Thomas Maitland.
--- fact 3
On 7 July 1801 he promulgated a constitution asserting his authority over the whole island of Hispaniola, which made him governor-general for life.
--- fact 4
Napoleon sent an expedition in 1802; Toussaint was arrested by deception and deported to France, where he died at Fort de Joux in April 1803.
--- fact 5
Jean-Jacques Dessalines proclaimed Haiti's independence on 1 January 1804.
--- context
Toussaint was the central figure of the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]], an event within the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]] that led to [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]] in Saint-Domingue. Toussaint Louverture sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a man born into slavery wrote a constitution and commanded armies, and that the empire he fought treated his rights as negotiable by treachery. The universal principles proclaimed in that era were tested most severely by people who were excluded from them.
--- question 1
How far did his constitution intend independence?

=== person/trajan
title: Trajan
type: person
date: 53 – 117 CE
themes: war, technology, trade
src: 82d31911002c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Trajan (53 to 117 CE) was Roman emperor from 98 to 117, presiding over the empire's greatest territorial extent and a major program of public building and infrastructure across the Roman world.
--- fact 1
Born in Hispania, in present-day Spain, he was the first Roman emperor born outside Italy, adopted as heir by the emperor Nerva in 97 after a career as a successful general.
--- fact 2
His conquest of Dacia, commemorated in the reliefs of Trajan's Column in Rome, added a wealthy new province rich in gold mines north of the Danube.
--- fact 3
He extended Roman territory to its largest extent by annexing Armenia and parts of Mesopotamia after a campaign against the Parthian Empire late in his reign, gains his successor Hadrian largely abandoned as unsustainable.
--- fact 4
His building programs included Trajan's Forum and Market in Rome, a new harbor at Ostia, and an extensive network of roads and bridges across the provinces, funded substantially by Dacian gold.
--- context
Trajan led the [[culture/romans|Romans]] from the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], expanding the empire to its widest extent. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Trajan's eastern conquests, which later Roman writers celebrated as the empire's high-water mark, were abandoned within a year of his death by his own successor as indefensible; the empire's territorial peak and its practical limits were reached almost simultaneously.
--- question 1
Would Trajan's eastern conquests have proven sustainable had he lived longer to consolidate them, or was Hadrian's withdrawal the more realistic assessment?
