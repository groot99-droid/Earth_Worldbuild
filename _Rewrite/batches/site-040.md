=== event/austronesian-expansion
title: Austronesian Expansion
type: event
date: c. 3000 BCE
themes: language, kinship
src: c5580a1eb58e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Speakers of Austronesian languages spread outward from Taiwan starting roughly 5,000 to 5,500 years ago, eventually reaching Madagascar in the west and Rapa Nui in the east.
--- fact 1
The most widely accepted model, called Out of Taiwan, places the origin of the language family on Taiwan.
--- fact 2
Migration proceeded through the Philippines and Indonesia, then into Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia.
--- fact 3
It reached Madagascar in the first millennium CE.
--- fact 4
The Austronesian language family has well over a thousand languages.
--- fact 5
The expansion relied on sailing canoes with outriggers and on a crop package that included rice, pigs, dogs, and chickens.
--- context
The expansion connects [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] to the far Pacific and to Africa, and produced the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]] via the [[culture/lapita-culture|Lapita Culture]]. It later joined the wider [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. Austronesian Expansion sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a single language family covers nearly half the circumference of the planet, spread by people in boats rather than by armies. Its span is a reminder that the sea can be a road as easily as a wall.
--- question 1
How closely does the language tree track the genetic and archaeological record?

--- summary
The 'Axial Age' is a term for the period of roughly 800 to 200 BCE in which major philosophical and religious traditions arose across Eurasia in China, India, Persia, Israel, and Greece.
--- fact 1
The term was coined by the philosopher Karl Jaspers in 1949.
--- fact 2
Figures often associated with the period include [[person/confucius|Confucius]], [[person/siddhartha-gautama|Siddhartha Gautama]], Zoroaster, Hebrew prophets, and Greek philosophers such as [[person/aristotle|Aristotle]]'s predecessors.
--- fact 3
Common themes include ethics, transcendence, and individual responsibility.
--- fact 4
Many historians criticize the concept as too neat and as underrating other periods and regions.
--- context
The Axial Age spans the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and early [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. It connects to [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and to the essay [[observer-note/story-glue-how-strangers-cooperate-at-scale|Story-Glue - How Strangers Cooperate at Scale]].
--- observer
The Observer notes the puzzle of near-simultaneous questions about how to live, arising in states that had grown large enough that old tribal rules no longer sufficed.

=== event/banded-iron-formations
title: Banded Iron Formations
type: event
date: 3.8 – 1.8 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: 34c9f7666890
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Banded iron formations are ancient sedimentary rocks of alternating iron-rich and silica-rich layers, deposited mostly between about 3.8 and 1.8 billion years ago. They are a major source of the world's iron ore.
--- fact 1
The layers are usually millimetres to centimetres thick. The oldest are about 3.8 billion years old, in Greenland, and deposition peaked about 2.7 to 2.4 billion years ago, with great deposits in Western Australia, South Africa and the Lake Superior region of North America.
--- fact 2
The leading explanation is that oxygen-poor seawater held dissolved iron, which was oxidized and settled out when oxygen or other oxidants appeared. Whether oxygen-producing bacteria or iron-using photosynthetic bacteria did most of the work is debated.
--- fact 3
Deposition largely ended about 1.8 billion years ago, probably as the deeper ocean lost its dissolved iron, and a few late formations appear during the Cryogenian ice ages; see [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]].
--- fact 4
Ore derived from these rocks is a major source of the iron used in steelmaking today.
--- context
Banded iron formations record the slow oxidation of the oceans before and during the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]], and they tie the [[event/origin-of-photosynthesis|Origin of Photosynthesis]] and the work of [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]] to changes in ocean chemistry. They belong to the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and continue into the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]], and to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the raw material of the species' steel is the residue of an ocean that rusted over about two billion years, a process driven by microbes with no aim beyond their own metabolism. The reading concerns only the link across time, and the debate over which microbes did the work does not affect it.
--- question 1
Which organisms or processes oxidized the dissolved iron?
--- question 2
Why did banded iron formations largely stop forming about 1.8 billion years ago?

=== event/battle-of-adwa
title: Battle of Adwa
type: event
date: 1896
themes: war
src: 0294a92c3519
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Battle of Adwa, fought on 1 March 1896, saw the Ethiopian army under Emperor Menelik II decisively defeat an invading Italian force, securing Ethiopia's independence.
--- fact 1
Ethiopian forces numbered roughly 73,000 to over 100,000 against about 14,500 to 18,000 Italian and colonial troops.
--- fact 2
More than 6,000 of the Italian army were killed, slightly more than half of them Italians and the rest African askari, and between 3,000 and 4,000 of those fighting under Italian command were taken prisoner.
--- fact 3
The war arose from a dispute over Article 17 of the Treaty of Wuchale, which read differently in Amharic and Italian.
--- fact 4
In October 1896 Italy signed the Treaty of Addis Ababa, abrogating Wuchale.
--- context
The victory made Ethiopia one of two African states to stay independent through the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]]. See [[person/menelik-ii|Menelik II]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a single word in a treaty translation set two states on the road to war. Adwa also became a symbol across the African diaspora of what resistance could achieve.

About fifteen years later the two powers concluded a peace treaty, around 1259 BCE, of which versions survive in both Egyptian and Hittite archives.

Kadesh shows the balance between the [[culture/hittites|Hittites]] and the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] just before the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]]. --- observer
The Observer notes that the same event is remembered as a triumph on both sides, and that the peace treaty that followed is preserved in two languages. Declaring victory and formalizing peace appear to be the same social technology working in different directions.

The battle fixed the frontier between the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] and the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]] in the oases of [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]], and is linked to the diffusion of [[technology/paper|Paper]] westward. === event/bengal-famine-of-1943
title: Bengal Famine of 1943
type: event
date: 1943 – 1944
themes: war, trade
src: 05f33ecbe987
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Bengal famine of 1943 killed an estimated 2 to 3 million people in British-ruled Bengal during World War II. Historians debate its causes, but agree that it was not simply a shortage of food and that wartime policy played a large part.
--- fact 1
Estimates of deaths from starvation, malaria, and other disease range widely, from about 800,000 to 3.8 million. A scholarly consensus is often given as about 2.1 million, with some estimates of 2.7 to 3 million.
--- fact 2
The economist Amartya Sen argued that the famine was an entitlement failure: food supply in Bengal did not fall sharply, but economic pressures prevented certain groups from buying it.
--- fact 3
After Burma and Singapore fell to Japan in 1942, rice imports from those countries stopped, which worsened Bengal's food crisis.
--- fact 4
British Prime Minister Winston Churchill has been criticized for war priorities and refusal to divert food to Bengal. The War Cabinet discussed requests for more grain shipments to India several times in late 1943 and early 1944, citing limited shipping and the need to keep reserves for military operations.
--- context
The famine took place under the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]] during [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]] and is part of the background to the end of British rule; see [[event/partition-of-india|Partition of India]]. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]] for how the vault treats deaths caused by policy.
--- observer
The Observer records this as a catastrophe in which millions of people died while food existed, and notes that a famine can be produced by who is entitled to buy rather than by how much grows. The argument over blame is itself a claim about who owed whom protection.
--- question 1
How much of the death toll was due to starvation and how much to disease such as malaria?
--- question 2
How much responsibility lies with wartime priorities in London and how much with authorities in India?

=== event/black-death

type: event
date: 1346 – 1353
themes: earth-systems, trade, religion
src: eaf4f1cdb9ca
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Black Death was a plague pandemic caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis that swept through Eurasia and North Africa from 1346 to 1353.
--- fact 1
It likely killed 30 to 60 percent of Europe's population, and estimates for Eurasia as a whole run to tens of millions of deaths, with some as high as 75 to 200 million.
--- fact 2
It spread along trade routes, via fleas on rodents and on people.
--- fact 3
Labor shortages after the pandemic raised wages in parts of Europe.
--- fact 4
Plague returned in waves for centuries.
--- context
The pandemic is a landmark of the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and shows how the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]] linked disease environments.
--- observer
The Observer notes how connection has a cost: the same exchange-web that carried silk and ideas also carried a pathogen. Every increase in connectivity in the record has disease consequences.
--- question 1
What were the death tolls in Asia and Africa?

The breakup of Pangaea isolated continental faunas and set the stage for the later biogeography of the [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]] and mammals. === event/british-raj
title: British Raj
type: event
date: 1858 – 1947
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 9b542dcbf7a9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The British Raj was the period of direct British rule over the Indian subcontinent, from 1858 to 1947. It began when the Crown took over from the East India Company after the Rebellion of 1857 and ended with the independence and partition of India and Pakistan.
--- fact 1
The Government of India Act, passed on 2 August 1858, transferred authority over India from the East India Company to the British Crown, as a response to the Indian Rebellion of 1857.
--- fact 2
The Company lost all its administrative powers, and its territories and armed forces passed to the Crown. The Company itself was formally dissolved by an Act of Parliament in 1874.
--- fact 3
The Act created a cabinet-level Secretary of State for India and a fifteen-member Council of India in London. In Calcutta the Governor-General became head of the Government of India and was called the Viceroy.
--- fact 4
The Raj lasted until the independence of India and Pakistan in 1947.
--- context
The Raj followed the [[event/indian-rebellion-of-1857|Indian Rebellion of 1857]] and ended with the [[event/partition-of-india|Partition of India]] in 1947. It included the [[event/bengal-famine-of-1943|Bengal Famine of 1943]] and a long nationalist movement associated with [[person/mahatma-gandhi|Mahatma Gandhi]]. It succeeded the earlier rule of the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] over much of the subcontinent.
--- observer
The Observer notes a change of ownership: an enterprise that had governed as a company was replaced by a state that governed through a ministry, and the change followed a rebellion that both sides remember as an atrocity committed by the other. A rule of few over many depended on shared administration, and ended when that administration was no longer accepted.
--- question 1
How should the Raj's effects on the economy, famine, and infrastructure be weighed against each other, and by whose standards?
--- question 2
How did Indian institutions and elites shape the Raj rather than only being shaped by it?

=== event/blling-allerd-warming
title: Bølling-Allerød Warming
type: event
date: c. 12.75 – 10.95 ka
themes: earth-systems
src: 41c31808cb2d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Bølling-Allerød was an abrupt warm interval from about 14,700 to 12,900 years ago, the first major warming after the Last Glacial Maximum. It ended just as abruptly with the onset of the Younger Dryas.
--- fact 1
The interval ran from about 14,700 to 12,900 years ago, following the Oldest Dryas, the final cold stage of the retreat from the Last Glacial Maximum.
--- fact 2
Warming was abrupt and large in the Northern Hemisphere, while the Southern Hemisphere cooled slightly at the same time, a pattern seen in other rapid climate shifts.
--- fact 3
Boreal forest and birch woodland spread across Western Europe and the North European Plain, including Doggerland, replacing tundra vegetation.
--- fact 4
A pulse of rapid sea-level rise, Meltwater Pulse 1A, began within the interval as the Laurentide and Eurasian ice sheets lost mass quickly; estimates of the rise range from roughly 10 to 20 meters over a few centuries.
--- fact 5
The interval was briefly interrupted by the Older Dryas, a cold spell of one to two centuries whose evidence is strongest in northern Europe, leading some to debate whether it was global or regional.
--- fact 6
It ended abruptly with the onset of the [[event/younger-dryas|Younger Dryas]], which itself lasted until about 11,700 years ago, when the Holocene began.
--- context
The Bølling-Allerød sits within the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]], following the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]] and preceding the [[event/younger-dryas|Younger Dryas]] and the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the climate the species inherited did not warm smoothly: a sharp rise, a cold century-long interruption, then a second abrupt swing back to near-glacial cold, all within about three thousand years. The stability that made later farming and cities possible had to wait for the swings to stop. The reading would weaken if closer dating showed the Older Dryas was a purely local signal with no wider significance.
--- question 1
Was the Older Dryas a global cooling event or a regional one?
--- question 2
Which ice sheet contributed most to Meltwater Pulse 1A?

=== event/campanian-ignimbrite-eruption
title: Campanian Ignimbrite Eruption
type: event
date: c. 39.85 ka
themes: earth-systems
src: 26f30ebec050
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Campanian Ignimbrite eruption of the Campi Flegrei caldera near Naples, Italy, about 39,850 years ago, was the largest explosive eruption in Europe in roughly the last 200,000 years. Its ash spread across the Mediterranean and Eastern Europe, and its role in Neanderthal decline around the same time is debated.
--- fact 1
A 2017 study combining radiocarbon and argon dating with a synchronized varve chronology dated the eruption precisely to about 39,850 years ago.
--- fact 2
The eruption released roughly 181 to 265 cubic kilometers of magma, a Volcanic Explosivity Index of 7, the largest in Europe in about 200,000 years.
--- fact 3
Its ash, the Y-5 layer, dispersed over about 3 million square kilometers and is used as a dated marker in sediment cores from the Mediterranean to the Russian Plain.
--- fact 4
The eruption fell close to the onset of Heinrich Event 4, a severe cold phase, but records show the ash postdates that onset by roughly 700 to 800 years, complicating a simple eruption-triggers-cooling account.
--- fact 5
Climate modeling found the cooling and acid deposition lasted only one to two years and that the most intense cooling largely missed the Western European areas where late Neanderthal populations lived.
--- fact 6
At several Italian sites, modern-human-associated stone tools appear stratigraphically before the ash layer, and some recalibrated Neanderthal extinction dates predate the eruption too; some researchers still argue it shaped cultural change further east, where modeled cooling was stronger.
--- context
The eruption falls within the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and is often discussed alongside the [[event/neanderthal-extinction|Neanderthal Extinction]] and the decline of [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]], though a direct link is contested. It occurs near the start of the [[era/upper-paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer treats the pairing of a great eruption with a Neanderthal decline near it in time as an old temptation: a single dramatic cause for a slow disappearance. The dating and modeling done since have mostly weakened that story rather than confirmed it, which the Observer takes as a caution against single-cause explanations for extinctions. The reading would weaken if new modeling showed the strongest cooling did reach the last Neanderthal populations after all.
--- question 1
Did the eruption meaningfully affect Neanderthal populations anywhere, even without its coldest effects in the west?
--- question 2
How exactly does its timing relate to the onset of Heinrich Event 4?

=== event/carboniferous-coal-forests
title: Carboniferous Coal Forests
type: event
date: 323 – 299 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 2d1da0273c7d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Carboniferous coal forests were swamp forests of wet tropical lowlands, mostly in the later Carboniferous, about 323 to 299 million years ago. Their buried plant matter became much of the world's coal.
--- fact 1
The forests grew mainly on the lowlands of the northern continent of Euramerica and in parts of China, and included tree-sized club-mosses such as Lepidodendron, tree ferns, giant horsetails and early conifers.
--- fact 2
Repeated rises and falls of sea level, linked to the Late Paleozoic Ice Age, drowned and re-exposed the swamps and stacked many separate coal seams.
--- fact 3
At Joggins in Nova Scotia, hollow fossil tree stumps contain remains of early amphibians and reptiles.
--- fact 4
Burial of plant carbon lowered atmospheric carbon dioxide and probably raised oxygen. Coal from these rocks fuelled the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] in Britain and other regions.
--- fact 5
About 305 million years ago the wet tropical forests collapsed into isolated patches as the climate became drier and cooler, an event linked to declines among amphibians and the rise of reptiles.
--- context
The Carboniferous coal forests follow the [[event/devonian-forests-and-the-rise-of-trees|Devonian Forests and the Rise of Trees]] and belong to the [[era/carboniferous-period|Carboniferous Period]]. Their end coincides with the [[event/assembly-of-pangaea|Assembly of Pangaea]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer records the difference in rates: the forests stored their carbon over tens of millions of years, and the species has burned much of it in a few centuries. The reading concerns rates only, and it would not be affected by the debate over why so much coal formed in this period.
--- question 1
Why did so much coal accumulate in this period and not in others?
--- question 2
What caused the collapse of the tropical forests about 305 million years ago?

--- summary

Northern glaciation began at about the same time.

--- observer
The Observer notes that a strip of land a few dozen kilometers wide altered the fates of thousands of species on two continents. The same narrow crossing would later matter to humans as a migration route and, much later, as a canal.

When did the land bridge become continuous, and how did that relate to ocean changes?

=== event/deccan-traps-eruptions
title: Deccan Traps Eruptions
type: event
date: 66.3 – 65.5 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 8672a231f276
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Deccan Traps eruptions were a huge outpouring of lava in west-central India, with the main phases at about 66.3 to 65.5 million years ago. They overlap the end-Cretaceous mass extinction, and their role in it is debated.
--- fact 1
The Deccan Traps are among the largest volcanic provinces on Earth; the lava flows pile up to more than two kilometres thick in the Western Ghats and once covered well over a million square kilometres.
--- fact 2
Radiometric dating places most of the volume within about a million years around the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary, with large pulses both before and after the Chicxulub impact. The relative timing is debated.
--- fact 3
The eruptions released carbon dioxide and sulfur gases, which could have caused warming, cooling episodes and acid rain, and stressed ecosystems before the impact.
--- fact 4
Some recent studies conclude that the impact, not the volcanism, was the main driver of the extinction; the question remains open.
--- fact 5
The eruptions are linked to the Réunion hotspot, which India passed over as it drifted north.
--- context
The Deccan Traps Eruptions fall at the end of the [[era/cretaceous-period|Cretaceous Period]] and overlap the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]. They took place before the [[event/collision-of-india-and-asia|Collision of India and Asia]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the end-Cretaceous extinction had at least two candidate causes at nearly the same time, and that separating them depends on dating precision of about a hundred thousand years out of sixty-six million. A single-cause story is easier to tell than to verify. The reading would weaken if dating showed the eruptions ended before the impact.
--- question 1
How much did the eruptions contribute to the extinction relative to the impact?
--- question 2
How did the timing of the biggest lava pulses compare with the impact?
