=== place/machu-picchu

mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Machu Picchu is a stone citadel built about 1450 in the Peruvian Andes, probably as a royal estate for the Inca ruler Pachacuti.
--- fact 1
It sits at about 2,430 meters above sea level.
--- fact 2
It was abandoned around the time of the Spanish conquest.
--- fact 3
Local people knew of it; Hiram Bingham brought it to international attention in 1911.
--- fact 4
It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983.
--- context
The site belongs to the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]]. See [[region/andes|Andes]].
--- observer
The Observer notes the engineering of terraces and drainage, and the way a ruler's retreat was built at the edge of what is buildable.

=== place/marib
title: Marib
type: place
date: c. 800 BCE
themes: trade, technology, religion
src: 2a181001f595
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Marib is a city in Yemen on the edge of the Ramlat al-Sabatayn desert, east of Sanaa, which was the capital of the Kingdom of Saba from about the 8th century BCE and the site of a great dam that irrigated the oasis.
--- fact 1
Marib appears in Sabaean inscriptions from about the 8th century BCE and was the capital of the Kingdom of Saba, whose wealth came from the incense trade.
--- fact 2
The Great Dam of Marib, first built in about the 8th century BCE, diverted seasonal floods from Wadi Adhanah into irrigation canals and was raised and repaired over many centuries; an inscription records a repair in 542 to 543 CE.
--- fact 3
The dam failed for the last time around the 6th century CE; the Quran refers to a flood that ended the prosperity of Saba, which tradition connects with the dam.
--- fact 4
Marib has temples of Almaqah, the chief god of Saba, including the oval Awwam temple, which local tradition calls Mahram Bilqis.
--- fact 5
A modern dam at Marib, built in the 1980s with funding from the United Arab Emirates, replaced the ancient system.
--- fact 6
Since 2015 Marib has been a frontline city in the Yemeni civil war and has taken in large numbers of people displaced by the fighting.
--- context
Marib was the capital of the [[culture/kingdom-of-saba|Kingdom of Saba]] and a node of the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]]; the failure of its dam is remembered in traditions that entered the sources of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. It sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Marib shows a state built on water as much as on incense: a dam that turned flood into farmland needed labor, tribute and coordination, and the inscribed repairs show that upkeep was a recurring political act. When order failed, so did the dam, which suggests that infrastructure and authority rise and fall together. The later story linking the dam's failure to a moral cause is a story-glue about the fall of a prosperous society. Historians debate whether the dam's collapse caused the decline or reflected a decline already under way, and the reading would weaken if the dam's failures are shown to be mainly hydrological.
--- question 1
When was the first dam built, and how large was the irrigated area?
--- question 2
Did the failure of the dam cause the decline of Marib, or follow it?

=== place/mausoleum-of-qin-shi-huang
title: Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang
type: place
date: c. 246 BCE
themes: war, art, religion
src: e0b917530c16
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang is the burial complex of the first emperor of unified China, near Xi'an in Shaanxi, begun in 246 BCE and known for the Terracotta Army of clay soldiers buried in pits east of the tomb mound.
--- fact 1
Construction of the Mausoleum began in 246 BCE, when Ying Zheng became king of Qin; he took the title Qin Shi Huang ("First Emperor") after unifying China in 221 BCE and died in 210 BCE.
--- fact 2
Sima Qian, writing about a century later, reports that 700,000 laborers worked on the tomb and palace, a figure that cannot be verified.
--- fact 3
Farmers digging a well found the Terracotta Army in 1974. The pits hold life-size clay soldiers, horses, chariots and bronze weapons, estimated at about 8,000 figures in total, of which more than 2,000 have been excavated.
--- fact 4
The figures were assembled from molded parts, finished by hand and originally painted in bright colors, which mostly faded after exposure to air.
--- fact 5
Other pits hold figures of officials, acrobats and musicians, and bronze waterbirds.
--- fact 6
The burial mound has not been excavated; soil around it has elevated mercury levels, consistent with Sima Qian's account of mercury in the tomb.
--- fact 7
Sima Qian also reports that childless women of the palace and workers who knew the tomb's secrets were sealed inside after the burial; archaeology has not confirmed this account.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed the Mausoleum in 1987.
--- context
The Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang belongs to the emperor who carried out the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]] and linked walls that became part of the [[place/great-wall-of-china|Great Wall of China]]; the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] followed the Qin. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a state that standardized script, weights and measures also produced its afterlife army from standardized parts, so a grief-rite and a status-signal were built with the same logic as the state's administration. The labor behind it, reported by Sima Qian in the hundreds of thousands, is a cost to record as suffering, though the figure is uncertain. The reading rests on the modular construction that excavators have documented for the warriors; it would weaken if the unopened tomb proves to differ sharply from the pits.
--- question 1
What does the central tomb chamber contain, and can it be opened without damage?
--- question 2
How many people worked on the mausoleum, and under what conditions?

=== place/mecca
title: Mecca
type: place
date: c. 500 CE
themes: religion, trade
src: f399dad79b97
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mecca is a city in the Hejaz region of western Saudi Arabia, about 70 kilometers inland from the Red Sea, the birthplace of Muhammad and the holiest city of Islam, whose sanctuary around the Kaaba is the destination of the annual Hajj pilgrimage.
--- fact 1
The date of Mecca's first settlement is unknown, and the identification of Mecca with the place Macoraba in Ptolemy's 2nd-century geography is disputed. Muslim sources written after Muhammad's time describe a sanctuary town governed by the Quraysh in the century before Islam.
--- fact 2
Islamic tradition places Muhammad's birth in Mecca about 570 CE, his move to Medina in 622, and Mecca's passing under Muslim control in 630 with little fighting.
--- fact 3
The Hajj is one of the Five Pillars of Islam, required once in a lifetime of Muslims able to make the journey; Saudi authorities have reported annual attendance in the low millions in recent decades.
--- fact 4
Historians disagree about Mecca's importance in long-distance trade before Islam; some describe it as a regional sanctuary and market town rather than a hub of the incense and spice trades.
--- fact 5
Mecca was ruled locally by the Sharifs under successive overlords, including the Ottomans from 1517, until the Saudi conquest of 1924 to 1925. Non-Muslims are barred from entering the city.
--- fact 6
In the crush at Mina, near Mecca, during the 2015 Hajj, the Saudi authorities reported 769 deaths, while counts compiled from other governments' figures exceeded 2,000.
--- context
Mecca is the central place of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], the destination of the Hijra from [[place/medina|Medina]], and a stop in the peninsula's wider [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]] setting. It came under the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]] and later the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]], and pilgrims came from far away, including the ruler of the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]], Mansa Musa, who made the Hajj in 1324. Mecca sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Mecca is a place where a meaning-engine draws millions of strangers to one point each year, creating a recurring exchange-web of people, goods and news that reached from West Africa to Southeast Asia. Pilgrims wear plain white garments (for men, two unstitched cloths), which reduces visible differences of rank during the rites. Pilgrims themselves describe the journey as an act of obedience and devotion, and this reading of its social effects is meant to sit beside that account, not replace it. Crowd disasters such as Mina in 2015 are a recurring cost of gathering at this scale and are recorded here as suffering. The reading would weaken if pilgrim networks turn out to have carried little trade or news compared with their devotional purpose.
--- question 1
What does archaeology show about pre-Islamic Mecca, given how little excavation has been done in the sacred precinct?
--- question 2
How large was Mecca's role in long-distance trade before Islam?

=== place/medina
title: Medina
type: place
date: 622 CE
themes: religion, war, kinship
src: 0f5e0d231702
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Medina is a city in the Hejaz region of western Saudi Arabia, known before Islam as Yathrib, to which Muhammad migrated in 622 CE and where he is buried; it is the second holiest city of Islam.
--- fact 1
Medina was an oasis settlement called Yathrib before Islam, inhabited by Arab tribes, chiefly the Aws and Khazraj, and by Jewish tribes; its earlier history is poorly documented.
--- fact 2
Muhammad and his followers migrated there from Mecca in 622 (the Hijra), the year from which the Islamic calendar counts; Muhammad died and was buried there in 632.
--- fact 3
The Constitution of Medina, a document preserved in later sources, sets out obligations among Muslim, Jewish and other groups; its date and authenticity are debated.
--- fact 4
Traditional Islamic sources report that two Jewish tribes, Banu Qaynuqa and Banu Nadir, were expelled from Medina, and that the men of a third, Banu Qurayza, were executed in 627, with figures reported from about 400 to 900; some modern historians question details of these accounts.
--- fact 5
Medina was the capital of the first caliphs until 656, when Ali moved to Kufa; the Umayyad caliphs later ruled from Damascus.
--- fact 6
The Prophet's Mosque in Medina, first built in 622, was enlarged by the Umayyad caliph al-Walid I in the early 8th century and repeatedly since; non-Muslims are barred from the central sanctuary area.
--- context
Medina was the destination of the Hijra from [[place/mecca|Mecca]] and the first capital of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], before rule passed to the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Medina is where a small community became a polity, and that its founding document, if early, tried to bind kin groups into one order with shared rules for settling disputes, a case of story-glue in the form of law. The same tradition records that defining who belonged also defined who did not, with exile and killing recorded as suffering for the tribes that fell outside. The reading depends on the early dating of the Constitution and on the reliability of sources written generations later, both of which specialists debate.
--- question 1
How early is the Constitution of Medina, and how reliable are the later accounts that preserve it?
--- question 2
What does archaeology show about Yathrib before 622?

=== place/mekong-river
title: Mekong River
type: place
date: c. 2000 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade, technology
src: 3a58d8561ac0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mekong River is a river of Asia about 4,350 kilometers long, rising on the Tibetan Plateau and flowing through China, Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia and Vietnam, whose annual flood supports rice farming and one of the world's largest inland fisheries.
--- fact 1
The Mekong rises on the Tibetan Plateau and flows through China, along the borders of Myanmar, Laos and Thailand, and through Cambodia and Vietnam into the South China Sea, where it forms a large delta.
--- fact 2
Rice-farming and fishing communities lived along the middle and lower Mekong from about the 3rd to 2nd millennia BCE, and the early port of Oc Eo in the delta, part of the state called Funan, traded from about the 1st century CE.
--- fact 3
The Tonlé Sap, a lake in Cambodia joined to the Mekong, changes its direction of flow with the season: floodwater flows into it in the wet season and back out in the dry season, supporting a major fishery.
--- fact 4
The lower basin has about 60 million people, and the river is among the world's most productive inland fisheries.
--- fact 5
A French exploration commission in 1866 to 1868 found that rapids made the Mekong unnavigable as a route to China, and the Mekong Delta was a major area of fighting in the Vietnam War.
--- fact 6
China has built a cascade of dams on the upper Mekong since the 1990s, and Laos began operating the Xayaburi dam on the lower river in 2019; the Mekong River Commission, formed in 1995, coordinates the lower-basin countries, and the effects of the dams on fish, sediment and flood timing are debated.
--- context
The Mekong River was the corridor of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] and its capital [[place/angkor|Angkor]], and its delta was a theater of the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] (its early farming communities) and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Mekong is a river whose annual pulse feeds fish and rice for tens of millions, and that dams upstream change that pulse for people downstream, a relation in which those who control the flow can alter conditions for others across claim-lines that the river itself ignores. The reversing flow of the Tonlé Sap shows a natural rhythm that a whole society organized its seasons around. The reading would weaken if measurements show that the dams' net effect on downstream fish and sediment is smaller than the models suggest.
--- question 1
How much have upstream dams changed the flood pulse, fish migration and delta sediment?
--- question 2
How did early states such as Funan use the river for trade?

=== place/merv
title: Merv
type: place
date: c. 500 BCE – 1221
themes: trade, war, science
src: 31560021f717
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Merv was a major oasis city on the Silk Roads in what is now Turkmenistan, a city of the Achaemenid Persians, a capital of the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun and of the Seljuk sultans, and sacked by the Mongols in 1221.
--- fact 1
Merv lies in the Murghab delta oasis near the modern city of Mary; Bronze Age towns of the region, such as Gonur Depe, date to about 2000 BCE, and the walled city of Erk Kala dates from the Achaemenid period, from about the 6th century BCE.
--- fact 2
Arab forces took Merv in 651, after the last Sasanian king, Yazdegerd III, was killed near it.
--- fact 3
Abu Muslim launched the Abbasid Revolution from Merv in 747, and the caliph al-Ma'mun ruled from Merv from about 813 to 818 before moving to Baghdad.
--- fact 4
Merv was a Seljuk capital, notably under Sultan Sanjar (reigned 1118 to 1157), whose mausoleum survives, and some estimates suggest it was among the largest cities in the world in the 12th century.
--- fact 5
The geographer Yaqut, who visited in 1219, described its libraries.
--- fact 6
The Mongol army took Merv in 1221; Persian chronicles report hundreds of thousands to over a million killed, figures that modern historians regard as greatly exaggerated but that reflect a severe massacre and destruction.
--- fact 7
Merv was rebuilt on a smaller scale in the 15th century and was finally abandoned in the late 18th century.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed Ancient Merv in 1999.
--- context
Merv was a satrapy center of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], the political base of the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] at its start, and a victim of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]]. It lay on the routes of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. Merv sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Merv's fortunes followed the claim-lines drawn by others: an irrigated oasis city depends on which empire's exchange-web runs through it, and Achaemenids, Seleucids, Arabs, Seljuks and Mongols each held it. The events of 1221 show that a city, unlike a mobile camp, cannot leave, and the reported loss of its libraries suggests that a city's stored writing is as exposed as its people, though those reports are hard to verify. The reading would weaken if the true death toll and the extent of the library losses prove much smaller than the chronicles say.
--- question 1
How many people lived in Merv at its 12th-century peak, and how many died in 1221?
--- question 2
What did Merv's libraries hold, and what happened to them?

=== place/mesa-verde
title: Mesa Verde
type: place
date: c. 550 CE – 1300
themes: kinship, earth-systems
src: 83547c1c343f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mesa Verde is a plateau in the Four Corners region of southwestern Colorado, USA, where Ancestral Puebloan farmers lived from about 550 CE until about 1300 and built the cliff dwellings of the late 12th and 13th centuries.
--- fact 1
Mesa Verde lies in Montezuma County, Colorado; people used the area for thousands of years before farming communities settled the mesa, and they lived there continuously for about 700 years until the late 13th century.
--- fact 2
By the end of the 12th century residents began building cliff dwellings in sandstone alcoves beneath the mesa rim; the park holds more than 5,000 archaeological sites, including about 600 cliff dwellings, and Cliff Palace is among the largest cliff dwellings in North America.
--- fact 3
Tree-ring records show a severe drought from 1276 to 1299, and drought, resource depletion and overpopulation are all proposed as causes of instability; the last residents left by about 1300.
--- fact 4
Pueblo peoples such as the Hopi, Zuni and Acoma regard the Ancestral Puebloans as their ancestors, and many Pueblo people prefer "Ancestral Puebloan" to the older term "Anasazi".
--- fact 5
The ranchers Richard Wetherill and Charlie Mason reached Cliff Palace on 18 December 1888, artifact collecting followed, and the site was protected as a national park in 1906.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed Mesa Verde in 1978.
--- context
Mesa Verde followed the peak of [[place/chaco-canyon|Chaco Canyon]], whose great houses declined in the 12th century, and it was contemporary with [[place/cahokia|Cahokia]] in the Mississippi valley. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a place called "abandoned" in one telling is "left" in another: the people who built the cliff dwellings did not vanish, and their descendants keep the memory of the move in sound-code and in place rather than in a mark-code. Building homes in alcoves and leaving them within about a century shows a community adjusting to a drying climate, and the Observer records the leaving as a choice made under hunger and strain. The reading would weaken if the dwellings prove to have served mainly as seasonal storage or ritual sites instead of homes.
--- question 1
Why did residents build in the alcoves after centuries of living on the mesa top, and how much was defense against conflict?
--- question 2
Where did the people who left go, and how did they shape the later Pueblo communities of the Rio Grande?

=== place/mexico-city
title: Mexico City
type: place
date: 1521
themes: trade, war, earth-systems
src: 13c7cf919e20
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Mexico City is the capital of Mexico, built by the Spanish after 1521 on the site of Tenochtitlan in the former Lake Texcoco, and today one of the largest cities in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Mexico City was rebuilt by the Spanish on the ruins of Tenochtitlan after the conquest of 1521, with a municipality founded in 1524 as México Tenochtitlán, and it became the capital of the Viceroyalty of New Spain, established in 1535.
--- fact 2
The Metropolitan Cathedral, begun in the 1570s and completed in 1813, stands beside the main square, and the buried Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan was uncovered in excavations after workers found the Coyolxauhqui stone in 1978.
--- fact 3
The lakes were drained by canals and tunnels from the 17th century in response to floods, and the city now covers much of the old lakebed; parts of it have sunk as much as nine meters and some areas still sink by nearly 50 centimeters a year.
--- fact 4
Silver and goods moved through the capital in the Spanish trade system, and the Manila galleons linked New Spain to Asia through the Pacific port of Acapulco.
--- fact 5
Mexican independence was secured in September 1821, with Mexico City as the capital of the new state.
--- fact 6
The soft lakebed amplifies earthquakes: the quake of 19 September 1985 (magnitude about 8) killed about 10,000 people by official counts, with some estimates several times higher, and the quake of 19 September 2017 (magnitude 7.1) killed more than 300.
--- fact 7
The 2020 census counted 9,209,944 people in Mexico City and 21,804,515 in Greater Mexico City, and UNESCO inscribed the Historic Centre and Xochimilco in 1987.
--- context
Mexico City succeeded [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] after the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]], and it was a hub of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] and the [[event/manila-galleon-trade|Manila Galleon Trade]]. It was the capital when independence came in the [[event/latin-american-wars-of-independence|Latin American Wars of Independence]]. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Mexico City is a capital built directly on an earlier capital, with the Spanish cathedral and plaza placed near the old temple precinct, one claim-line drawn over another, and that the pull of the earlier city's site, its ancestor-weight, shaped the choice. The Mexica lived on the lake through causeways and chinampas, while the modern city sinks into the drained lakebed and is managed by pumping and drainage, so a site chosen for power carries its hazards into later centuries. The reading would weaken if records show that the Spanish chose the site mainly for practical reasons of labor and roads and not for its symbolic value.
--- question 1
How much of Tenochtitlan's street plan and canal system survives beneath the historic center?
--- question 2
How are earthquakes and subsidence changing where and how people live on the old lakebed?

=== place/mogao-caves
title: Mogao Caves
type: place
date: c. 366 CE
themes: religion, art, language
src: e6ecac4052a6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Mogao Caves are a complex of Buddhist cave temples cut into a cliff near Dunhuang, in Gansu, China, begun by tradition in 366 CE and decorated with murals and sculptures over about a thousand years at a crossroads of the Silk Roads.
--- fact 1
The Mogao Caves lie near the oasis of Dunhuang at the edge of the desert, where Silk Road routes met; Han China had established a commandery at Dunhuang in the late 2nd century BCE.
--- fact 2
By tradition the first cave was dug in 366 CE by the monk Yuezun, and work continued into the 14th century; the site has roughly 735 caves, about 490 of which hold murals or painted sculptures, with murals covering about 45,000 square meters.
--- fact 3
The art shows Indian, Central Asian and Chinese influences, and donors, including officials, merchants and local rulers, are painted into many caves.
--- fact 4
Dunhuang was under Tibetan rule from about 786 to 848, was governed by a local regime from 848, and passed to the Tangut state of Western Xia in about 1036.
--- fact 5
The "Library Cave" (Cave 17), sealed around the early 11th century, was opened in 1900 by Wang Yuanlu, a Daoist priest who guarded the caves; it held tens of thousands of manuscripts in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Sogdian, Khotanese and other languages, including a printed Diamond Sutra dated 868.
--- fact 6
Aurel Stein in 1907, Paul Pelliot in 1908 and others obtained large numbers of these manuscripts, now held in London, Paris and elsewhere; the removals are described in China as theft and by the acquiring institutions as purchases.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Mogao Caves in 1987, and the Dunhuang Academy has conserved them since 1944.
--- context
The Mogao Caves grew with the trade of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], and held documents written by [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] and others. They sit in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the record of a caravan crossroads survived because a room was sealed: contracts, letters, school exercises and Buddhist texts in many scripts, kept by dry air, a rare case of memory-outsourcing that preserved ordinary life and not only palace records. Donors painted into the murals beside sacred figures joined status-signal and devotion in one image. The scattering of the manuscripts across several countries shows how the record of one place can become the property of many claim-lines. The reason the room was sealed is debated, and the reading would weaken if it proves to have been routine disposal of worn-out texts and not a deliberate act of preservation.
--- question 1
Why was the Library Cave sealed, and by whom?
--- question 2
How should the manuscripts taken from the Mogao Caves be held and shared today?

=== place/monte-alban
title: Monte Alban
type: place
date: c. 500 BCE
themes: language, religion
src: f65c98c1f019
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Monte Alban was the hilltop capital of the Zapotec people in the Oaxaca Valley of Mexico, founded around 500 BCE and abandoned by about 800 CE.
--- fact 1
The site sits on a leveled mountaintop overlooking three valleys.
--- fact 2
Carved stones known as Danzantes, dated to the early centuries of the city, show figures with glyphs, part of an early Mesoamerican writing tradition.
--- fact 3
The city grew to a population of well over ten thousand in its Classic period.
--- fact 4
It maintained relations with Teotihuacan, and depictions at Teotihuacan show Zapotec visitors.
--- fact 5
It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1987.
--- context
Monte Alban was a major political center of Mesoamerica in the period between the [[culture/olmecs|Olmecs]] and [[place/teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]], and one of the birthplaces of [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] in the region. Monte Alban sits in the [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a city was placed on a mountaintop that had to be leveled by hand and supplied with water from far below. A capital sited for defense and display rather than convenience says something about what its founders valued.
--- question 1
Are the Danzantes true writing?

Monte Verde is an archaeological site in southern Chile that, for decades, was considered the best evidence that people lived in South America about 14,500 years ago, before the Clovis culture of North America; its age is now debated.

=== place/nalanda
title: Nalanda
type: place
date: c. 427 CE – 1200
themes: science, religion, language
src: 3e02ab271628
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nalanda is an archaeological site in Bihar, India, of a Buddhist monastic university founded in about the 5th century CE that drew students from across Asia until it was sacked around 1200.
--- fact 1
Nalanda was founded in about the 5th century CE, traditionally under the Gupta ruler Kumaragupta I, and grew into a large monastic university.
--- fact 2
The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang studied at Nalanda in the 630s and 640s and reported about 10,000 residents; another Chinese pilgrim, Yijing, described it in 675.
--- fact 3
Students and visitors came from China, Tibet, Korea, Central Asia and Southeast Asia, and in the 9th century a ruler of Srivijaya funded a monastery at Nalanda.
--- fact 4
Nalanda taught Buddhist philosophy, logic, grammar and medicine.
--- fact 5
Forces under Bakhtiyar Khalji sacked Nalanda around 1200; later traditions say its library burned for months, and historians debate how completely it was destroyed and whether its decline began earlier.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Archaeological Site of Nalanda Mahavihara in 2016, and a new Nalanda University opened in 2014.
--- context
Nalanda was founded under the [[culture/gupta-empire|Gupta Empire]], was visited by pilgrims from the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]] and supported by rulers of [[culture/srivijaya|Srivijaya]], and it fell during the raids that preceded the [[culture/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Nalanda concentrated teachers, texts and students from across Asia, an exchange-web of ideas in which monks carried texts and methods to China and Tibet, a case of memory-outsourcing in an institution and not in a single book. Its loss around 1200 shows that a store of knowledge depends on the institution that keeps it, and that the loss of an institution can be slower and less dramatic than later traditions of one great fire suggest. The reading would weaken if new evidence shows that Nalanda's decline was mainly the result of reduced patronage before its sack.
--- question 1
How much of Nalanda's decline came before the sack of around 1200?
--- question 2
How large was the library, and what did it hold?

=== place/nara
title: Nara
type: place
date: 710 – 784 CE
themes: religion, art, language
src: 53b0a1dd2c06
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nara is a city in Japan's Kansai region that was the capital, then called Heijō-kyō, from 710 to 784 CE, a period in which the Japanese court adopted Chinese models of city planning, writing and state Buddhism.
--- fact 1
Heijō-kyō was established as the capital in 710 under Empress Genmei, on a grid plan modeled on the Tang capital Chang'an.
--- fact 2
Court scholars compiled the chronicles Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720) at Nara, written with Chinese characters.
--- fact 3
A smallpox epidemic in 735 to 737 is estimated by some historians to have killed a quarter to a third of Japan's population, though the figure is uncertain.
--- fact 4
In 743 Emperor Shōmu ordered a giant bronze Buddha, which was dedicated at Tōdai-ji in 752; the statue is about 15 meters tall.
--- fact 5
The Shōsōin storehouse at Tōdai-ji preserves objects from the 8th-century court, including items that reflect trade along the Silk Roads.
--- fact 6
The court moved to Nagaoka in 784 and to Heian-kyō (Kyoto) in 794, a move often attributed in part to the political influence of Buddhist institutions at Nara.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara in 1998.
--- context
Nara imitated [[place/chang-an|Chang'an]], the capital of the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], and was succeeded as the capital by [[place/kyoto|Kyoto]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Nara shows a court importing a whole order at once: a Chinese city plan, Chinese characters, a state-sponsored Buddhism, and chronicles that used memory-outsourcing to fix the ruling house's origin story in writing. The Great Buddha was ordered a few years after a smallpox epidemic that killed a large part of the population, which suggests that a ruler facing mass death turned to a meaning-engine and to public works, though that link is an inference from the sources. The reading would weaken if the Buddha project proves to have been mainly a response to court rivalries.
--- question 1
How many people died in the epidemic of 735 to 737, and how did the losses affect the court's plans?
--- question 2
Why did the court leave Nara in 784?

=== place/newgrange
title: Newgrange
type: place
date: c. 3200 BCE
themes: religion, art, science
src: 6309057cca34
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Newgrange is a large passage tomb in the Boyne Valley of County Meath, Ireland, built about 3200 BCE by Neolithic farmers and aligned so that the winter solstice sunrise lights its inner chamber.
--- fact 1
Newgrange is a mound about 12 meters high and roughly 80 meters across, with a passage about 19 meters long leading to a cross-shaped chamber, and radiocarbon dates place its construction about 3200 BCE, older than the Great Pyramid of Giza.
--- fact 2
A "roof box" above the entrance admits sunlight along the passage for about 17 minutes around sunrise at the winter solstice.
--- fact 3
Kerbstones around the base carry carved designs, including spirals, and cremated remains of a small number of people were found in the chamber.
--- fact 4
The white quartz wall at the front was reconstructed in the 1970s, and whether it originally looked like that is debated.
--- fact 5
Newgrange was used again in later periods, and Roman coins and other offerings have been found there; later Irish tradition associated the Boyne complex with legendary figures.
--- fact 6
Newgrange lies with the nearby passage tombs at Knowth and Dowth, and UNESCO inscribed the Brú na Bóinne complex in 1993.
--- context
Newgrange belongs to the farming societies that followed [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in Europe, and it stands in the same broad tradition of monuments as [[place/stonehenge|Stonehenge]]; it predates the [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Newgrange has a chamber built so that sunrise light at the winter solstice enters for a short time, which suggests careful, sustained observation of the sky by a farming society that had no mark-code to record what the alignment meant. The meaning is inferred: a calendar, a grief-rite for the dead, or both, and the evidence supports the alignment more strongly than any one explanation of its purpose. The reading would weaken if the roof box is shown to be a later addition and not part of the original design.
--- question 1
What did the winter solstice alignment mean to the builders?
--- question 2
How many people built Newgrange, and how long did it take?

=== place/nile-river
title: Nile River
type: place
date: c. 5500 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade, technology
src: eeed1fa15df9
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Nile River is a river of northeastern Africa about 6,650 kilometers long, flowing north to the Mediterranean, whose annual flood supported farming and the states of Egypt and Nubia from about the 6th millennium BCE.
--- fact 1
The Nile flows north from the highlands of east and central Africa to the Mediterranean; its two main branches, the White Nile from the Lake Victoria region and the Blue Nile from Lake Tana in Ethiopia, meet at Khartoum, and the Ethiopian highlands supply most of the seasonal flood.
--- fact 2
Farming communities are documented in the Nile Valley and the Fayum from about the 6th millennium BCE, and the summer flood deposited silt that kept the valley fertile.
--- fact 3
Egyptian officials measured the flood on nilometers, and in later periods taxation was tied to its height.
--- fact 4
The states of Egypt, after the Unification of Egypt, and of Kush, upstream in Nubia, grew along the river.
--- fact 5
The Aswan High Dam, completed in 1970, ended the annual flood in Egypt and created Lake Nasser; about 100,000 Nubians were relocated, and monuments such as Abu Simbel were moved.
--- fact 6
Eleven countries share the Nile basin. A 1959 agreement between Egypt and Sudan allocated most of the river's measured flow to those two states, and Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, begun in 2011 and filled in stages from 2020, is a subject of dispute over water with Egypt and Sudan.
--- context
The Nile River is the axis of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] and the state formed in the [[event/unification-of-egypt|Unification of Egypt]], and it linked Egypt with the upstream [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (its earliest farming communities) and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Nile turned a predictable flood into a state: a river whose height could be measured, forecast and taxed rewarded record-keeping, an early case of pattern-taming tied to administration. The river's modern history shows that claim-lines drawn across water are harder to keep than lines on land, since a 1959 allocation between two states now faces the claims of upstream countries. The reading would weaken if the flood proves to have been too variable to forecast well enough to shape administration.
--- question 1
How much did variation in the Nile flood affect Egyptian politics and famine?
--- question 2
How will the water of the Nile be shared among the basin countries?

=== place/paris
title: Paris
type: place
date: c. 250 BCE
themes: science, art, war
src: bb4bfa29cf7f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Paris is the capital of France, on the Seine River, which grew from a settlement of the Gaulish Parisii on the Île de la Cité in about the 3rd century BCE into a Roman town, a medieval capital, a center of revolutions and one of Europe's largest cities.
--- fact 1
Paris grew from a settlement of the Parisii on the Île de la Cité in about the 3rd century BCE; Rome took the area in 52 BCE and built the town of Lutetia.
--- fact 2
Clovis made Paris his capital in about 508, and the Capetian kings made it the center of the French kingdom from 987; Notre-Dame was begun in 1163, and the University of Paris had developed by about 1200.
--- fact 3
Paris had perhaps 200,000 inhabitants around 1300, among the largest cities in Europe, and lost a large share of its people to the Black Death in 1348.
--- fact 4
The Bastille fell on 14 July 1789 during the French Revolution, and thousands of people were executed in Paris in the Reign of Terror of 1793 to 1794.
--- fact 5
Baron Haussmann rebuilt central Paris in the 1850s and 1860s with wide boulevards, sewers and parks.
--- fact 6
The suppression of the Paris Commune in 1871 killed many thousands of people in a week of fighting and executions, with estimates of about 10,000 to 20,000.
--- fact 7
Germany occupied Paris from June 1940 to August 1944; in July 1942 French police arrested about 13,000 Jews in the Vel' d'Hiv roundup, most of whom were later deported to Auschwitz.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed Paris, Banks of the Seine, in 1991.
--- context
Paris was the stage of the [[event/french-revolution|French Revolution]] and a center of the [[event/enlightenment|Enlightenment]]; it was founded on ground later ruled by the [[culture/romans|Romans]], and under German occupation in [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]] it was the scene of roundups that fed the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Paris has repeatedly been remade as a display of the state's story-glue and as a stage for its overthrow: a cathedral for the medieval monarchy, revolutionary crowds, and 19th-century boulevards planned for order and circulation. The same city organized the roundup of its Jewish residents in 1942 and now commemorates it, and the Observer records the deaths of the Terror, the Commune and the deportations as suffering under different regimes. The reading would weaken if the city's monuments prove to have been shaped more by local building needs than by successive regimes' claims.
--- question 1
How large was Paris at its medieval peak, and how did the Black Death change it?
--- question 2
How have the Commune and the 1942 roundup been remembered in French public life?

=== place/pasargadae
title: Pasargadae
type: place
date: c. 550 BCE
themes: art, war, religion
src: 3d19828c35db
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pasargadae was the first capital of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great in about 550 BCE on the plateau of Fars in present-day Iran, and the site of his tomb.
--- fact 1
Pasargadae lies in Fars province, southwestern Iran, about 90 kilometers northeast of Persepolis.
--- fact 2
Cyrus the Great founded Pasargadae after defeating the Median king Astyages, in about 550 BCE, and it served as a royal seat until Darius I and his successors developed Persepolis and Susa.
--- fact 3
Pasargadae has an audience hall, residential palaces, a gate with a relief of a winged figure, and a formal garden with water channels that is regarded as an early example of the Persian garden.
--- fact 4
Stonemasons from other parts of the empire, including Lydia and Ionia in western Anatolia, contributed to its masonry, and its architecture combines Persian, Elamite, Mesopotamian and Anatolian elements.
--- fact 5
The Tomb of Cyrus, a stepped base topped by a gabled stone chamber, stands at the site; Greek writers report that Alexander visited it and ordered its restoration after it had been robbed.
--- fact 6
Inscriptions naming Cyrus appear at Pasargadae, though some scholars argue that they were added under Darius I.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Pasargadae in 2004.
--- context
Pasargadae was the first capital of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] and was later overshadowed by [[place/persepolis|Persepolis]]. Cyrus, its founder, took [[place/babylon|Babylon]] in 539 BCE, and Alexander visited the site during [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. Pasargadae sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Pasargadae shows an empire assembling its style from its subjects: masons from Lydia, motifs from Mesopotamia and Elam, and a garden layout later copied across Persian courts, an exchange-web of craftsmen and ideas made visible in stone. A founder's tomb that a later conqueror chose to visit and restore suggests that story-glue about kingship could pass across dynasties and even across enemies. The reading would weaken if the building phases prove to belong mostly to Darius I, since the site would then tell more about a later king's claim on Cyrus's legacy.
--- question 1
Which buildings at Pasargadae date to Cyrus and which to later kings?
--- question 2
What were the roles of Pasargadae and Persepolis in court ritual?
