=== person/ashurbanipal
title: Ashurbanipal
type: person
date: c. 685 – 631 BCE
themes: war, language
src: 76c27ecadf78
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ashurbanipal (about 685 to about 631 BCE) was king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire from 669 BCE. He is known for his conquests, for the royal library at Nineveh, and for palace reliefs showing war and lion hunts.
--- fact 1
Ashurbanipal was born about 685 BCE, a son of Esarhaddon, and became king of Assyria in 669 BCE, with his brother Shamash-shum-ukin as king of Babylon.
--- fact 2
Assyrian forces sacked Thebes in Egypt in 663 BCE, ending Kushite rule in Egypt.
--- fact 3
Shamash-shum-ukin rebelled in 652 BCE, and Ashurbanipal took Babylon in 648 BCE after a long war. In campaigns of about 647 to 646 BCE he defeated Elam, and Susa was destroyed in the campaign of about 646 BCE.
--- fact 4
His libraries at Nineveh, found in the 19th century, held tens of thousands of clay tablets and fragments, including a version of the Epic of Gilgamesh. He boasted of his own scribal education.
--- fact 5
His inscriptions and reliefs record brutal treatment of defeated enemies, including mass deportation, mutilation and execution.
--- fact 6
He died about 631 BCE (some place it about 627). Nineveh fell to Babylonians and Medes in 612 BCE.
--- context
Ashurbanipal ruled the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]. His library preserved writings in a tradition reaching back to the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]]; see [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ashurbanipal gathered the written memory of a whole civilization while recording, in the same palace, the punishment of those who resisted him. The library shows memory-outsourcing used as a tool of rule: collecting omen and ritual texts was a way of commanding knowledge as well as territory. The reading would weaken if the library proved to have been assembled mainly by earlier kings and scribes, making his own role smaller than his boasts suggest.
--- question 1
How much of the library's collecting was his own initiative and how much the work of his scribes?

=== person/attila
title: Attila
type: person
date: c. 406 – 453 CE
themes: war, trade
src: 0cd2d5508567
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Attila (about 406 to 453) was ruler of the Huns from 434, first with his brother Bleda and from about 445 alone. He led Hunnic armies against the Eastern and Western Roman empires, and almost all written sources about him were composed by Greeks and Romans.
--- fact 1
Attila's birth year is uncertain, with estimates from the 390s to the early 400s. He became joint ruler in 434 and sole ruler after Bleda died, probably about 445.
--- fact 2
After the Treaty of Margus (435) the Eastern Roman Empire doubled its yearly gold tribute, and after 443 it is reported to have reached 2,100 Roman pounds.
--- fact 3
In 451 he invaded Gaul and fought a Roman and Visigothic army at the Catalaunian Plains. In 452 he invaded northern Italy. He withdrew both times.
--- fact 4
He died in 453 after his wedding to Ildico. Priscus, as reported by Jordanes, describes a severe bleed. A later claim that his wife killed him is rejected by most historians. His empire broke up within a few years.
--- fact 5
Priscus, an envoy who visited his court in 449, left the only known eyewitness description. Attila's capital and tomb have not been found.
--- context
Attila's power was based on the middle Danube in [[region/europe|Europe]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and his campaigns added to the pressures behind the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]]. See [[culture/romans|Romans]], [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Huns left no writing of their own, so a ruler remembered as one of history's great enemies is known only through his opponents. The gold paid to him was an exchange-web run in reverse, payment in return for peace. A specialist could argue that Roman diplomacy and a Germanic revolt, more than any dependence on one man, broke the Hunnic polity, which would weaken a reading of the empire as personal.
--- question 1
How far can the accounts of his court be trusted, given that the main eyewitness was an envoy of the empire he was pressing?
--- question 2
Where were his capital and his grave?

=== person/augustus
title: Augustus
type: person
date: 63 BCE – 14 CE
themes: war, trade, art
src: f3ea721fd09a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Augustus (63 BCE to 14 CE) was the first Roman emperor, who ended a century of civil war and built the imperial system that lasted for centuries.
--- fact 1
Born Gaius Octavius, he was the adopted heir of Julius Caesar.
--- fact 2
After defeating rivals, he took the name Augustus in 27 BCE while keeping republican forms.
--- fact 3
His reign began the Pax Romana, a long period of relative stability.
--- fact 4
He reorganized the army, provinces, and taxation.
--- context
See [[culture/romans|Romans]] and [[place/rome|Rome]]. Augustus created the political shape of the empire that dominated the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a skilled piece of story-glue: a monarch who insisted he was only 'first citizen', so that the old republican stories continued to serve a new order.
--- question 1
How deliberate was the pretense of restoring the republic?

=== person/ba-trieu
title: Ba Trieu
type: person
date: c. 226 – 248 CE
themes: war, kinship
src: 26368fcc8c89
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ba Trieu (Trieu Thi Trinh; 226 to 248 CE) led an uprising against the Chinese Eastern Wu administration of northern Vietnam in 248 CE, becoming a lasting symbol of Vietnamese resistance to foreign rule.
--- fact 1
She is said to have raised a rebel force in Cuu Chan, in present-day Thanh Hoa province, at about age nineteen, alongside her brother Trieu Quoc Dat.
--- fact 2
Later chronicles describe her riding into battle on an elephant in gold-decorated armor, though these vivid details come from accounts written many centuries after her death rather than contemporary record.
--- fact 3
Eastern Wu sent the general Lu Yin to suppress the revolt; after several months of fighting, her forces were defeated later in 248.
--- fact 4
Vietnamese tradition holds that she died by suicide on Mount Tung rather than be captured, at about age twenty-three; a temple to her still stands near the site.
--- context
Ba Trieu's revolt took place in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], against the Chinese administration that had controlled the region since the Han conquest. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ba Trieu is known almost entirely through Vietnamese chronicles written centuries after her death, during periods when a symbol of resistance to Chinese rule served a clear political purpose. The core event, a suppressed revolt in 248, likely happened; the vivid personal details are harder to separate from later invention.
--- question 1
How much of her story was shaped by later Vietnamese nation-building rather than contemporary memory?

=== person/ban-zhao
title: Ban Zhao
type: person
date: c. 45 – 117 CE
themes: language, kinship
src: ed6a0972eef3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ban Zhao (about 45 to about 117 CE) was a Chinese historian and scholar of the Eastern Han dynasty who helped complete the Book of Han after her brother's death and wrote Lessons for Women. She was also a tutor and adviser at court.
--- fact 1
Ban Zhao was born about 45 CE (some sources say about 49) into a scholarly family. Her father Ban Biao and her brother Ban Gu wrote much of the Book of Han (Hanshu), a history of the Western Han dynasty.
--- fact 2
Ban Gu died in prison in 92 CE with the history unfinished, and Ban Zhao helped complete it by supplying the missing part of the Eight Tables.
--- fact 3
She served at the imperial court as a tutor to the empress and her ladies and, from about 106 CE, as an adviser to the regent Empress Deng.
--- fact 4
She is believed to have written Lessons for Women (Nüjie), in seven chapters, about 106 CE, though the date is not firmly established. It argues that girls should be educated and sets out rules of conduct for wives.
--- fact 5
Later writers used Lessons for Women as a standard text for teaching girls obedience, and modern scholars disagree on whether it reinforced or challenged limits on women.
--- fact 6
She died about 117 CE; some sources say about 120.
--- context
Ban Zhao worked under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one woman helped complete the standard history of a dynasty and also wrote the text most used to shape women's conduct, so her legacy points in two directions at once. Because Lessons for Women was copied by later readers who valued its advice, the record may reflect what they wanted from her as much as what she meant. The reading would weaken if evidence showed the text was heavily edited after her lifetime.
--- question 1
Did she intend Lessons for Women as a defense of women's education, as a guide to conduct, or both?

=== person/basawan
title: Basawan
type: person
date: c. 1560 – 1600
themes: art
src: f0618779590d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Basawan was a Hindu painter in the imperial workshop of the Mughal emperor Akbar, active from about 1560 to about 1600. His birth and death dates are unknown, so the dates here mark his period of activity. Akbar's historian ranked him among the best of the workshop's more than a hundred painters.
--- fact 1
Almost nothing is known of Basawan's life beyond his paintings and a brief praise in Abu'l-Fazl's Ain-i-Akbari. He was a Hindu and probably joined the imperial workshop as a young man in the 1560s.
--- fact 2
Akbar reigned from 1556 to 1605. The workshop's Persian masters, Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad, had trained in the tradition of Behzād and directed Indian artists in producing vast illustrated manuscripts, among them the Hamzanama, 1,400 large paintings.
--- fact 3
Abu'l-Fazl praised his skill in composition, faces, colour and portraiture, and wrote that many critics preferred him even to the celebrated Daswanth.
--- fact 4
His name is written in the margins of paintings in the Razmnama (a Persian translation of the Mahabharata), the Darabnama, the Akbarnama and small books of Persian poetry made in Lahore in the 1590s. In large history manuscripts he often designed the composition and another artist added the colour.
--- fact 5
From 1580 Jesuit missionaries brought European engravings and paintings to Akbar's court. He studied them closely, adapting their shading and deep space, and was among the first Indian painters to absorb European modelling and perspective.
--- fact 6
He was active until about 1600. His son Manohar became a leading painter under Jahangir. His paintings are held in the Victoria and Albert Museum, the British Library and the Chester Beatty Library.
--- context
Basawan worked in the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. His workshop's founders trained in the tradition of [[person/behzad|Behzad]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes an imperial workshop that fused Persian masters, Hindu painters and European prints into one style, a deliberate joining of tool-lineages by a ruler who wanted his history illustrated. What survives of the individual is a name in a margin, written by librarians, and one passage of praise, so the personality that scholars trace is reconstructed from a labour that was often divided between designer and colourist. Any reading of him rests on attribution and should be held loosely.
--- question 1
How can his own hand be separated from that of the colourists and finishers who worked on the same pages?

=== person/basil-ii
title: Basil II
type: person
date: 958 CE – 1025
themes: war, religion
src: 8c6697e6f2d0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Basil II (958 to 1025) was Byzantine emperor for nearly fifty years, the longest reign in Roman or Byzantine imperial history, remembered above all for his decades-long war against the First Bulgarian Empire.
--- fact 1
He became co-emperor as a child in 960 but did not gain full personal control of the government until his twenties, after suppressing rebellions by powerful aristocratic generals including Bardas Skleros and Bardas Phokas.
--- fact 2
His long campaigns against Bulgaria culminated in the decisive Battle of Kleidion in 1014, after which Bulgaria was absorbed into the Byzantine Empire by 1018, earning him the later epithet "Bulgar-slayer" (Boulgaroktonos).
--- fact 3
A story recorded by later chroniclers claims he blinded thousands of Bulgarian prisoners after Kleidion, leaving one eye to each of every hundredth man to lead the rest home; modern historians debate how literally this account should be taken.
--- fact 4
He left the imperial treasury full and the empire at its greatest territorial extent since the 7th century, but died without an heir, and the strong central rule he had imposed did not survive his less capable successors.
--- context
Basil II ruled the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] in the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Basil II's reign is often read backward from the Byzantine Empire's later decline, framing him as the last strong emperor of a golden age. That framing depends partly on hindsight: the empire's later troubles came from succession and external pressures Basil could not have controlled from beyond the grave, whatever the soundness of his own institutions.
--- question 1
How much of the Byzantine Empire's difficulties after 1025 stemmed from institutional weaknesses already present under Basil, rather than simply the absence of an equally capable successor?

=== person/behzad
title: Behzad
type: person
date: c. 1455 – 1535
themes: art
src: 4ce0fb1badd9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kamāl ud-Dīn Behzād (also spelled Bihzad, born about 1455 to 1460 and died about 1535) is the most celebrated painter in the Persian tradition. He led the royal manuscript workshops at Herat and later Tabriz, and many works are attributed to him on grounds of style.
--- fact 1
Behzād was probably born about 1455 to 1460 in Herat, capital of the Timurid dynasty founded by [[person/timur|Timur]]. Orphaned young, he was brought up by the painter Mirak Naqqash, head of the royal library, and was protected by the poet and minister Mir Ali-Shir Nava'i.
--- fact 2
Under Sultan Husayn Bayqara, who ruled Herat from 1469 to 1506, he became the leading artist of the royal kitabkhana, the library workshop. His most famous surviving works date from this period, including paintings in a Bustan of Sa'di (1488) and a Khamsa of Nizami (1494 to 1495).
--- fact 3
After Herat fell to the Uzbeks in 1506 and then to the Safavid Shah Ismail I in about 1510, he moved to Tabriz, where in 1522 Ismail appointed him director of the royal library. He died about 1535.
--- fact 4
A legend says that during the Battle of Chaldiran in 1514 the shah hid Behzād in a cave, in some tellings together with a famous calligrapher, because he valued him more than his army.
--- fact 5
His figures have individual faces and gestures and are often busy with everyday tasks. He often hid his signature discreetly in the architecture of a painting.
--- fact 6
His followers Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad trained in his tradition and went on to found the Mughal imperial workshop under Humayun and Akbar.
--- fact 7
His name became a byword for excellence, which led to many false attributions. Scholars debate which works are by his own hand.
--- context
Behzād worked first in the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]] and then in the [[culture/safavid-empire|Safavid Empire]], on the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a royal library workshop, where calligraphers, illuminators, painters and binders worked together, is an institution of memory-outsourcing: it made the books through which a court remembered its poems and histories. The individual name attached to such a collective product then became a standard of excellence, and standards attract false claims. Much of what is called Behzād's may be the work of others, and for many pictures style is the only guide.
--- question 1
Which of the paintings attributed to Behzād are by his own hand rather than his workshop?

=== person/buster-keaton
title: Buster Keaton
type: person
date: 1895 – 1966
themes: art
src: 705e5e51fe72
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Buster Keaton (1895 to 1966) was an American silent-film comedian, writer and director known as the deadpan "Great Stone Face," who built his comedy on engineered physical stunts he performed himself. Ranked with Charlie Chaplin among silent comedy's defining stars, he made *The General* (1926), now considered among the greatest films ever made.
--- fact 1
Born in 1895 into a vaudeville family, Keaton performed from age three in the act The Three Keatons, taking falls that built his later physical control.
--- fact 2
He entered film in 1917 under Roscoe "Fatty" Arbuckle, and by 1920 was writing, directing and starring in his own comedies, moving into features with *Our Hospitality* (1923).
--- fact 3
*Sherlock Jr.* (1924) placed him inside a shifting film-dream using in-camera measurement rather than editing tricks, still studied for its daring.
--- fact 4
*The Navigator* (1924), about passengers adrift on an ocean liner, was his biggest commercial success of the decade.
--- fact 5
*The General* (1926), built around full-scale trains and a locomotive plunged off a burning bridge, flopped on release but is now ranked among cinema's greatest films.
--- fact 6
In *Steamboat Bill, Jr.* (1928) he performed one of the most dangerous shots ever filmed, standing where a falling house facade's open window would clear him.
--- fact 7
A 1928 MGM contract, taken under financial pressure, cost him creative control; the next decade brought decline, divorce and alcoholism.
--- fact 8
Rediscovered by critics from the 1950s, he received an honorary Academy Award in 1959 and died of lung cancer in 1966.
--- fact 9
His influence runs through later stunt performers such as Jackie Chan, who has cited Keaton's real, visible danger as a model.
--- context
Keaton worked in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], building comedies from the era's trains, boats and machinery. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a performer trained from infancy to fall safely, so his stunts read as a disciplined tool-lineage of bodily skill rather than improvised daring. His blank face amid engineered chaos worked as a status-signal, drawing attention by withholding the reaction expected of it. That *The General* failed on release while a later generation built the reputation it now holds shows a species revising its own memory-outsourcing of achievement well after the fact. The 1930s decline reads as plain record, unlike the reverent tone in which his stunts' zero margin for error is retold by admirers.
--- question 1
Why did *The General* fail at the box office in 1926 despite the technical ambition later critics would call unmatched?

=== person/caravaggio
title: Caravaggio
type: person
date: 1571 – 1610
themes: art, religion
src: 07180f1ec050
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Michelangelo Merisi, called Caravaggio (1571 to 1610), was an Italian painter who transformed European painting around 1600 with harsh theatrical light and street-level realism. His turbulent life ended at 38, but his influence spread across Europe within a generation.
--- fact 1
Caravaggio was born in Milan in September 1571. His name comes from the nearby town his family came from. He trained in Milan with Simone Peterzano and arrived in Rome about 1592.
--- fact 2
Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte took him into his household. His first public commission, two large paintings of St Matthew for the Contarelli Chapel in San Luigi dei Francesi (1599 to 1600), caused a sensation. Several later altarpieces were rejected as indecorous.
--- fact 3
Police records list brawls, assaults and insults. In 1606 he killed a man, Ranuccio Tomassoni, and fled Rome under sentence of death. He then lived in Naples, Malta and Sicily.
--- fact 4
He died in July 1610 at Porto Ercole in Tuscany, on his way to Rome hoping for a papal pardon.
--- fact 5
He painted directly on the canvas, apparently without preparatory drawings, from live models posed under a single strong light. His backgrounds are dark and bare (tenebrism).
--- fact 6
Key works include Judith Beheading Holofernes (about 1598 to 1599), The Calling of Saint Matthew (1599 to 1600) and Supper at Emmaus (1601).
--- fact 7
The "Caravaggisti" included Orazio and Artemisia Gentileschi, Jusepe de Ribera, Georges de La Tour and the Utrecht painters, and through them his influence reached [[person/diego-velazquez|Diego Velázquez]] and [[person/rembrandt-van-rijn|Rembrandt van Rijn]]. A 1951 exhibition in Milan revived a reputation that had faded.
--- context
Caravaggio worked in Rome, Naples, Malta and Sicily in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. His followers included [[person/artemisia-gentileschi|Artemisia Gentileschi]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that placing sacred events in the streets of contemporary Rome, with dirty feet and hired models, was read by some patrons as truth and by others as indecorum, which shows a meaning-engine policing its own images. The violent biography is well documented in police records, which makes it easy to read the life into the pictures. His reputation faded and returned after a 1951 exhibition, so the present esteem is a recent judgment.
--- question 1
How much of his working method, without drawings, can be recovered from surviving evidence?

=== person/catherine-de-medici
title: Catherine de' Medici
type: person
date: 1519 – 1589
themes: kinship, religion, war
src: 287573c5bd7f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Catherine de' Medici (1519 to 1589) was queen of France and, as mother to three kings, one of the most powerful figures in France during the Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots.
--- fact 1
Born into the powerful Medici banking family of Florence, she married the future Henry II of France in 1533 and became queen in 1547.
--- fact 2
After Henry's death in 1559 she served as regent and chief political influence for her sons Francis II, Charles IX and Henry III during a period of escalating conflict between Catholics and Protestant Huguenots.
--- fact 3
She is widely held responsible, though the exact extent of her role is debated, for approving the assassination of Huguenot leaders that triggered the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre in August 1572, in which thousands of Huguenots were killed in Paris and other cities.
--- fact 4
She pursued marriage alliances and diplomacy across Europe to secure the Valois dynasty's position, though her efforts did not prevent the eventual extinction of the Valois line with the death of her last son in 1589.
--- context
Catherine de' Medici ruled at the French court in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and its Wars of Religion. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Catherine's later reputation as a scheming, sinister figure was shaped heavily by hostile Huguenot pamphlets written during her lifetime and repeated by later historians; separating a mother managing three weak sons' survival in a genuinely lethal court from a caricature of poison and plotting is not straightforward from the surviving record.
--- question 1
How much personal responsibility did Catherine bear for the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre, versus reacting to a crisis that spiraled beyond any single actor's control?

=== person/chandragupta-maurya
title: Chandragupta Maurya
type: person
date: c. 340 – 297 BCE
themes: war, trade, religion
src: 31950a31b687
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Chandragupta Maurya (about 340 to 297 BCE) founded the Maurya Empire, the first political power to unite most of the Indian subcontinent, after overthrowing the Nanda dynasty of Magadha around 321 BCE.
--- fact 1
With guidance from the strategist Chanakya (Kautilya), Chandragupta seized the throne of Magadha and built an administration described in the Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft attributed to Chanakya.
--- fact 2
Around 305 BCE he fought the Diadoch ruler Seleucus I Nicator; the resulting settlement ceded territory in the northwest to the Maurya and included a marriage alliance, while Seleucus received war elephants in return.
--- fact 3
The Greek ambassador Megasthenes, sent to Chandragupta's court at Pataliputra, wrote the Indica, a major but now fragmentary source on Mauryan India.
--- fact 4
Later Jain tradition holds that Chandragupta abdicated, became an ascetic under Bhadrabahu, and died by ritual fasting (sallekhana) at Shravanabelagola in Karnataka, though this account survives mainly in sources written well after his reign.
--- context
Chandragupta founded the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and his settlement with the Seleucid successor state opened diplomatic contact between South Asia and the Hellenistic Mediterranean. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a kingdom built partly on a treatise about statecraft and control ended, in the tradition at least, with its founder walking away from all of it to starve himself in a cave. Whether that ending reflects an actual late-life conversion or a later Jain community's wish to claim him is hard to separate from the record.
--- question 1
How much of the Arthashastra reflects Chandragupta's own reign rather than later compilation under his successors?

=== person/charlemagne
title: Charlemagne
type: person
date: c. 748 – 814 CE
themes: war, religion
src: dfde36c76c72
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Charlemagne (c. 748 to 814) was king of the Franks who conquered much of western and central Europe and was crowned emperor by the pope in Rome on Christmas Day 800.
--- fact 1
He became sole king of the Franks in 771.
--- fact 2
He conquered the Lombards in Italy, the Saxons, and parts of Spain's northern border.
--- fact 3
Pope Leo III crowned him emperor on 25 December 800.
--- fact 4
His court promoted a revival of learning, the Carolingian Renaissance, and the spread of a clear minuscule script.
--- fact 5
His empire was divided among his descendants after his death in 814 and, under the Treaty of Verdun in 843, split into three kingdoms.
--- context
Charlemagne's empire revived the imperial title in the west after the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] and was recognized by, but competed with, the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]]. His successors faced raids from the [[culture/norse|Norse]]. Charlemagne sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler who could barely write his own name sponsored one of the largest projects of copying texts in the early Middle Ages. Much ancient literature survives because Carolingian scribes copied it in a new, legible script.
--- question 1
How unified was Charlemagne's empire in practice?

=== person/charles-darwin
title: Charles Darwin
type: person
date: 1809 – 1882
themes: science, religion, earth-systems
src: acab33b52570
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Charles Darwin (1809 to 1882) was an English naturalist who proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection, published in 'On the Origin of Species' in 1859.
--- fact 1
His voyage on HMS Beagle (1831 to 1836) gave him observations that led to his theory.
--- fact 2
Alfred Russel Wallace independently reached the same idea, and the two announced it together in 1858.
--- fact 3
'The Descent of Man' (1871) applied the theory to human origins.
--- fact 4
Modern genetics later provided the mechanism of inheritance that Darwin lacked.
--- context
Darwin's work underlies the deep-time history in this vault; see [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] and [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]]. He worked in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the theory gives the species its own place in the tree of life. It placed humans among the animals without diminishing them, a claim many found hard to accept.

=== person/charles-rennie-mackintosh
title: Charles Rennie Mackintosh
type: person
date: 1868 – 1928
themes: art
src: 7692e31d84c5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Charles Rennie Mackintosh (1868 to 1928) was a Glasgow architect-designer who led the "Glasgow Style" of Art Nouveau, treating buildings, furniture and interiors as one unified art. His Glasgow School of Art and total interior schemes made him a major influence on European design, though home recognition came only after his death.
--- fact 1
Born in Glasgow in 1868, Mackintosh trained as an architect while attending evening classes at the Glasgow School of Art, where he met his future wife, artist Margaret Macdonald.
--- fact 2
With Macdonald, her sister Frances and Herbert MacNair, he formed "The Four," a symbolist group whose style became the Glasgow Style.
--- fact 3
At Honeyman and Keppie he won an anonymous 1896 competition for his own art school's new building; its 1909 completion made his reputation.
--- fact 4
He then designed houses, tearooms and a school around Glasgow, including Hill House (1902-1904), the Willow Tearooms (1903) and Scotland Street School (1903-1906), with Macdonald designing the furnishings throughout.
--- fact 5
His towering ladder-back chairs, shaped as much to define a room as to be sat in, became his best-known design.
--- fact 6
Commissions dried up after 1910, through changing taste and his own difficult manner with clients; by 1914 he had left Glasgow for England.
--- fact 7
In the 1920s he and Macdonald settled in southern France, where he turned to watercolours; he returned to London for cancer treatment and died there in 1928, in obscurity and hardship.
--- fact 8
Though nearly forgotten in Britain by his death, his work had already shaped Josef Hoffmann and the Wiener Werkstätte via the 1900 Vienna Secession, and now ranks among the era's most important architect-designers.
--- context
Mackintosh worked in Glasgow, within [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], for the tearoom trade funding his interiors.
--- observer
The Observer sees a status-signal built for one city's cafes that became, without its maker's help, an export. Mackintosh treated a building, its chairs and its wall stencils as one problem, a case of making an environment cohere as a single statement of taste; his chairs traded comfort for silhouette. His ideas reached Vienna and shaped Hoffmann's workshop even as his home market lost interest, reputation and trade travelling along separate exchange-webs. His late turn to watercolours reads as withdrawal, not collapse, and the admiring frame around his career is largely later historians' work.
--- question 1
How much of his post-1910 loss of commissions was changing taste rather than his own manner with clients?

=== person/chico-mendes
title: Chico Mendes
type: person
date: 1944 – 1988
themes: earth-systems, trade, kinship
src: 191b7595bc9b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chico Mendes (Francisco Alves Mendes Filho, 1944 to 1988) was a Brazilian rubber tapper, union leader and environmental activist in the state of Acre. He organized rubber tappers to defend the forest against clearing for cattle ranching and was murdered in December 1988.
--- fact 1
Chico Mendes was born on 15 December 1944 near Xapuri, Acre, into a family of rubber tappers (seringueiros).
--- fact 2
He helped found the rural workers' union in Xapuri in 1977 and was among the founders of the Workers' Party in Acre in 1980.
--- fact 3
He organized *empates*, non-violent standoffs in which tappers and their families stood in front of crews clearing forest.
--- fact 4
In 1985 he helped found the National Council of Rubber Tappers, which promoted protected "extractive reserves" where residents could harvest forest products.
--- fact 5
In 1987 he lobbied the Inter-American Development Bank in the United States over highway funding in the Amazon, and received a United Nations Environment Programme Global 500 award.
--- fact 6
He was shot dead at his home in Xapuri on 22 December 1988. The rancher Darly Alves da Silva and his son Darci were convicted of the murder in 1990.
--- fact 7
Extractive reserves became a legal category in Brazil in 1990.
--- context
Chico Mendes worked in the [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]] during the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], amid the forest clearing of the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]. The forest he defended has a long human history; see [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Mendes joined a labor tactic, standing bodies in front of chainsaws, to an ecological argument, and that success came in the form of law only after his death. Ranchers described the forest as open land while tappers described it as a home and an income, so each side's claim-line rested on a different account of the same trees. A specialist could argue that extractive reserves have had mixed economic results for residents, which would weaken the claim that his approach protected both forest and livelihoods.
--- question 1
How well have extractive reserves protected forest and livelihoods since 1990?

=== person/christopher-dresser
title: Christopher Dresser
type: person
date: 1834 – 1904
themes: art, technology
src: 275278303db9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Christopher Dresser (1834 to 1904) was a botanist turned designer, now regarded as one of the first modern industrial designers. He worked as an independent consultant for dozens of manufacturers and produced geometric silverware and ceramics that anticipated 20th-century modernism.
--- fact 1
Dresser was born in Glasgow in 1834 and trained at the Government School of Design in London. He lectured on botany and in 1859 received an honorary doctorate from the University of Jena for research into plant structure.
--- fact 2
He moved into design in the 1860s and published The Art of Decorative Design (1862) and Principles of Decorative Design (1873), arguing that ornament should follow the structural logic of natural forms rather than copy their surface.
--- fact 3
In 1876 and 1877 he travelled to Japan, one of the first Western designers to do so, partly on commission from Tiffany & Co. to report on Japanese art and manufacturing.
--- fact 4
He designed for Minton, Wedgwood, Coalbrookdale, Hukin & Heath and James Dixon & Sons, and was art director of the Linthorpe Art Pottery. He died in 1904.
--- fact 5
He reduced plants and shells to underlying geometry and embraced industrial methods, electroplating and moulded earthenware, that many reformers rejected. Key works include an electroplated teapot (1879), a toast rack (about 1880) and the Linthorpe "sea urchin" vase.
--- fact 6
He was long overshadowed by William Morris and the craft wing of the Aesthetic Movement and has been substantially rediscovered since the late 20th century. His insistence that a mass-made object could be both functional and beautiful anticipated principles usually credited to the Bauhaus.
--- context
Dresser worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], designing for the machine at the height of the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], in contrast to [[person/william-morris|William Morris]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a botanist who built ornament from the structure of plants rather than their surface, and who accepted the machine that many contemporaries rejected. That his work was long overshadowed by the craft wing of his own movement shows a later story-glue about design, the handmade as honest, deciding which designers are remembered. His recent rediscovery as an ancestor of modernism is the reverse move, choosing a lineage backward. Both are readings of a body of objects that largely speak for themselves.
--- question 1
How much of the work sold under his name was designed by him rather than by manufacturers' staff?

=== person/christopher-wren
title: Christopher Wren
type: person
date: 1632 – 1723
themes: art, science, technology
src: 9f20e8f547ca
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Christopher Wren (1632 to 1723) was an English mathematician and astronomer, a founder of the Royal Society, who became an architect after the Great Fire of London in 1666. He designed more than fifty City churches and a new St Paul's Cathedral, completed in 1710.
--- fact 1
Wren was born in East Knoyle, Wiltshire, the son of a clergyman. He was appointed professor of astronomy at Gresham College at 25, then Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford, and was a founding member of the Royal Society in 1662.
--- fact 2
His architectural career began with the Sheldonian Theatre in Oxford. The Great Fire of September 1666 destroyed old St Paul's and most of the City.
--- fact 3
He was appointed Surveyor of the King's Works in 1669 and spent the rest of his working life on the City's parish churches and on St Paul's. He died in 1723, aged 90, and is buried in the cathedral crypt.
--- fact 4
He combined the classical vocabulary of Inigo Jones and Palladio with French and Italian Baroque elements he studied on a research trip to Paris, the only time he travelled outside England.
--- fact 5
The dome of St Paul's is three independent shells nested inside one another, so that exterior profile, interior ceiling and structural core could each be optimised separately.
--- fact 6
Key works include the Royal Observatory, Greenwich (1675 to 1676), the Royal Hospital Chelsea (1682 to 1692) and the Monument to the Great Fire (1671 to 1677).
--- fact 7
Many City churches were later damaged or demolished, by Victorian redevelopment and by German bombing, but a substantial number still stand. Photographs of the dome above the smoke of the Blitz in 1940 circulated worldwide.
--- context
Wren worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]], and drew on [[person/andrea-palladio|Andrea Palladio]]'s classical vocabulary. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a fire which destroyed a city also gave a mathematician the chance to become its chief builder, so that catastrophe became the hinge of a career and a skyline. The triple-shell dome is pattern-taming applied to stone. St Paul's later became a story-glue for national endurance through the photographs of 1940, a meaning laid on the building by later events rather than one Wren intended.
--- question 1
Which of the City churches he designed survive in their original form?

=== person/cixi
title: Cixi
type: person
date: 1835 – 1908
themes: war, kinship
src: b3c21ce93957
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Empress Dowager Cixi (1835 to 1908) was the dominant political figure of late Qing China, ruling as regent for most of the period from 1861 to 1908.
--- fact 1
She became regent in 1861 for her young son, the Tongzhi Emperor, and later for the Guangxu Emperor.
--- fact 2
Her period includes the Self-Strengthening Movement, an effort to modernize China's defenses and industry.
--- fact 3
She supported the Boxers in 1900, and after their defeat she pursued the 'New Policies' reforms.
--- fact 4
Historians debate her legacy; older accounts portray her as a reactionary, and recent work stresses her political skill and the limits she faced.
--- context
Cixi ruled the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]] through the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]] aftermath and the [[event/boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]]. Compare [[person/wu-zetian|Wu Zetian]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that, as with earlier women rulers, the record on Cixi was written largely by her opponents. See [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]].

=== person/claude-monet
title: Claude Monet
type: person
date: 1840 – 1926
themes: art
src: a562288853e0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Claude Monet (14 November 1840 to 5 December 1926) was a French painter, the leader and purest practitioner of Impressionism. His Impression, Sunrise gave the movement its name, and he spent his life painting the changing effects of light.
--- fact 1
Monet was born in Paris and grew up in Le Havre, where as a teenager he sold caricatures. Eugène Boudin persuaded him to paint outdoors. In Paris he met Camille Pissarro and befriended Renoir, Sisley and Bazille.
--- fact 2
The 1860s and 1870s were years of poverty. During the Franco-Prussian War he took refuge in London, where he saw Turner's paintings and met the dealer Paul Durand-Ruel.
--- fact 3
In 1874 he and his friends held an exhibition independent of the official Salon. Critics mocked them, and the name "Impressionist" stuck.
--- fact 4
In 1883 his family settled at Giverny in Normandy, where he created a flower garden and a water garden with a Japanese bridge, which became his main subject.
--- fact 5
He painted the same subject repeatedly in changing conditions: haystacks, poplars and Rouen Cathedral. Key works include Impression, Sunrise (1872), Stacks of Wheat (1890 to 1891) and the Water Lilies.
--- fact 6
In his last decade he fought cataracts and worked on the giant Grandes Décorations, which he gave to the French state as a monument to peace after the First World War. He died at Giverny.
--- fact 7
The Abstract Expressionists rediscovered his late water lilies in the 1950s. His house and gardens opened to the public in 1980, and in 2019 one of his Haystacks sold for $110.7 million.
--- context
Monet worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], learned from [[person/j-m-w-turner|J. M. W. Turner]] and from Japanese prints by [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]] and [[person/utagawa-hiroshige|Utagawa Hiroshige]], and gave his last great work to France after [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter whose subject was the act of seeing, and whose method, the same motif in changing light, shows that one scene is many scenes. A movement was named by critics who meant to mock it, and the name became a badge, so insult and category overlapped. His paintings later became commodities of very high price, $110.7 million for a Haystacks in 2019, a status-signal the critics of 1874 could not have predicted.
--- question 1
How did his cataracts change the colour of his late paintings?

--- summary
Cleopatra VII was the last active ruler of the Ptolemaic Kingdom of Egypt, who allied with Julius Caesar and Mark Antony and died in 30 BCE after their defeat by Octavian.
--- fact 1
She belonged to the Ptolemaic dynasty, founded by a Macedonian general of Alexander the Great.

Ancient sources say she spoke several languages and, unlike most of her Ptolemaic predecessors, learned Egyptian.
--- fact 3
She had a son with Julius Caesar and children with Mark Antony.
--- fact 4
Octavian defeated Antony and Cleopatra at the Battle of Actium in 31 BCE.
--- fact 5
After her death in 30 BCE, Egypt became a Roman province.
--- context
Cleopatra was a Greek-speaking ruler of the land of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]], descended from the generals of [[person/alexander-the-great|Alexander the Great]]. Her defeat by [[person/augustus|Augustus]] ended the last Hellenistic kingdom. Cleopatra VII sits in the [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].

=== person/confucius
title: Confucius
type: person
date: c. 551 – 479 BCE

src: 029acc93bdc1

observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Confucius (Kong Fuzi, 551 to 479 BCE) was a Chinese teacher and thinker whose ideas about ethics, family, and government shaped East Asian societies for over two thousand years.
--- fact 1
His sayings were collected by disciples in the Analects.
--- fact 2
Central ideas include ren (humaneness), li (ritual propriety), and filial piety.
--- fact 3
He was not widely influential during his lifetime; his ideas rose to prominence under the Han dynasty.
--- fact 4
Confucian learning later became the basis of the imperial civil service examinations.
--- context
Confucius is one of the figures linked with the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]]. See [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and [[region/east-asia|East Asia]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a teacher without political power supplied the ethical framework of a large state. The kin-lattice, projected outward, became a model for government.

How much of the Analects reflects Confucius himself?
