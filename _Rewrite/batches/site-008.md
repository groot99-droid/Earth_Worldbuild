=== place/persepolis
title: Persepolis
type: place
date: c. 518 BCE
themes: art, war
src: 256c43ddc2d8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Persepolis was a ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire in southwestern Iran, begun around 518 BCE under Darius I and burned by Alexander the Great's army in 330 BCE.
--- fact 1
Construction began under Darius I around 518 BCE and continued under his successors.
--- fact 2
The Apadana reliefs show delegations from across the empire bringing gifts.
--- fact 3
Alexander's army burned the palace in 330 BCE; ancient sources give conflicting motives, including revenge and drunken impulse.
--- fact 4
Thousands of Elamite tablets, the Persepolis Fortification Tablets, record rations and administration.
--- fact 5
It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1979.
--- context
Persepolis embodies the imperial ideology of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] founded by [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], and its destruction was part of [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. Persepolis sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the reliefs show subject peoples arriving with tribute in orderly procession, an image of empire as harmonious exchange. Every empire tells its own story in stone, and the stone lasts longer than the story's rivals.
--- question 1
Was the burning of Persepolis deliberate policy or an accident?

=== place/petra
title: Petra
type: place
date: c. 312 BCE
themes: trade, art
src: 6ad7558c7600
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Petra is a rock-cut city in the mountains of southern Jordan that served as the capital of the Nabataean kingdom from about the 4th century BCE until Rome annexed the kingdom in 106 CE.
--- fact 1
Petra lies in a basin among sandstone mountains, entered through a gorge about 1.2 kilometers long known as the Siq.
--- fact 2
Greek writers describe an expedition against the Nabataeans in 312 BCE and their rock refuge, which is usually identified with Petra.
--- fact 3
Petra's carved facades, including al-Khazneh ("the Treasury"), date mostly from about the 1st century BCE to the 1st century CE. Most facades are thought to be tombs, though the use of some, including the Treasury, is debated.
--- fact 4
Petra was supplied by dams, channels, ceramic pipes and cisterns in a desert climate, and its wealth is generally attributed to the caravan trade in incense and other goods. Estimates of its peak population are uncertain, with commonly cited figures of about 20,000 to 30,000.
--- fact 5
After Roman annexation in 106 CE, Petra became part of the province of Arabia. An earthquake in 363 CE destroyed many buildings and the city declined afterward; the Swiss traveler Johann Ludwig Burckhardt brought it to European attention in 1812.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed Petra in 1985.
--- context
Petra was the capital of the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]] and a node in the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]] before its annexation by the [[culture/romans|Romans]]; its sister site is [[place/hegra|Hegra]] in Saudi Arabia. The site lies in Jordan, at the northern edge of the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]]. Petra sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Petra's most visible investment, the carved facades, is largely funerary, so a trading people's wealth became permanent chiefly as grief-rites and status-signals for the dead, while the caravans left little trace. A city that lived on stored water and control of a passage, not on farmland, shows how control of a route can stand in for control of land. The reading depends on the tomb attribution, which is well supported for many facades but debated for others such as the Treasury.
--- question 1
What were the carved buildings of Petra used for, and how many were tombs, banquet halls or temples?
--- question 2
What did Petra depend on after the caravan routes shifted?

=== place/pompeii
title: Pompeii
type: place
date: c. 700 BCE – 79 CE
themes: earth-systems, trade, art
src: 389f674cb929
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Pompeii was a Roman town on the Bay of Naples in Italy, founded in about the 7th century BCE, that was buried under volcanic ash and pumice when Mount Vesuvius erupted in 79 CE.
--- fact 1
Pompeii was founded in about the 7th century BCE by the Oscans, came under Greek and Etruscan influence, and became a Roman colony in 80 BCE.
--- fact 2
An earthquake in 62 or 63 CE damaged the town, and repairs were still under way when Vesuvius erupted in 79 CE.
--- fact 3
Pompeii's population is estimated at about 11,000, with estimates ranging from about 10,000 to 20,000.
--- fact 4
The eruption buried Pompeii under pumice and ash, and pyroclastic surges killed people who had stayed; about 1,100 bodies have been found in the excavated town, and many inhabitants are thought to have fled.
--- fact 5
The traditional date of the eruption is 24 August, but a charcoal inscription found in 2018 suggests a later date, possibly in October.
--- fact 6
Excavations began in 1748, and in 1863 Giuseppe Fiorelli began pouring plaster into voids left by decayed bodies to produce casts of victims; graffiti, shop signs and election notices survive on the walls.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the archaeological areas of Pompeii, Herculaneum and Torre Annunziata in 1997.
--- context
Pompeii was a town of the [[culture/romans|Romans]] and part of the wider world governed from [[place/rome|Rome]]. It sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] (its Oscan foundation) and the region [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Pompeii is a case where a natural catastrophe left an unusually complete record of ordinary life, shop signs, graffiti, bakeries and casts, an unintended act of memory-outsourcing that no one planned. The Observer records the deaths of the people found there as suffering, and notes that the casts of victims raise the question of how such remains should be shown. The record is biased toward one town on one day and toward what was left behind, and the reading would weaken if new counts show that the number of people who died was much smaller or larger than the current figures suggest.
--- question 1
How many people died at Pompeii, and how many escaped?
--- question 2
On what date did Vesuvius erupt in 79 CE?

=== place/port-royal
title: Port Royal
type: place
date: 1655 – 1692
themes: trade, war, earth-systems
src: a924e1324694
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Port Royal was an English port town at the tip of the Palisadoes spit at the entrance to Kingston Harbour, Jamaica, which grew after 1655 as a base for privateers and traders and lost about two-thirds of its area to an earthquake on 7 June 1692.
--- fact 1
England took Jamaica from Spain in 1655, and Port Royal was fortified from 1656; English governors issued commissions to privateers, who used the port as a base to attack Spanish ships and towns.
--- fact 2
Henry Morgan, a privateer who later became lieutenant governor of Jamaica, led raids from the colony, including the sack of Panama in 1671.
--- fact 3
Port Royal was a trading port for the island's goods and a landing and sale point for enslaved Africans; it had about 6,500 residents in 1692.
--- fact 4
On 7 June 1692 an earthquake, and the liquefaction of the sandy ground beneath the town, sank about two-thirds of Port Royal into the harbor. About 2,000 people are estimated to have died in the quake and its immediate aftermath, and several thousand more died of injury and disease in the following weeks.
--- fact 5
Some contemporary accounts read the earthquake as divine judgment.
--- fact 6
Survivors founded Kingston across the harbor, a fire in 1703 destroyed much of what remained of Port Royal, and the Royal Navy used the town as a base into the early 20th century.
--- fact 7
Underwater archaeology from the 1950s, and again from the 1980s, has recovered buildings and household objects from the submerged area.
--- context
Port Royal grew as the English Caribbean took shape after the Spanish colony at [[place/santo-domingo|Santo Domingo]] had already set the pattern of European settlement, and its trade touched the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]]. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a port whose wealth came from state-licensed raiding and from trade that included the sale of enslaved people, and whose sudden loss some contemporaries explained through their own meaning-engine as divine judgment. The sinking also froze a working town's daily life in place, an unusual case in which a disaster acts as memory-outsourcing for people who left few documents. The popular image of Port Royal as uniquely lawless is a later story-glue, and how far the record supports it is debated. A historian could challenge the first sentence by showing that privateering brought the town less wealth than ordinary trade did.
--- question 1
How much of Port Royal's wealth came from privateering, from ordinary trade and from the sale of enslaved people?
--- question 2
How much of the submerged town remains to be recorded?

Potosi turned the conquered [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] into a source of silver for the world economy. Its silver crossed the Pacific through the [[event/manila-galleon-trade|Manila Galleon Trade]], and its labor system followed the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-inca-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]]. Potosi sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a labor system inherited from the conquered was repurposed to extract wealth for the conquerors. A mountain that fed global trade became a symbol of how far an exchange-web could stretch and how much suffering could sit at its origin.

=== place/rapa-nui
title: Rapa Nui
type: place
date: c. 1200
themes: art, earth-systems
src: 736df7468a2f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rapa Nui (Easter Island) is a remote Pacific island settled by Polynesians around 1200 CE, famous for nearly a thousand monumental stone statues called moai.
--- fact 1
The island lies in the southeastern Pacific, thousands of kilometers from the nearest large land.
--- fact 2
Estimates for the arrival of the Polynesian settlers range from as early as about 300 CE, based on contested early radiocarbon dates, to about 1200 CE, the date favored by many recent studies.
--- fact 3
About 400 moai (397 in one count) lie in the quarry of Rano Raraku in various stages of completion, and about 95 percent of the island's monolithic statues were carved from its volcanic tuff. Published counts of the total number of moai differ.
--- fact 4
The popular story of self-inflicted ecological collapse is contested. Researchers point to introduced rats, which may have been the main cause of deforestation, and to disease and slave raids. In December 1862 Peruvian slavers captured about 1,000 islanders, and reports put the total taken in 1862 to 1864 at more than 1,400. Most of the captives died in Peru, and a smallpox epidemic that followed the return of a few survivors in 1863 reportedly killed about a thousand more. These figures come from popular summaries and vary between accounts.
--- fact 5
Rapa Nui National Park was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1995.
--- context
Rapa Nui was the far eastern edge of the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] and of the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]]' voyaging world. Rapa Nui sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the island is often told as a parable of self-destruction, but the record suggests that outside pressures did much of the damage. Stories that fit a moral are chosen more readily than stories that fit the evidence.
--- question 1
How large was the population at its peak, and what caused its decline?

=== place/robben-island
title: Robben Island
type: place
date: 1658 – 1996
themes: war, kinship
src: a2f5ccf8744f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Robben Island is a small island in Table Bay, about 7 kilometers off Cape Town, South Africa, used from the 17th century to 1996 as a place of banishment, a leprosy colony and a prison, and best known for the political prisoners held there under apartheid, including Nelson Mandela.
--- fact 1
Robben Island lies in Table Bay about 7 kilometers from Cape Town and covers about 5 square kilometers; its name comes from the Dutch word for seals.
--- fact 2
The Dutch East India Company began using Robben Island as a place of banishment in the 1650s; among those sent there was the Khoikhoi interpreter Autshumato, in 1658.
--- fact 3
A leprosy colony operated on the island from 1846 to 1931, and it served as a military base in the Second World War.
--- fact 4
From 1961 to 1996 Robben Island was a maximum-security prison where the apartheid state held political prisoners, including Nelson Mandela (imprisoned on the island for 18 of his 27 years, from 1964), Robert Sobukwe, Walter Sisulu and Govan Mbeki.
--- fact 5
Prisoners worked in a limestone quarry, and the glare damaged Mandela's eyes; prisoners organized study and debate among themselves, and the prison was nicknamed a "university".
--- fact 6
The last political prisoners were released in 1991 and the prison closed in 1996.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Robben Island in 1999, and former prisoners lead tours of the island today.
--- context
Robben Island was first used as a place of banishment by the [[event/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]], and its years as an apartheid prison belong to the wider struggle over minority rule in the period of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]]. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Robben Island shows water used as a wall: for more than three centuries authorities sent people they wanted removed from society, exiles, patients and dissidents, to a place where isolation was cheap to enforce. The same isolation concentrated a generation of leaders who studied and argued together, so a prison also worked as a school and an exchange-web of ideas, an outcome its designers did not intend, and the Observer records the years of imprisonment and forced labor as suffering. The reading would weaken if accounts of prisoners' study and organization describe only a small, unrepresentative part of the prison population.
--- question 1
How did prisoners on Robben Island organize themselves, and how far did their organization reach beyond the island?
--- question 2
How should the island present its earlier uses, such as the leprosy colony and the exile of Khoikhoi leaders?

=== place/rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela
title: Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela
type: place
date: c. 1200
themes: religion, art, technology
src: 4b0d57e06afd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela are eleven churches carved down into volcanic rock in the highlands of northern Ethiopia, traditionally attributed to King Lalibela of the Zagwe dynasty in the early 13th century and still in use for worship.
--- fact 1
Lalibela lies at about 2,500 meters in the Amhara Region of Ethiopia and has eleven monolithic churches, in two main groups and one separate cross-shaped church, Bete Giyorgis.
--- fact 2
The churches are traditionally attributed to King Lalibela, who reigned around the late 12th and early 13th centuries; some scholars propose that some churches are older.
--- fact 3
Builders first cut trenches into the rock and then carved the exterior and interior of each church from the block left standing, joining the churches by trenches and tunnels and cutting drainage channels.
--- fact 4
Lalibela is presented in tradition as a "New Jerusalem", with features named after places in the Holy Land, such as a stream called the Jordan; the link with the loss of Jerusalem to Saladin in 1187 is traditional and is not documented in contemporary sources.
--- fact 5
The churches remain in active use, and large numbers of pilgrims come at major festivals.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela in 1978.
--- context
The Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela were built under the [[culture/zagwe-dynasty|Zagwe Dynasty]] in a region shaped earlier by the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] and its town [[place/aksum|Aksum]], and the "New Jerusalem" tradition relates them to the time of the [[event/crusades|Crusades]]. They sit in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Lalibela reverses the usual order of building: workers removed rock and did not add it, cutting free-standing churches down into the ground, a labor-heavy technique that also makes the buildings hard to destroy. The "New Jerusalem" tradition shows a place made to stand in for a distant sacred site when access to the original was in doubt, a meaning-engine adjusting to political change. Because the link to 1187 is not documented in contemporary sources, the reading would weaken if the churches prove to have been planned around a different rationale.
--- question 1
Which of the churches were built in the reign of Lalibela, and which are earlier or later?
--- question 2
How many workers were involved, and how long did the carving take?

=== place/santo-domingo
title: Santo Domingo
type: place
date: 1496
themes: trade, war, religion
src: 1f36f67ec2a0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Santo Domingo is the capital of the Dominican Republic, on the south coast of Hispaniola, founded by Spain in 1496 and commonly described as the oldest continuously inhabited European-founded city in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Santo Domingo was founded in 1496 by Bartholomew Columbus on the east bank of the Ozama River, and governor Nicolás de Ovando moved it to the west bank in 1502 after storm damage.
--- fact 2
Santo Domingo became the seat of Spanish administration in the Caribbean, with the first Real Audiencia in the Americas (1511), a cathedral begun in the 1510s and completed in 1540, and a university founded in 1538.
--- fact 3
Expeditions to Puerto Rico, Cuba and the mainland were organized from Hispaniola under this colonial administration.
--- fact 4
The Taíno population of Hispaniola fell sharply under forced labor, violence and disease, including a smallpox epidemic beginning in 1518. Estimates of the pre-contact population range from tens of thousands to over a million, and a Spanish count in 1514 recorded about 26,000 Taíno on the island.
--- fact 5
Enslaved Africans were brought to Hispaniola from the early 1500s; in December 1521 enslaved Africans rebelled on a sugar estate near the city, an uprising commonly described as the first of its kind in the Americas.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Colonial City of Santo Domingo in 1990.
--- context
Santo Domingo was the base for the first phase of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] in the Caribbean, which brought devastation to the [[culture/taino|Taino]] and drew the island into the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]]. Its administrators and settlers later took part in the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]] and other mainland ventures. The city stands on [[place/hispaniola|Hispaniola]], in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that within about four decades Spain had placed a high court, a cathedral and a university on a shore where none had stood, extending a claim-line through stone institutions while the people already on the island were drawn into a forced-labor system. The city marks a point where two exchange-webs, one from Europe and one from the Americas, began moving people, crops and pathogens in both directions. The rebellion of 1521 shows that those held in the system resisted it from early on. Because the population figures rest on Spanish colonial counts, the size of the Taíno loss is uncertain even though its direction is not.
--- question 1
How large was the pre-contact population of Hispaniola, and how much of its decline came from disease compared with forced labor and violence?
--- question 2
How did Santo Domingo's role change as Spain's attention shifted to the mainland?

=== place/sarai
title: Sarai
type: place
date: c. 1250 – 1395
themes: trade, war, religion
src: b46ae451cc74
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sarai was the capital of the Golden Horde, the western part of the Mongol Empire, on the lower Volga River in present-day southern Russia, founded around 1250 by Batu Khan and sacked by Timur in 1395.
--- fact 1
Batu Khan, a grandson of Chinggis Khan, founded Sarai (Persian for "palace") about 1250 on the lower Volga, near Selitrennoye in present-day Astrakhan Oblast; a second city, Sarai Berke, was built upstream, near present-day Volgograd, and the chronology of the two is debated.
--- fact 2
Rus' princes traveled to Sarai to pay tribute and receive a yarlyk (charter) confirming their rule, and an Orthodox bishopric of Sarai was established in 1261.
--- fact 3
Öz Beg Khan (reigned 1313 to 1341) adopted Islam as the Horde's state religion.
--- fact 4
The traveler Ibn Battuta visited in the 1330s and described a large city with markets and people of many nations.
--- fact 5
Plague reached the Black Sea port of Caffa in 1346 during a siege by Golden Horde forces and spread from there through Mediterranean ports; the Horde's role in its spread is debated.
--- fact 6
Timur sacked Sarai in 1395 in his war with Khan Tokhtamysh, and the city did not recover its former status.
--- context
Sarai was the capital of the Golden Horde, a successor state of the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] created by the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], and it was sacked by forces of Timur, founder of the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]]. It lay on trade routes linked to the spread of the [[event/black-death|Black Death]]. Sarai sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Sarai was a capital whose main function was to collect and pass on tribute, and where rulers of distant cities came to be confirmed in office by a document that could be withdrawn, a claim-line maintained by paperwork as much as by garrisons. A steppe empire that moved between camps also built permanent cities with mosques and workshops, and Sarai housed several religions under one authority. The period is remembered differently in Russian, Tatar and Central Asian national histories, so the same evidence supports several stories. Historians who stress the Horde's role as a trading partner and not an overlord would challenge the first sentence.
--- question 1
How large was Sarai, and how did its population change over time?
--- question 2
Which of the two Sarais served as the capital at different times?

observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Stonehenge is a monument of standing stones on Salisbury Plain in southern England, begun as a circular earthwork around 3000 BCE and rebuilt in stone over the next several centuries.
--- fact 1
The first phase was a circular ditch and bank, around 3000 BCE.
--- fact 2
The large sarsen stones, of which the biggest weigh around 25 tons, were sourced from around 25 kilometers away, according to a 2020 study identifying West Woods as the source.
--- fact 3
The smaller bluestones were brought from the Preseli Hills in Wales, more than 200 kilometers away.
--- fact 4
A 2024 study reported that the Altar Stone came from northern Scotland, roughly 750 kilometers away.
--- fact 5
The site was used for burials, with cremated remains from early in its history, and its axis aligns with the solstice sunrise and sunset.
--- context
Stonehenge shows that farming communities in Britain could organize large collective labor and long-distance transport before metal tools. It stands alongside monuments such as [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]] as an example of monumental building in the Neolithic and Bronze Age. Stonehenge sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that people moved multi-ton stones over long distances and aligned them to the sun, without a written record of why. Monuments outlast the meanings that produced them, and the meaning is then rebuilt by each generation of visitors.
--- question 1
What was the site used for, and by whom?
--- question 2
How were the largest stones moved?

=== place/tikal
title: Tikal
type: place
date: c. 1000 BCE – 950 CE
themes: religion, war, language
src: 4c59b22dbbc4
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tikal is a large Maya city in the rainforest of northern Guatemala, with traces of farming from about 1000 BCE, a dominant Classic-period kingdom whose rulers left inscriptions from the 3rd to the 9th century CE, and a city almost deserted after about 950.
--- fact 1
Tikal, called Yax Mutal in inscriptions and the seat of a kingdom called Mutul, lies in the Petén region of Guatemala; traces of farming date to about 1000 BCE, and monumental building began about 400 to 300 BCE.
--- fact 2
Inscriptions record that in January 378 a warlord named Siyaj K'ak' arrived from the west and Tikal's king died the same day; a new king linked to Teotihuacan was then installed, and scholars debate whether the event was a conquest or an alliance.
--- fact 3
In 562 Tikal was defeated by Calakmul and Caracol and its king was apparently captured and sacrificed; in 695 Jasaw Chan K'awiil I defeated Calakmul, and Tikal revived.
--- fact 4
Estimates of Tikal's peak population range from about 10,000 to 90,000 for the city, with far higher numbers proposed for its surrounding region, and archaeologists debate them.
--- fact 5
The last dated monument was set up in 869, and after about 950 Tikal was almost deserted, during the wider Classic Maya collapse.
--- fact 6
Modesto Méndez and Ambrosio Tut visited in 1848, Alfred Maudslay documented the site in 1881 to 1882, and the University of Pennsylvania excavated it from 1956 to 1970; Guatemala made it a national park in 1955 and UNESCO inscribed it in 1979.
--- context
Tikal was one of the largest kingdoms of the [[culture/maya|Maya]] and had ties to [[place/teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] in central Mexico; its emptying is part of the [[event/classic-maya-collapse|Classic Maya Collapse]]. Tikal sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] by its earliest settlement, with its peak in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], in the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Tikal's kings set their wars, accessions and rites in stone in a mark-code, so the city's political history is read largely through the claims of its rulers, which favor legitimacy. Temples raised over royal tombs tied the ruler's body to the standing of the city, a status-signal built to be seen across the forest, and the emptying of the city shows that a monument-building center can be left within a few generations. The reading would weaken if epigraphic and archaeological work shows that non-royal households, not kings, drove most decisions about settlement and abandonment.
--- question 1
How large was Tikal's population at its peak, and how was it fed and supplied with water?
--- question 2
Was the event of 378 a conquest by Teotihuacan or an alliance between elites?

=== place/troy
title: Troy
type: place
date: c. 3000 BCE
themes: war, art, language
src: 6a0b20fa9362
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Troy is an archaeological site at Hisarlık in northwestern Turkey, occupied from about 3000 BCE into the Byzantine era in nine main layers, and identified by many scholars as the city of Homer's Iliad.
--- fact 1
Troy stands at Hisarlık, near the Dardanelles in Çanakkale Province, Turkey; excavators distinguish nine main settlement layers, Troy I to Troy IX, beginning around 3000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Heinrich Schliemann began excavations at Troy in 1870, and later excavators included Wilhelm Dörpfeld, Carl Blegen and Manfred Korfmann.
--- fact 3
Schliemann's "Treasure of Priam" came from Troy II, about 2500 BCE, far older than any Trojan War; he took it out of the Ottoman Empire, it disappeared from Berlin in 1945 and resurfaced in Moscow in the 1990s.
--- fact 4
Troy VI (about 1700 to 1300 BCE) was a large fortified city, and Troy VIIa was destroyed by fire around 1180 BCE and is often proposed as the city of the Trojan War, if the war has a historical basis.
--- fact 5
Hittite texts mention a land called Wilusa in western Anatolia, whose name is often linked to Ilios, a Greek name for Troy; the identification is debated.
--- fact 6
Whether a Trojan War took place is unknown; the Iliad was composed in Greek around the 8th century BCE, centuries after the events it describes.
--- fact 7
Greeks and Romans later built the city of Ilion at the site, and UNESCO inscribed Troy in 1998.
--- context
Troy had the [[culture/hittites|Hittites]] and the [[culture/mycenaean-greeks|Mycenaean Greeks]] as neighbors and was remembered by the later [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]]; Alexander visited it in 334 BCE during [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. The destruction of Troy VIIa falls close in time to the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Troy is a place where a story led to a spade: Schliemann set out to find the city of the Iliad and did find a city, though not necessarily the city of the poem, which shows how a narrative can direct an excavation and then be mistaken for its evidence. The poem's story-glue gave the site a long life as a destination, from Alexander to modern tourists. The reading would weaken if the identification of Wilusa with Troy is refuted, or if Troy VIIa proves unrelated to any historical conflict.
--- question 1
Did a war take place at Troy, and if so, in which layer?
--- question 2
How large was the population of Troy VI?

--- summary
The Upano Valley settlements are a network of ancient urban centers in the Ecuadorian Amazon, documented by lidar and reported in 2024, occupied from about 500 BCE for many centuries.
--- fact 1
The network spans roughly 300 square kilometers (about 115 square miles) and includes some 15 urban centers.
--- fact 2
More than 6,000 rectangular earthen platforms, probably house and civic foundations, are linked by roads and drainage canals.

The valley probably held at least 10,000 farmers, and possibly many more, about 2,000 years ago.

=== place/ur
title: Ur
type: place
date: c. 3800 – 500 BCE
themes: religion, trade, language
src: ee3faf6bb226
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ur was a Sumerian city in southern Iraq, occupied from about 3800 BCE and abandoned around 500 BCE, known for its Great Ziggurat, its royal cemetery, and its role as the capital of the Third Dynasty of Ur about 2100 BCE.
--- fact 1
Ur lies at Tell el-Muqayyar in Dhi Qar Governorate, southern Iraq, near the Euphrates; it was occupied by the late Ubaid period and grew into a city in the 3rd millennium BCE.
--- fact 2
Leonard Woolley excavated Ur from 1922 to 1934 and found a royal cemetery dated to about 2600 to 2500 BCE, with graves holding gold and lapis lazuli jewelry, lyres and, in some, the bodies of many attendants placed with the dead; how the attendants died is debated.
--- fact 3
Enheduanna, a daughter of Sargon of Akkad and high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur about 2300 BCE, is the earliest named author known.
--- fact 4
Ur-Nammu (reigned about 2112 to 2095 BCE) founded the Third Dynasty of Ur, built the Great Ziggurat for Nanna, and is credited with the earliest surviving law code.
--- fact 5
Ur fell to Elamite forces about 2004 BCE and later became a smaller city; it was abandoned around 500 BCE, and proposed causes include a shift in the Euphrates and drier conditions.
--- fact 6
The biblical "Ur of the Chaldees", birthplace of Abraham, is often identified with Ur, but the identification is debated.
--- fact 7
UNESCO included Ur in the "Ahwar of Southern Iraq" listing in 2016.
--- context
Ur was a city of the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] alongside [[place/uruk|Uruk]], and it belongs to the story of [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]] and the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] in southern Mesopotamia. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the royal cemetery of Ur preserves an early case of attendants buried with a ruler, a grief-rite and status-signal in one; whether they died willingly, under coercion or in a ritual understood as honor cannot be settled from the bones, and the Observer records the possibility of coerced death as suffering. A city whose temple priestess is the first named author and whose ruler issued an early law code suggests an early state that used mark-code to bind rule to the gods. The reading would weaken if new studies show that the burials differ from those of other early Mesopotamian cities in ways that make Ur unrepresentative.
--- question 1
How did the attendants in the Royal Cemetery die, and did they take part willingly?
--- question 2
Why was Ur abandoned around 500 BCE?

=== place/valley-of-the-kings
title: Valley of the Kings
type: place
date: c. 1500 – 1070 BCE
themes: religion, art, technology
src: e552b70e7c49
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Valley of the Kings is a valley on the west bank of the Nile opposite Thebes, in Egypt, where pharaohs of the New Kingdom were buried in rock-cut tombs from about 1500 to 1070 BCE.
--- fact 1
The Valley of the Kings served for royal burials of the 18th to 20th Dynasties, from about 1500 BCE until about 1070 BCE, and holds more than 60 tombs cut into limestone hills.
--- fact 2
The rulers chose hidden rock-cut tombs in place of the pyramids of earlier periods, such as those at Giza.
--- fact 3
Most tombs were robbed in antiquity; records of trials of tomb robbers survive from the 20th Dynasty, and priests later moved royal mummies to hidden caches.
--- fact 4
The workmen's village of Deir el-Medina yielded ration accounts and an account of a strike over unpaid rations in about 1157 BCE, often described as one of the earliest recorded labor strikes.
--- fact 5
Howard Carter found the tomb of Tutankhamun (KV62) in November 1922, largely intact and holding thousands of objects.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis, including the Valley of the Kings, in 1979.
--- context
The Valley of the Kings served the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] of the New Kingdom, whose main temple was [[place/karnak|Karnak]], and it replaced the pyramid tradition of the [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the move from monumental pyramids to hidden rock-cut tombs changed how rulers guarded their grief-rite investment: concealment replaced display, yet the tombs were still robbed, which suggests that no design made a store of wealth safe from the people who knew where it was. The strike at Deir el-Medina shows that the workforce building a ruler's afterlife was paid in rations and could act collectively when payment failed. The reading would weaken if the shift from pyramids proves to have been driven mainly by changes in belief and not by fear of robbery.
--- question 1
Why did New Kingdom rulers abandon pyramids for hidden tombs?
--- question 2
How many people worked on the royal tombs, and how were they organized?
