=== species/dunkleosteus
title: Dunkleosteus
type: species
date: 372 – 360 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 8dad795fc1c3
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Dunkleosteus was a large armored placoderm fish of the Late Devonian with bony blades in place of teeth. Its best-known species, D. terrelli, comes from the Cleveland Shale of Ohio; its size has been revised downward and its bite force is under review.
--- fact 1
The genus is reported from the Late Devonian, about 382 to 359 million years ago, but D. terrelli is best documented from about 372 to 360 million years ago, chiefly in the Cleveland Shale of Ohio. Ten species have been named, and about four are widely accepted.
--- fact 2
Older reconstructions gave lengths of 6 to 10 m. A 2023 study using the eye socket and cheek plate estimated about 3.4 m for typical adults and about 4.1 m for the largest. It has not been independently replicated.
--- fact 3
A 2007 study modeled the skull as a linked lever system and estimated a bite force of about 4,400 newtons at the tip and 5,400 at the blade edge, for an individual then thought to be 6 m long. A 2025 study argues that these figures need re-examination.
--- fact 4
The name was coined in 1956 by Jean-Pierre Lehman to honor David Dunkle. Placoderms died out at the end-Devonian Hangenberg event, about 359 million years ago.
--- context
Dunkleosteus lived in the [[era/devonian-period|Devonian Period]], the Age of Fishes, alongside lobe-finned relatives of [[species/tiktaalik|Tiktaalik]] during the [[event/fish-to-tetrapod-transition|Fish-to-Tetrapod Transition]]. Placoderms declined in the [[event/late-devonian-extinction|Late Devonian Extinction]]. Compare the arthropod predators, [[species/eurypterids|Eurypterids]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the animal long shown as the great predator of its age shrank by about half on re-measurement, the ordinary condition of deep-time knowledge, in which a confident number is a reading of a few skulls. A whole class of armored fishes left no descendants while a lobe-finned relative went on to land, and nothing in the record marked which would. The reading would weaken if independent studies reproduced the older, larger sizes.
--- question 1
How large did Dunkleosteus grow, and how hard could it bite?
--- question 2
Which of the named species are valid?

=== species/eurypterids
title: Eurypterids
type: species
date: 467 – 252 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: ccc16570e5eb
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Eurypterids, or sea scorpions, were predatory arthropods that lived from the Ordovician to the Permian, about 467 to 252 million years ago. They were relatives of spiders and horseshoe crabs, not true scorpions, and included one of the largest arthropods known, about 2.5 m in body length.
--- fact 1
The oldest well-described eurypterid, Pentecopterus from Iowa, is about 467 to 458 million years old. Moroccan fragments reported in 2025 may be about 479 million years old but are tentatively assigned.
--- fact 2
All eurypterids are chelicerates, the group of spiders, scorpions, and horseshoe crabs. A 2013 analysis placed them closer to arachnids than to horseshoe crabs, which remains debated.
--- fact 3
The largest, Jaekelopterus rhenaniae of the Early Devonian of Germany, was estimated in 2007 from a pincer fragment to have a pincer about 46 cm long and a body about 2.5 m long, without the extended pincers. Another study argued for a smaller size.
--- fact 4
Only the earliest eurypterids were strictly marine; many later forms lived in brackish or fresh water. A 6-m trackway in Carboniferous sandstone in Scotland has been attributed to a land-walking Hibbertopterus, an inference.
--- fact 5
After the Late Devonian extinction only three families reached the Carboniferous and Permian. The last records are Permian, about 254 to 252 million years old at the latest.
--- context
Eurypterids arose during the [[event/great-ordovician-biodiversification|Great Ordovician Biodiversification]] beside [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]], flourished in the [[era/silurian-period|Silurian Period]] and [[era/devonian-period|Devonian Period]], declined in the [[event/late-devonian-extinction|Late Devonian Extinction]], and were gone by the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]]. Compare the armored fish [[species/dunkleosteus|Dunkleosteus]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one of the largest arthropods ever to live belonged to a group now known chiefly to specialists, and that its size is scaled from a fragment of claw. A lineage that lasted about 215 million years and left no descendants shows that persistence does not guarantee survival. The reading would weaken if eurypterids proved to have been displaced by competitors, not by environmental change.
--- question 1
When did the last eurypterids die out?
--- question 2
How large did Jaekelopterus grow?

=== species/goat
title: Goat
type: species
date: c. 8000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 0bcb1baf09b6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic goat was bred from the wild bezoar ibex in southwestern Asia around 10,000 years ago, one of the first animals kept by farming communities, and it remains valued today for milk, meat and fiber, especially on marginal land unsuited to other livestock.
--- fact 1
Domestic goats descend from the bezoar ibex, a wild goat still found in the Zagros Mountains of Iran and formerly widespread in Anatolia, and domestication is dated to about 10,000 years ago in Southwest Asia and Eastern Europe.
--- fact 2
The earliest clear archaeological evidence for goat domestication comes from Ganj Dareh in Iran, with further early sites at Jericho, Choga Mami, Djeitun and Çayönü dating domestication to between about 8,000 and 9,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Neolithic herders used goats for milk and meat and also made use of their dung as fuel and their bones, hair and sinew for tools, building materials and clothing.
--- fact 4
Goats remain economically important today for dairy, providing about 2 percent of the world's total annual milk supply, and for fiber such as cashmere and mohair, valued in part for their hardiness on land too marginal for cattle or sheep.
--- fact 5
More than 1.1 billion goats were living worldwide as of 2022, of which about 150 million were in India, the largest single-country population; India was also the largest goat-milk producer that year, followed by Bangladesh and South Sudan.
--- context
Goats were among the earliest animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]], a pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent alongside [[species/sheep|Sheep]], domesticated in a similar place and period and often herded together. Goats sit in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: that the goat's continuing importance on land too poor for cattle or sheep suggests its domestication gave early farming communities a way to draw food and materials from marginal ground that would otherwise have supported no livestock at all, extending the reach of the Settling into harsher terrain. That goats and sheep were domesticated in the same general region and period, and are still often herded together, points to an early kin-lattice of pastoral practice that treated the two species as complementary rather than as competitors for the same land. The reading would weaken if goat and sheep domestication are shown to have occurred at meaningfully different times or in different communities rather than in close succession.
--- question 1
How closely linked were the domestication of goats and sheep, and did one community likely tame both, or did the practices spread separately?
--- question 2
How has goat herding on marginal land changed as climate change alters which regions count as marginal?

=== species/hallucigenia
title: Hallucigenia
type: species
date: 510 – 505 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 075d071ae3f5
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hallucigenia is a small, worm-like Cambrian animal with paired back spines and clawed legs, known from the Burgess Shale of Canada and from China, about 510 to 505 million years ago. It is known for being reconstructed upside down in 1977 and for the 2015 discovery of its head.
--- fact 1
Three species are named: H. sparsa from the Burgess Shale of British Columbia, H. fortis from Chengjiang and H. hongmeia from Guanshan, both in Yunnan, China. The Burgess Shale is about 505 million years old by fossil zones; Chengjiang is at most 518.
--- fact 2
H. sparsa is about 3 cm long, though published maxima reach 5.5 cm, and it is rare, about 0.19 percent of specimens counted in the Walcott Quarry.
--- fact 3
In 1977 Simon Conway Morris reconstructed it walking on stilt-like spines with tentacles along its back. Work on Chinese specimens in 1991 showed that the spines were on top and the "tentacles" were legs.
--- fact 4
In 2015 Martin Smith and Jean-Bernard Caron showed that the supposed head was a decay stain. The true head has a pair of simple eyes, a mouth ringed with plates, and a toothed throat.
--- fact 5
Its claws resemble those of living velvet worms, and it is placed among the lobopodians, stem relatives of that group. Some analyses place it more basally among the panarthropods.
--- context
Hallucigenia lived in the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]], during and after the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], and shared the Burgess Shale with [[species/anomalocaris|Anomalocaris]] and [[species/pikaia|Pikaia]]. At Chengjiang Fossil Site it lived alongside [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that an animal a few centimeters long was drawn upside down for fourteen years and its head found only in 2015. Much of the deep past is a reading of evidence, and the reading changes when specimens arrive. The reading would weaken if later study showed the 2015 head identification to be mistaken.
--- question 1
Where does Hallucigenia sit among the panarthropods?
--- question 2
How many valid species does the genus contain?

=== species/horse
title: Horse
type: species
date: c. 2200 BCE
themes: war, trade, technology
src: fa8c443348fd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic horse descends from a lineage that a 2021 genetic study traced to the Pontic-Caspian steppe by about 2200 BCE, and its use for riding and chariot traction transformed warfare, transport and steppe societies across Eurasia.
--- fact 1
A 2021 study of 264 ancient genomes traced modern domestic horses to a lineage that arose in the Volga-Don region of the Pontic-Caspian steppe by about 2200 BCE; horses lived wild across much of the continent before then, and that older history is outside this note.
--- fact 2
The Botai culture of Kazakhstan (about 3700 to 3500 BCE) left horse teeth with wear consistent with bits and traces of horse milking, but a 2018 study found modern domestic horses are not closely related to Botai horses, whose feral descendants are the Przewalski's horses, and some researchers hold that Botai people hunted and managed wild horses without fully domesticating them.
--- fact 3
Horse-drawn chariots appear in Sintashta and Petrovka graves dated about 2100 to 1700 BCE; within perhaps 500 years chariot evidence appears in Greece, Egypt and Mesopotamia, and within another 500 years in China.
--- fact 4
Horse bones appear in Mesopotamia from about 2000 BCE, where horses began to replace hybrid equids in war, and in the Iron Age mounted cavalry rose and mounted archers eventually displaced chariots.
--- fact 5
Columbus brought horses to Hispaniola on his 1493 voyage and Hernán Cortés landed in Mexico in 1519 with 16 horses; wild horses had vanished from the Americas at least 10,000 years earlier.
--- fact 6
After the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, horses left by the Spanish spread among Pueblo, Apache and Comanche communities, and by the late 1700s most peoples of the western plains were mounted.
--- context
Horses first shaped human history through the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]] and the [[technology/chariot|Chariot]], following the earlier and separate [[culture/botai-culture|Botai Culture]], and later mounted steppe peoples such as the [[culture/scythians|Scythians]], the [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]] and the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] built their power on them. The horse's return to the Americas belongs to the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. The Horse sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the horse changed the speed at which humans could move armies, messages and goods, so a claim-line that once held for weeks of travel could be crossed in days, and steppe societies that lived on horseback were able to reach agricultural states that were far away. The Plains peoples' adoption of horses within roughly a century after 1680 shows how quickly one animal can rebuild a way of life, and the sanctioned harm carried out on horseback across many centuries is recorded here as suffering. The reading would weaken if genetic work shows that the horse's spread owed less to riding communities than to trade in animals alone.
--- question 1
Were the Botai people true domesticators of horses, and what happened to their herds and their tradition?
--- question 2
What changed between about 2200 and 2000 BCE that made horses so valuable in war so quickly?

=== species/last-universal-common-ancestor
title: Last Universal Common Ancestor
type: species
date: 4.2 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: ca35e2e11205
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The last universal common ancestor, or LUCA, is the inferred population of cells from which all living Bacteria and Archaea, and so all life today, descend. It is reconstructed from the genes of living organisms, not from fossils, with age estimates from about 3.5 to 4.3 billion years ago.
--- fact 1
LUCA is not a named organism or a fossil. It is the last population ancestral to every cell alive today; other lineages living beside it left no descendants.
--- fact 2
A 2024 study dated LUCA to about 4.2 billion years ago (about 4.09 to 4.33, depending on the clock model), using gene duplications that predate LUCA. Older estimates gave 3.5 to 3.8 billion years, and some researchers doubt a LUCA older than 4 billion years.
--- fact 3
The same study inferred an anaerobic cell with about 2,650 proteins. A 2016 study with stricter criteria traced only 355 protein families to LUCA and pictured a heat-loving cell of hydrothermal vents.
--- fact 4
The oldest widely accepted traces of life are stromatolites in Western Australia, about 3.43 to 3.48 billion years old. Older claims, such as graphite in a 4.10-billion-year-old zircon from Jack Hills, are disputed.
--- fact 5
Where the root of the tree of life lies is unresolved and shifts every reconstruction.
--- context
LUCA falls near the boundary of the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] and the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]]. It follows the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]] and precedes the split into Bacteria, including the [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]], and [[species/archaea|Archaea]], the lineage behind the [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]]. Evidence includes [[species/stromatolites|Stromatolites]] and the [[place/jack-hills-zircons|Jack Hills Zircons]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that every cell of the species, like every cell of every living thing, descends from a population known only by inference, whose age is redrawn with each new method. The Observer treats LUCA as a precondition, one lineage among many that happened to leave descendants, and reads no direction into that survival. The reading would weaken if early gene exchange proved so extensive that no single ancestral population can be defined.
--- question 1
When did LUCA live, and was it one population or a community of cells exchanging genes?
--- question 2
Where does the root of the tree of life lie?

=== species/llama-and-alpaca
title: Llama and Alpaca
type: species
date: c. 4000 BCE
themes: trade, religion
src: 1e75b200b8e6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The llama and the alpaca are South American camelids domesticated in the high Andes of Peru about 6,000 years ago from the guanaco and the vicuña, and they were the Inca Empire's only beasts of burden and a main source of fiber.
--- fact 1
DNA analysis has confirmed that the guanaco is the wild ancestor of the llama and the vicuña is the wild ancestor of the alpaca.
--- fact 2
The earliest evidence of domestication comes from high-altitude puna sites in the Peruvian Andes between about 4,000 and 4,900 meters; at Telarmachay, about 170 kilometers northeast of Lima, animal remains suggest that early domestic llamas and alpacas were under human control by about 6,000 to 5,500 years ago and that a herding economy was established by about 5,500 years ago.
--- fact 3
Llamas were the only beasts of burden in the Inca Empire, and they also supplied meat and fiber; llama figures and llamas themselves were placed in burials as offerings, and one scholar argues that widespread Andean farming depended on llama dung as fertilizer.
--- fact 4
Alpaca fiber is much finer than llama fiber, about 10 to 15 micrometers in diameter against 20 to 40 micrometers.
--- fact 5
A colonial-era writer estimated that as many as 300,000 llamas were employed hauling cargo from the mines of Potosí alone, but after Europeans introduced horses, mules and donkeys the llama's importance as a beast of burden greatly diminished.
--- fact 6
More than seven million llamas and alpacas lived in South America in 2007.
--- context
Llamas carried goods along the roads of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] between [[place/cusco|Cusco]] and its provinces, drew on the highland pastures near [[place/lake-titicaca|Lake Titicaca]], and hauled silver from the mines of [[place/potosi|Potosi]] until European pack animals arrived through the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. The Llama and Alpaca sit in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a state whose only beasts of burden were llamas built its roads and storehouses for animals that carry a limited load and must be rested and fed, so the tool-lineage of Inca transport was shaped by the animal available and not by the wheel or the horse. After 1532 the exchange-web replaced this carrier with imported animals, so the Spanish conquest changed not only who ruled the routes but what moved along them. The reading would weaken if excavation shows that human porters carried far more of the Inca Empire's cargo than llama caravans did.
--- question 1
How large were llama caravans in the Inca Empire, and how did herds move between highland pastures and the coast?
--- question 2
Did the Spanish conquest and the arrival of horses and mules cause the numbers of llamas and alpacas to fall, and by how much?

=== species/maize
title: Maize
type: species
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: religion, trade
src: 8caf683e98f7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Maize is a cereal grass domesticated from wild teosinte in the Balsas River valley of southwestern Mexico about 9,000 years ago, and it became the staple crop and a central religious symbol across much of the pre-Columbian Americas before spreading worldwide after 1492.
--- fact 1
Maize was domesticated from Balsas teosinte in the Balsas River valley of Mexico about 9,000 years ago; stone milling tools bearing maize residue found near Iguala, Guerrero, are about 8,700 years old.
--- fact 2
Maize farming reached highland Ecuador by at least 8,000 years ago and the Colombian Andes between roughly 7,000 and 6,000 years ago, and spread north into what is now New Mexico and Arizona by about 4,100 years ago, with large-scale adoption in eastern North America by about 900 CE.
--- fact 3
By 3,000 years ago maize was central to Olmec culture, including its calendar and mythology, and later Maya and Aztec societies treated maize as a deity and processed it by nixtamalization, soaking and cooking it with an alkaline solution that improves its nutritional value.
--- fact 4
After 1492 Spanish settlers carried maize to Europe within decades, and it had become a common peasant food in southern Europe by the 17th century, and it also reached West Africa and the Philippines.
--- fact 5
Hybrid corn breeding, beginning with James Reid's Yellow Dent in the 1860s and double-cross hybrids from about 1918 to 1922, made hybrid maize the dominant form grown from the 1940s onward.
--- fact 6
Global maize production reached about 1.16 billion tonnes in 2020, led by the United States, China and Brazil, and it is used mainly for animal feed, with the rest split between human food, starch and syrup production, and ethanol and other biofuels.
--- context
Maize's domestication is described in more detail under [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]] and belongs to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Americas; its post-1492 spread is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It was central to the religion and calendar of the [[culture/olmecs|Olmecs]] and later the [[culture/maya|Maya]] and the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]]. Maize sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: that maize's deep place in Mesoamerican meaning-engines, as a deity and a calendar marker, reflects how thoroughly the plant reorganized daily and ritual life once it became a society's dominant food source, elevating a crop into a claim about the order of the world. Maize's rapid globalization after 1492, reaching Europe, Africa and Asia within a lifetime, shows an exchange-web moving faster than the older, slower spread of Old World grains. The reading would weaken if maize's religious centrality proves to have been less universal across Mesoamerican societies than the Olmec, Maya and Aztec evidence suggests.
--- question 1
How did the deification of maize in Mesoamerica compare with the treatment of other staple crops in other early farming societies?
--- question 2
How much of maize's post-1492 global spread depended on active promotion by colonial administrations rather than independent local adoption?

=== species/pikaia
title: Pikaia
type: species
date: 505 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 2912aa164902
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pikaia is a small, eel-like swimming animal from the Burgess Shale of British Columbia, about 505 million years old. Reinterpreted in 1979 as an early chordate, it is often cited as a relative of vertebrate ancestors, though not thought to be one.
--- fact 1
Pikaia gracilens is known only from the Walcott Quarry of the Burgess Shale, dated to about 505 million years ago by fossil zones, with no radiometric date and no later fossils.
--- fact 2
It is about 5 cm long and flattened from side to side, with about 100 muscle blocks, a bilobed head with short tentacles, a dorsal fin, and paired appendages.
--- fact 3
Charles Walcott described it in 1911 as a worm. In 1979 Simon Conway Morris and Harry Whittington read it as a chordate, and a 2012 redescription of 114 specimens read a narrow strand as a notochord. A 2024 study argued that earlier reconstructions were upside down.
--- fact 4
Older chordate-like animals from Chengjiang, about 518 million years old, include Haikouichthys, so Pikaia is not the earliest known chordate.
--- fact 5
In Wonderful Life (1989), Stephen Jay Gould used Pikaia to argue that history is contingent. Conway Morris later argued that evolution converges on similar solutions.
--- context
Pikaia lived in the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]] soon after the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], sharing the Burgess Shale with [[species/hallucigenia|Hallucigenia]] and [[species/anomalocaris|Anomalocaris]]. It is a relative of the lineage leading to vertebrates, later including fishes such as [[species/tiktaalik|Tiktaalik]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Pikaia became famous less for what it was than for the argument it served: a fossil small enough to lose in a tray of shale was used to ask whether the species' own lineage could have failed to exist. Contingency and convergence are competing readings of the same fossils, and Pikaia settles neither. The reading would weaken if the Cambrian record showed many independent chordate lineages, making chordate survival nearly certain.
--- question 1
Is Pikaia a stem-group chordate or a convergent form?
--- question 2
How much did chordate survival depend on lineages like it?

=== species/potato
title: Potato
type: species
date: c. 8000 BCE
themes: trade, earth-systems
src: b95a5b5343d6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The potato is a tuber crop domesticated in the highlands of southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia, near Lake Titicaca, roughly 7,000 to 10,000 years ago, and it later reshaped diet and population growth across Europe after its introduction there in the 16th century.
--- fact 1
The potato was domesticated from wild relatives in the S. brevicaule species complex, in the region around Lake Titicaca in southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia, an estimated 7,000 to 10,000 years ago; the earliest verified tuber remains, from Ancón on the central Peruvian coast, date to about 2500 BCE.
--- fact 2
Andean farmers developed thousands of potato varieties adapted to different altitudes, and Quechua and Aymara communities traditionally made chuño, a freeze-dried potato product, to preserve the crop through the region's cold nights.
--- fact 3
In Inca religious tradition the deity Axomama was associated with the fertility of potatoes and the soil.
--- fact 4
Spanish colonizers introduced the potato to Europe in the second half of the 16th century; European farmers were initially slow to adopt it, but after about 1750 it became an important staple, and the crop is estimated to have driven roughly a quarter of Old World population and urban growth between 1700 and 1900.
--- fact 5
Potato blight, caused by the water mold Phytophthora infestans, destroyed crops across Ireland and parts of Scotland from 1845, contributing to the Great Famine; estimates of Irish deaths run from about 700,000 to over 1 million, alongside roughly 1 million more who emigrated, and Ireland's population fell from about 8.4 million in 1844 to about 6.6 million by 1851.
--- fact 6
Global potato production reached about 390 million tonnes in 2024, led by China, India and Ukraine.
--- context
The potato was domesticated in the highlands around [[place/lake-titicaca|Lake Titicaca]] and became a staple of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]]; its spread to Europe after 1492 is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] and, alongside grains domesticated elsewhere, belongs to the wider story of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the potato's introduction to Europe shows a single crop reshaping a continent's demography over roughly two centuries, and that its dependence there on a small number of varieties left the food supply vulnerable to a single pathogen, a risk the Andean growers had reduced by keeping thousands of varieties in cultivation. The Irish famine is recorded here as mass suffering caused jointly by a crop disease and by the political and economic arrangements, including food exports during the crisis, that shaped who could obtain food. The reading would weaken if the population and urbanization gains credited to the potato in Europe prove to owe more to other agricultural and economic changes of the period.
--- question 1
How much did Andean crop diversity reduce the risk of famine there compared with Europe's narrower range of potato varieties?
--- question 2
How much of the Irish famine's death toll resulted from the blight itself as opposed to political and economic decisions made during the crisis?

=== species/rice
title: Rice
type: species
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 5962c231faed
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Rice is a cereal grass domesticated from a wild ancestor in the Yangtze valley of China from about 9,000 years ago, and it is now the staple food of more than half the world's population.
--- fact 1
Rice was domesticated from wild _Oryza rufipogon_ by Neolithic communities in the Upper and Lower Yangtze valley, in what is now China, about 9,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
Genetic evidence for the gene that stops rice grains from shattering is identical in the two main cultivated groups, japonica and indica, which points to a single original domestication event rather than two separate ones; indica rice is thought to have arisen later, when japonica reached India about 4,500 years ago and hybridized with a local wild or semi-wild rice.
--- fact 3
Rice farming reached Korea and Japan between about 5,500 and 3,200 years ago, and island Southeast Asia and Taiwan between about 5,500 and 4,000 years ago, carried in part by Austronesian-speaking migrations, and mainland Southeast Asia by about 5,000 years ago.
--- fact 4
Rice is the staple food of more than half the world's population, and China and India together consumed several hundred million tonnes of it in the early 2020s.
--- fact 5
The Green Revolution rice variety IR8 was bred in 1966 at the International Rice Research Institute by crossing an Indonesian and a Chinese variety, producing a short, strong-stemmed plant that resisted falling over under heavy fertilizer use.
--- fact 6
Global rice production reached about 800 million tonnes in 2023, with about 90 percent grown in Asia, and less than 8 percent of the crop traded internationally.
--- context
Rice's domestication is described in more detail under [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]] and belongs to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] alongside cereals domesticated elsewhere; its 20th-century breeding is part of the [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]]. Rice farming shaped the population and economy of the [[place/yellow-river|Yellow River]] region and other East Asian river valleys. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that rice farming, especially the flooded-field methods used across much of Asia, ties a community to sustained cooperative labor: building and maintaining paddies, canals and terraces is a task larger than any one household, which the Observer reads as a kin-lattice pressure toward dense, cooperative settlement. That most of the world's rice is grown and eaten within Asia, with little traded across borders, shows a staple that remains tied to the regions that farm it rather than becoming a fully global exchange-good in the way wheat has. The reading would weaken if paddy-field cooperation proves to have mattered less to social organization than dry-field cereal farming elsewhere.
--- question 1
Was there truly a single domestication event for rice, or did separate lineages contribute more than current genetic evidence suggests?
--- question 2
How did paddy irrigation systems shape the social organization of the communities that built and maintained them?

=== species/sheep
title: Sheep
type: species
date: c. 9000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 4ef157a97a38
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic sheep was bred from the wild mouflon in Mesopotamia between about 11,000 and 9000 BCE, first kept for meat, milk and skins, and only later selectively bred for wool, and it now numbers well over a billion animals worldwide.
--- fact 1
Sheep were domesticated from the mouflon, a wild sheep of southwestern Asia, in an estimated window between 11,000 and 9000 BCE in Mesopotamia, and the practice had spread to Mehrgarh in the Indus Valley by about 7000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Early domestic sheep were kept mainly for meat, milk and skins rather than fleece; archaeological evidence, including statuary from Iran, suggests deliberate selection for woolly sheep began around 6000 BCE, roughly two millennia after initial domestication, a shift sometimes called the secondary products revolution.
--- fact 3
The earliest known woven wool garments date to around 4000 BCE, and Neolithic farmers in what is now France, among the Castelnovien people, were keeping domestic sheep by about 6000 BCE.
--- fact 4
Sheep are raised in large numbers today across Australia, New Zealand, the southern regions of South America, South Africa and the British Isles, and China and India also hold very large flocks by absolute count; the world's domestic sheep population was estimated at around 1.2 billion in 2019.
--- context
Sheep were among the earliest animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] and, alongside crops such as wheat and barley, formed one pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent; they were commonly herded alongside the [[species/goat|Goat]], domesticated in a similar place and period. Sheep sit in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: that the roughly two-thousand-year gap between sheep domestication and deliberate wool breeding suggests early herders did not domesticate the animal with fleece production already in mind, but discovered and then bred for a secondary use only once the animal was already established in their kin-lattice, a pattern the Observer reads as evidence that a domesticated species' eventual economic role can differ substantially from the reason it was first tamed. The animal's spread across so much of the pastoral world since then shows a tool-lineage of herding techniques traveling with the species itself. The reading would weaken if evidence of early wool use is found that predates the currently accepted timeline for woolly-sheep breeding.
--- question 1
What use did early herders make of sheep in the roughly two millennia before wool breeding began, beyond meat, milk and skins?
--- question 2
How much did sheep domestication in Mesopotamia influence, or draw on, the roughly contemporary domestication of goats in the same region?

=== species/sorghum
title: Sorghum
type: species
date: c. 3500 BCE
themes: trade, earth-systems
src: 4267ea03a5dd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sorghum is a drought-tolerant cereal grass domesticated in eastern Sudan more than 5,000 years ago, and it remains a staple food across Africa, Central America and South Asia today.
--- fact 1
Sorghum bicolor was domesticated in eastern Sudan, in the area of the Atbara and Gash rivers, more than 5,000 years ago; archaeological evidence near Kassala, linked to the Neolithic Butana Group culture, dates domestication to about 3500 to 3000 BCE, and a piece of sorghum bread from Predynastic Egypt, about 5,100 years old, survives in the Egyptian Museum in Turin.
--- fact 2
The crop spread to the Indian subcontinent by about 4,000 years ago and reached West Africa by about 3,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
During the medieval Arab agricultural expansion, sorghum was carried across the Arab world as far as Al-Andalus in Spain.
--- fact 4
Sorghum is highly drought- and heat-tolerant and efficient with nitrogen, which makes it valuable in arid and semi-arid regions, and it remains a food staple in Africa, Central America and South Asia, ranking as the fifth most widely grown cereal crop in the world.
--- fact 5
Global production reached about 57 million tonnes in 2023, led by the United States, Mexico and Ethiopia, and the grain is used for food, animal feed and biofuel, including ethanol, with an energy return comparable to sugarcane and higher than maize.
--- context
Sorghum's domestication is one of Africa's own contributions to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], distinct from and roughly contemporary with the grain crops domesticated in the Fertile Crescent, East Asia and the Americas; its spread to India and later across the Arab world followed routes that also carried other goods along the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] and, in a later period, the scholarly exchange of the [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that sorghum's drought tolerance made it a crop suited to regions that wheat, rice and maize could not reliably serve, so its spread traces a different exchange-web than the more commonly told story of Old World grain crops, one shaped by aridity rather than fertile river valleys. Its long-standing importance in regions now facing more frequent drought under a changing climate gives an old crop renewed attention as a hedge against food insecurity. The reading would weaken if sorghum's spread into India and the Arab world proves to have followed political conquest more than independent agricultural adoption.
--- question 1
How independently did sorghum domestication occur relative to the other cereal domestications in the Fertile Crescent, East Asia and the Americas?
--- question 2
How much could wider sorghum cultivation reduce vulnerability to drought in regions that currently depend on less drought-tolerant staples?

=== species/soybean
title: Soybean
type: species
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: dbb27ccb0936
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The soybean is a legume domesticated in the region between the Yellow and Huai rivers in China between roughly 9,000 and 6,000 years ago, long central to East Asian diets through foods such as tofu and soy sauce, and now grown worldwide chiefly as animal feed and oil.
--- fact 1
The soybean was domesticated in north-central China, between the Yellow River and the Huai River, with earliest evidence from the site of Jiahu in Henan province dated to between about 9,000 and 7,800 years ago.
--- fact 2
Chinese tradition credits the legendary Emperor Shennong, dated in myth to 2853 BCE, with naming the soybean one of five sacred grains alongside rice, wheat, barley and millet.
--- fact 3
Fermented soy foods such as soy sauce, fermented bean paste, natto and tempeh, along with tofu made from soy milk, developed over many centuries in East Asia; the earliest Japanese textual reference to soybeans appears in the Kojiki, compiled in 712 CE.
--- fact 4
The soybean was introduced to the Americas and Europe in the 18th century, with the first recorded North American cultivation in 1765 on Skidaway Island, Georgia, and the first French cultivation in 1779.
--- fact 5
Genetically modified, herbicide-tolerant soybean varieties were introduced commercially in 1995 and made up about 8 percent of United States soybean acreage by 1997, rising to about 93 percent by 2010.
--- fact 6
In 2019 Brazil, the United States and Argentina were the largest producers, together accounting for roughly four-fifths of world output, and about 80 percent of the global soybean crop is used to feed livestock.
--- context
The soybean's domestication is one of East Asia's founding contributions to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], developed alongside rice and other regional crops; its adoption of genetically modified varieties from the 1990s parallels the yield-focused breeding of the 20th-century [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]] in other crops. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the soybean has passed through two very different uses over its long relationship with humans: for millennia mainly a food transformed by fermentation and pressing into tofu, sauce and other dishes across East Asia, and in the last century mainly an input, grown at enormous scale chiefly to feed livestock and produce oil, in a system reliant on genetically modified varieties barely three decades old. This shift shows how a crop's tool-lineage of use can change faster than the plant itself. The reading would weaken if the rapid adoption of genetically modified soy proves to have depended more on regulatory approval sequencing than on farmer preference.
--- question 1
How much of the soybean's shift toward livestock feed and oil, rather than direct human food, reflects changing global diets versus agricultural economics?
--- question 2
How did the introduction of genetically modified soybeans affect farming communities differently across producing countries?

=== species/sponges
title: Sponges
type: species
date: 540 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 0c75e225e146
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sponges (phylum Porifera) are simple aquatic animals without true tissues or organs and among the earliest branches of the animal family tree. Unambiguous fossils begin in the earliest Cambrian, about 535 million years ago; older fossil and chemical claims are disputed.
--- fact 1
Sponges have no true tissues or organs; flagellated cells drive water through a jelly-like body. About 9,900 valid species are described, over 90 percent of them demosponges.
--- fact 2
Undisputed sponge spicules first appear about 535 million years ago in South China. Sponge-like archaeocyaths were common from about 530 to 500 million years ago.
--- fact 3
A 2009 study read a rare sterol in rocks 635 million years old or older as a sponge biomarker. Later work showed that algae or protists could make similar compounds.
--- fact 4
Older body fossils, such as Otavia in Namibia (about 760 million years) and structures in 890-million-year-old Canadian reefs, are read by critics as mineral grains or as unconfirmed.
--- fact 5
Molecular clocks place the common ancestor of living animals roughly 830 to 650 million years ago. Whether sponges or comb jellies branched off first is unresolved.
--- context
Sponges belong to the late [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] and early Paleozoic, near the base of animal lineages that include the [[species/ediacaran-biota|Ediacaran Biota]] and those of the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]]. Their origin bears on the [[event/origin-of-multicellularity|Origin of Multicellularity]] and on the [[era/cryogenian-period|Cryogenian Period]] of [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the oldest animals may have been among the humblest, with no organs, and that searches for their first appearance keep producing claims that later fail on inspection. The Observer treats sponges as a precondition, not a stage on a road to something else. The reading would weaken if a securely dated sponge fossil from well before the Ediacaran were confirmed.
--- question 1
Are there sponge body fossils older than the Cambrian?
--- question 2
Which animal lineage branched off first?
