=== event/indian-rebellion-of-1857
title: Indian Rebellion of 1857
type: event
date: 1857 – 1858
themes: war, religion, kinship
src: d98c51607060
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Indian Rebellion of 1857 began as a mutiny of sepoys (Indian soldiers) of the East India Company army at Meerut on 10 May 1857 and grew into a wider uprising across northern and central India, ending British East India Company rule.
--- fact 1
The rebellion lasted until 1858 and was suppressed by the British.
--- fact 2
The rebel leaders included the last Mughal emperor and Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi, who died in battle on 17 June 1858.
--- fact 3
A Royal Proclamation of 1 November 1858 transferred rule from the Company to the British Crown.
--- fact 4
It is called the Sepoy Mutiny by many British writers and the First War of Independence by many Indian historians.
--- context
The uprising ended the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] in name and led to direct Crown rule in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]]. See [[person/rani-lakshmibai|Rani Lakshmibai]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the same event carries different names in different languages of memory. The name chosen already contains an argument about what happened.
--- question 1
How widespread was popular, as opposed to military, participation?

=== event/indian-removal-and-the-trail-of-tears
title: Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears
type: event
date: c. 1830 – 1850
themes: war, kinship
src: f8caf9a2b99a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Indian Removal Act of 1830 authorized the United States to relocate Native American nations from the southeastern states to lands west of the Mississippi, leading to forced marches known as the Trail of Tears.
--- fact 1
The Act was signed by President Andrew Jackson in 1830.
--- fact 2
It affected the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek, and Seminole nations.
--- fact 3
In 1838 to 1839, about 16,000 Cherokee were forced to march west; commonly cited estimates put deaths at around 4,000.
--- fact 4
The Supreme Court had ruled in Worcester v. Georgia (1832) in favor of Cherokee rights, but the ruling was not enforced.
--- context
The removals were part of the westward expansion of the United States in [[region/north-america|North America]]. Compare the political traditions of the [[culture/haudenosaunee-confederacy|Haudenosaunee Confederacy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a legal system ruled in favor of a people, and the executive branch declined to enforce it. Law is only one part of what determines outcomes; enforcement is another.
--- question 1
What were the total deaths across all the removals?

=== event/indo-european-language-spread
title: Indo-European Language Spread
type: event
date: c. 3000 – 1000 BCE
themes: language, war, kinship
src: 67758024bcbb
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Languages of the Indo-European family, including ancestors of English, Hindi, Persian, Greek, Latin, and Russian, spread across Eurasia beginning several thousand years ago. Where the family began is still disputed.
--- fact 1
Today, Indo-European languages are the first language of roughly 40 to 46 percent of the world's population, depending on the estimate.
--- fact 2
The leading view for the Indo-European homeland has been the Pontic-Caspian steppe, supported by ancient DNA from Yamnaya-related groups. Two papers in Nature in 2025 refine this. They place the ancestral community, speaking what they call proto-Indo-Anatolian, in the Caucasus-Lower Volga region about 4400 to 4000 BCE, with the Yamnaya descending from it and spreading the language later. On this account the Anatolian branch, which includes Hittite, did not come from the steppe.
--- fact 3
A 2023 study in Science proposed a hybrid model, with an earlier homeland south of the Caucasus and a later branch moving north onto the steppe. Some linguists dispute the 2025 interpretation.
--- fact 4
An alternative hypothesis links the origin to Anatolia and the spread of farming.
--- fact 5
The relationship between language, genes, and archaeology remains actively debated.
--- context
The spread happened in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and is tied to the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]] and to horse and wheel technology; see [[technology/the-wheel|The Wheel]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a language family can outlive the empires that spread it. A shared tongue is traceable evidence of ancient contact between peoples who no longer remember it.
--- question 1
Which hypothesis for the homeland best fits all the evidence? The 2025 genetic results narrow the steppe hypothesis but do not settle the linguistic argument.

--- summary
The Industrial Revolution was the shift, beginning in Britain about 1760, from hand production and animal power to machine production driven by coal and steam.
--- fact 1
Key developments included mechanized textile production, the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]], and railways.
--- fact 2
Economic output per person began to rise steadily for the first time in history.
--- fact 3
Urban populations grew rapidly, with harsh factory conditions and child labor.
--- fact 4
The process spread to continental Europe, North America, and later Asia.
--- context
It opens the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and connects to [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] trade and empire; see [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer calls it the unlocking of stored sunlight: coal and later oil are ancient photosynthesis. For the first time, a species multiplied its available energy by an order of magnitude in a few generations.

=== event/interbreeding-of-homo-sapiens-with-neanderthals-and-denisovans
title: Interbreeding of Homo sapiens with Neanderthals and Denisovans
type: event
date: c. 60 – 40 ka
themes: kinship, science
src: 0f34c2770273
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Homo sapiens interbred with Neanderthals and with Denisovans, as ancient and present-day DNA show. Most people outside Africa carry about 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal ancestry, and some Oceanian populations carry up to about 5 percent Denisovan ancestry.
--- fact 1
Present-day people outside Africa carry about 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal ancestry. Oceanian populations carry up to about 5 percent Denisovan ancestry, and other groups much less.
--- fact 2
A 2024 study concluded that most Neanderthal gene flow into modern humans came in one extended episode about 50,500 to 43,500 years ago, lasting about 7,000 years. Denisovan gene flow is dated more loosely, and some studies infer more than one episode.
--- fact 3
Denisova 11, a bone fragment from Denisova Cave, belonged to a girl at least 13 years old who lived about 90,000 years ago. Her mother was a Neanderthal and her father a Denisovan, and she is the only known first-generation hybrid.
--- fact 4
Some inherited variants were useful: a Denisovan variant of EPAS1 helps Tibetans at high altitude, and archaic variants affect immune genes. Archaic ancestry is scarcer in gene-rich regions, which suggests that many variants were harmful and were removed.
--- context
The interbreeding took place around and after the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]] and involved [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]], [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]], and the [[species/denisovans|Denisovans]]. It overlaps the [[event/neanderthal-extinction|Neanderthal Extinction]] and the [[event/peopling-of-europe-by-homo-sapiens|Peopling of Europe by Homo sapiens]], and the ancient-DNA methods that revealed it belong to the [[event/genomic-revolution|Genomic Revolution]]. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that boundaries between human groups that later stories treat as fixed were, in the Paleolithic, permeable to the point of shared descent. Other human species live on in most living people as minority ancestry, passed down the kin-lattice from a few thousand years of contact. The reading would weaken if many of these DNA signals proved to come from ancient population structure in Africa instead of interbreeding.
--- question 1
How many separate episodes of interbreeding occurred, and where?
--- question 2
What did the inherited DNA do for those who carried it, and what did it cost them?

--- summary
The Last Glacial Maximum was the most recent time when ice sheets reached their greatest extent, about 26,500 to 19,000 years ago.
--- fact 1

=== event/late-heavy-bombardment
title: Late Heavy Bombardment
type: event
date: 4.1 – 3.8 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: 52d8480da0b5
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Late Heavy Bombardment is a hypothesized spike in asteroid impacts on the inner Solar System, roughly 4.1 to 3.8 billion years ago, inferred mainly from radiometric ages of lunar impact rocks.
--- fact 1
The hypothesis grew from Apollo-era rock samples whose impact-melt ages cluster near 3.8 to 3.9 billion years.
--- fact 2
Some researchers argue the cluster reflects sampling of a few huge basin impacts rather than a real surge in the whole Solar System's impact rate.
--- fact 3
The Moon preserves the impact record; Earth's earliest crust has been largely destroyed by plate tectonics and erosion.
--- fact 4
Some dynamical models, such as the Nice model, propose that a shift in the giant planets' orbits could have triggered such a surge.
--- fact 5
Whether the episode happened at all, and whether it would have sterilized early life, remains debated.
--- context
The Late Heavy Bombardment, if real, falls at the end of the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] and overlaps the earliest evidence for life, so it bears on [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]]. It follows the [[event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon|Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon]] and the [[event/formation-of-the-solar-system-and-earth|Formation of the Solar System and Earth]]. Late Heavy Bombardment sits in the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that much of Earth's first chapter is read from another world's scars: the Moon kept the record that Earth erased. A history-keeping species is reconstructing its planet's early history from its neighbor's surface. The reading would be weakened if the impact-age cluster proved to be a sampling artifact.
--- question 1
Was there a distinct spike, or a long declining tail of accretion?
--- question 2
Could life have survived or arisen during the peak?

The Messinian Salinity Crisis was a period from about 5.96 to 5.33 million years ago when the Mediterranean Sea was cut off from the Atlantic and left thick salt deposits on its floor.
--- fact 1
Restriction of the Gibraltar gateway caused the Mediterranean to evaporate and deposit more than a million cubic kilometers of salt.

The crisis ended about 5.33 million years ago, when Atlantic water returned through the Gibraltar region.

The salt deposits lie beneath the present Mediterranean seafloor.

The crisis shaped the basin around which later Mediterranean civilizations grew; see [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]]. === event/middle-pleistocene-transition
title: Middle Pleistocene Transition
type: event
date: c. 1.25 Ma – 700 ka
themes: earth-systems
src: 57e9f1001ec2
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Middle Pleistocene Transition was a change in the rhythm of the ice ages between about 1.25 million and 0.7 million years ago. Glacial cycles lengthened from about 41,000 to about 100,000 years, with larger ice sheets and colder glacial stages.
--- fact 1
Marine oxygen-isotope records show glacial cycles paced by the 41,000-year tilt cycle until about 1.25 million years ago and dominated by cycles of about 100,000 years by about 0.7 million years ago. Estimates of the start and end vary.
--- fact 2
The 100,000-year cycles carry more ice and have a sawtooth shape: slow growth, then rapid melting.
--- fact 3
The shift came without a matching change in orbital forcing, so its cause lies inside the climate system. Proposed causes include declining atmospheric carbon dioxide, removal of loose regolith beneath northern ice sheets so that ice grew thicker and more stable, and changes in deep-ocean circulation.
--- fact 4
Some reconstructions place a slowdown of deep-ocean circulation near 0.9 million years ago; others find only modest change. Whether the shift was gradual or abrupt is debated.
--- context
The transition falls within the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and [[era/quaternary-period|Quaternary Period]]. It changed the rhythm that began with the [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]] under the pacing described in [[event/milankovitch-cycles-and-the-ice-ages|Milankovitch Cycles and the Ice Ages]], and it set up the later cycles, including the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. It overlaps the range of [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]]; no causal link to hominin evolution is established.
--- observer
The Observer files the transition under preconditions: the ice-age world every later people lived in changed its tempo partway through the Quaternary, from a 41,000-year beat to a 100,000-year one. Longer glacial stages mean longer stretches of lower sea level and shrunken habitable ranges, though the Observer does not claim to know how hominins responded. The reading would weaken if the change proved to be an artifact of how sediment records are dated and aligned.
--- question 1
What triggered the change when orbital forcing did not change?
--- question 2
Did longer, harsher glacial stages alter hominin populations in Africa and Eurasia?

=== event/milankovitch-cycles-and-the-ice-ages
title: Milankovitch Cycles and the Ice Ages
type: event
date: c. 2.58 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c9c7a9d0de50
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Milankovitch cycles are slow, regular changes in the shape of Earth's orbit, the tilt of its axis, and the wobble of that axis. They shift how sunlight is spread across latitudes and seasons, and since about 2.6 million years ago they have paced the growth and melting of the Quaternary ice sheets.
--- fact 1
The three cycles are eccentricity (orbit shape; about 100,000 and 400,000 years), obliquity (axial tilt, between about 22.1 and 24.5 degrees; 41,000 years), and precession (repeating about every 19,000 to 23,000 years).
--- fact 2
Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković calculated between the 1920s and 1940s how these changes alter sunlight by latitude. In 1976 Hays, Imbrie, and Shackleton found the same three periods in oxygen-isotope records from deep-sea sediment cores, which track global ice volume.
--- fact 3
The orbital changes alter the distribution of sunlight far more than its yearly total, so ice sheets, ocean circulation, and atmospheric carbon dioxide amplify the effect.
--- fact 4
Glacial cycles followed the 41,000-year tilt cycle until about 1.25 million years ago, then lengthened to about 100,000 years; see [[event/middle-pleistocene-transition|Middle Pleistocene Transition]]. Why the 100,000-year cycle dominates is unresolved.
--- fact 5
The cycles are far older than the ice ages; what changed about 2.7 million years ago was the climate's response (see [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]]).
--- context
Milankovitch cycles pace the glacial stages of the [[era/quaternary-period|Quaternary Period]] and [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]], including the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]], and the current interglacial, the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]]. The [[event/younger-dryas|Younger Dryas]] interrupted the last warming. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' whole written record sits inside one warm phase of a cycle set by the geometry of Earth's orbit, a cycle uncovered by pattern-taming from Milanković's sums to the 1976 sediment cores. === event/neanderthal-extinction
title: Neanderthal Extinction
type: event
date: c. 41 – 39 ka
themes: kinship, earth-systems
src: 8f9cebe1a055
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Neanderthals disappeared from the archaeological record of Europe about 41,000 to 39,000 years ago, according to radiocarbon dating of 40 sites. They vanished after several thousand years of overlap with Homo sapiens, and no single cause is agreed.
--- fact 1
A 2014 study using improved radiocarbon dating at 40 sites from Spain to Russia found that the Mousterian, the Neanderthal stone-tool industry, ended by about 41,030 to 39,260 calibrated years before present. It estimated an overlap with Homo sapiens of about 2,600 to 5,400 years, a mosaic and not a sudden replacement.
--- fact 2
Claims of later survival, such as Gorham's Cave in Gibraltar at about 28,000 years ago, rest on indirect dating and are widely regarded as unproven.
--- fact 3
Proposed factors include unstable climate, with a cold, dry spell called Heinrich Stadial 4 around 40,000 years ago; competition with Homo sapiens; disease; and small, inbred populations.
--- fact 4
Neanderthal DNA survives in present-day people outside Africa at about 1 to 2 percent, so some Neanderthal ancestry was absorbed; see [[event/interbreeding-of-homo-sapiens-with-neanderthals-and-denisovans|Interbreeding of Homo sapiens with Neanderthals and Denisovans]].
--- fact 5
A very large eruption in Italy about 40,000 years ago, the Campanian Ignimbrite Eruption, has been suggested as a factor. Its effect on Neanderthals is disputed.
--- context
The extinction ends the [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] lineage near the boundary between the [[era/middle-paleolithic|Middle Paleolithic]] and the [[era/upper-paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]]. It overlaps the [[event/peopling-of-europe-by-homo-sapiens|Peopling of Europe by Homo sapiens]]. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer treats this as a case in which the species has a stake in the answer, and is wary of the tidy version in which one group beat another. The evidence supports thousands of years of contact, some interbreeding, small Neanderthal populations, and hard climate. The reading would weaken if a well-dated site showed direct conflict or a sudden collapse.
--- question 1
Did any Neanderthal populations survive after about 39,000 years ago?
--- question 2
How much did climate, competition, disease, and small population size each contribute?

type: event
date: 3 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: d628aa12ae10
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The onset of plate tectonics is the start of the motion of rigid plates of Earth's outer shell, with ocean floor recycled into the mantle at subduction zones. Proposed dates range from before 4 billion to about 0.8 billion years ago, and the question remains one of the most disputed in Earth science.
--- fact 1
Plate tectonics moves about a dozen large plates and recycles ocean floor at subduction zones. Earth is the only planet known to have it.
--- fact 2
Proposed onsets include before 4.0 billion years ago, about 3.2 billion years ago, about 2.5 billion years ago and about 0.8 billion years ago. Some researchers argue for a gradual transition from a rigid outer lid, or for episodic subduction long before modern-style plate motion.
--- fact 3
Blueschists, which form in cold subduction zones, and ultra-high-pressure metamorphic rocks appear in the record only after about 0.8 billion years ago, which some geologists read as a marker of modern-style subduction.
--- fact 4
Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912, and plate tectonics gained wide acceptance in the 1960s.
--- fact 5
Plate tectonics builds continents and recycles carbon; whether it was needed for a long-lived biosphere is debated.
--- context
The Onset of Plate Tectonics is tied to the growth of continents in the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and to the [[event/earliest-continental-crust|Earliest Continental Crust]]. Its later effects include the [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]] and the [[event/collision-of-india-and-asia|Collision of India and Asia]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Earth's surface has recycled its own earliest evidence, so when the process began may be settled only indirectly. Some geologists treat plate tectonics as a precondition for a long-lived biosphere, and the Observer keeps that as an open hypothesis. It reads the current disagreement as information about the limits of the record, and it would weaken if a reliable early rock record were found.
--- question 1
When did plate tectonics begin, and was the change gradual or sudden?
--- question 2
Was plate tectonics required for a long-lived biosphere?

=== event/origin-of-bipedalism
title: Origin of Bipedalism
type: event
date: 7 – 3.7 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c7657be34286
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Habitual upright walking arose in the human lineage in Africa between roughly 7 and 4 million years ago. It is the earliest hallmark of the hominins, millions of years older than large brains or stone tools, but its starting point and cause are disputed.
--- fact 1
Sahelanthropus tchadensis, from Chad and about 7 million years old, has a skull base that suggests upright posture. A 2022 study of a femur argued for habitual bipedalism, but others dispute this, and its status as a hominin is debated.
--- fact 2
Orrorin tugenensis, from Kenya and about 6 million years old, is known from thigh bones read as evidence of bipedality; the reading is controversial.
--- fact 3
Ardipithecus ramidus, from Ethiopia and about 4.4 million years old, had a pelvis combining climbing and walking features and a grasping big toe. How well it walked is debated, and its excavators read its site as woodland, not open savanna.
--- fact 4
Footprints at Laetoli, Tanzania, about 3.66 million years old and attributed to [[species/australopithecus-afarensis|Australopithecus afarensis]], are the earliest direct record of upright walking.
--- fact 5
Proposed reasons include energy efficiency, carrying food or infants, heat regulation, and feeding from thin branches. None is agreed.
--- context
The origin of bipedalism follows the [[event/divergence-of-the-hominin-lineage|Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]] and falls within the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]]. It precedes the [[event/emergence-of-the-genus-homo|Emergence of the Genus Homo]] and the [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]] by millions of years. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' ancestors walked upright for millions of years before they had large brains or made recognizable tools, so the posture was not an adaptation for those things, and nothing in the record shows it heading toward them. The Observer reads it as a precondition: hands freed from walking could later carry and make. The reading would weaken if the earliest claimed bipeds proved to be apes with independently evolved postures.
--- question 1
Was Sahelanthropus a habitual biped, and was it a hominin?
--- question 2
Did upright walking begin on the ground or in the trees?
