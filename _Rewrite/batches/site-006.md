=== place/hispaniola
title: Hispaniola
type: place
date: c. 4000 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade, war
src: 498cdb61841d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hispaniola is the second-largest island of the Caribbean, in the Greater Antilles, divided between Haiti in the west and the Dominican Republic in the east, and the site of the first lasting European settlement in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Hispaniola covers about 76,000 square kilometers; its features include Pico Duarte, at 3,098 meters the highest peak in the Caribbean, and Lake Enriquillo, a saline lake about 40 meters below sea level.
--- fact 2
The earliest evidence of human presence on Hispaniola is dated by many archaeologists to about 4000 BCE, though estimates vary; by 1492 the island was home to Taíno societies organized into several chiefdoms, and Spanish chroniclers report the names Ayiti, Quisqueya and Bohío for it.
--- fact 3
Columbus reached Hispaniola in December 1492, and the Spanish founded La Navidad (destroyed in 1493), La Isabela (1493) and Santo Domingo (1496).
--- fact 4
The Taíno population of Hispaniola declined sharply after 1492 under forced labor, violence and disease.
--- fact 5
In 1697 Spain ceded the western third of Hispaniola to France, which developed the sugar and slave colony of Saint-Domingue; the Haitian Revolution (1791 to 1804) ended slavery and French rule there.
--- fact 6
The Dominican Republic became independent in 1844, after Haitian rule over the whole island from 1822.
--- context
Hispaniola links the [[culture/taino|Taino]], the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], the city of [[place/santo-domingo|Santo Domingo]], the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (its earliest human presence) and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one island now holds two states, two languages and two colonial histories because a treaty between distant European powers drew a claim-line through it in 1697, with no reference to anything on the island. Hispaniola compresses the Atlantic system into one place, with an indigenous society, a first European base, a plantation colony built on enslaved labor and a successful revolution of enslaved people that founded a state, all within about three centuries. The pattern is not unique, since other Caribbean islands show parts of it, and a specialist could weaken the reading by showing that Hispaniola was more exceptional or more ordinary than it suggests.
--- question 1
When did people first reach Hispaniola, and from where?
--- question 2
How large was the Taíno population of the island in 1492?

=== place/isfahan
title: Isfahan
type: place
date: 1051
themes: art, religion, trade
src: b3959feee618
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Isfahan is a city on the Zayande River in central Iran, a Seljuk capital from 1051 and the capital of the Safavid Empire from 1598, when Shah Abbas I rebuilt its center around the great square Naqsh-e Jahan.
--- fact 1
Isfahan stands on the Zayande River on the Iranian Plateau and was settled long before Islam; it became a Seljuk capital in 1051, and its Friday Mosque reflects more than a thousand years of mosque architecture (UNESCO inscribed it in 2012).
--- fact 2
After a revolt in 1387, Timur's forces killed a large part of Isfahan's population; chroniclers give figures of tens of thousands.
--- fact 3
Shah Abbas I moved the Safavid capital from Qazvin to Isfahan in 1598 and built the Naqsh-e Jahan (Imam) Square, the Shah and Sheikh Lotfollah mosques, the Ali Qapu palace and covered markets.
--- fact 4
Estimates by 17th-century European visitors put Isfahan's population at several hundred thousand, and a Persian saying called the city "half the world".
--- fact 5
In 1604 to 1605 Shah Abbas forcibly moved many Armenians from Julfa on the Aras River into Safavid territory, and many died on the way; the survivors founded New Julfa, an Armenian quarter of Isfahan, in about 1606.
--- fact 6
Afghan forces under Mahmud Hotak besieged Isfahan in 1722, and the resulting famine killed many tens of thousands of its inhabitants; the fall of the city was a decisive blow to Safavid rule.
--- fact 7
Isfahan ceased to be the capital in the 18th century, and UNESCO inscribed Meidan Emam, Esfahan in 1979.
--- context
Isfahan was the capital of the [[culture/safavid-empire|Safavid Empire]], a rival of the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]], and it had earlier suffered the massacre ordered by Timur, founder of the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Isfahan under Shah Abbas is a capital designed as a display: a square where a palace, a royal mosque and a merchants' bazaar face one another, placing the ruler's authority, the religious establishment and commerce in one visible arrangement, a status-signal in urban form. The same ruler's forced movement of the Armenians, which the Observer records as suffering, produced a prosperous merchant community that traded silk across Eurasia, an exchange-web outcome of coercion. The reading would weaken if the wealth of New Julfa proves to have come mainly from trading networks that existed before Safavid patronage.
--- question 1
How large was Isfahan's population at its peak?
--- question 2
How many Armenians died during the deportation from Julfa?

=== place/issyk-kul
title: Issyk-Kul
type: place
date: c. 800 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade, science
src: fd0acc8807bf
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Issyk-Kul is a large mountain lake in the northern Tian Shan of Kyrgyzstan, at about 1,600 meters above sea level, on the routes between the steppe and the oases and near cemeteries whose graves have been linked to an early stage of the Black Death.
--- fact 1
Issyk-Kul has an area of about 6,200 square kilometers, a maximum depth of about 668 meters and no outlet; the lake is slightly saline and does not freeze in winter, and its name means "warm lake" in Kyrgyz.
--- fact 2
Saka burial mounds around the lake date from the 1st millennium BCE; the Saka were Iranian-speaking pastoralists related to the Scythians.
--- fact 3
Underwater surveys have reported settlement remains and objects from the shallows, dated variously from the Bronze Age to the Middle Ages; interpretations vary.
--- fact 4
Routes across the Tian Shan connecting the steppe with the oases of Central Asia passed near the lake.
--- fact 5
Gravestones in cemeteries at Kara-Djigach and Burana, in the region of Issyk-Kul, record deaths from pestilence in 1338 to 1339.
--- fact 6
A 2022 study reported Yersinia pestis DNA from graves at Kara-Djigach and concluded that the strain was close to the ancestor of the lineage that caused the Black Death; some researchers question whether this locates the origin of the pandemic.
--- context
Issyk-Kul was used by Saka groups related to the [[culture/scythians|Scythians]] and lay near routes of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. Its region is one candidate for the beginning of the [[event/black-death|Black Death]]. It sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a community's grief-rites left the evidence that later science used: gravestones that named the cause of death, read six centuries afterward through ancient DNA, an unplanned act of memory-outsourcing that dated the disease's path. A warm, ice-free mountain lake on routes between pastoral and farming societies drew successive peoples, and the same routes carried the pathogen. The origin of the Black Death is still debated, and the reading would weaken if later studies place the pandemic's origin elsewhere.
--- question 1
Did the Black Death begin near Issyk-Kul, or did a plague lineage already circulate more widely?
--- question 2
What do the submerged sites on the lake bed show about earlier settlement?

=== place/jack-hills-zircons
title: Jack Hills Zircons
type: place
date: 4.4 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 7b26c06a5ad1
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Jack Hills zircons are tiny crystals from the Jack Hills of Western Australia, some dated to about 4.4 billion years, the oldest known pieces of Earth's crust.
--- fact 1
The zircon grains occur in ancient sedimentary rock in the Narryer Terrane of Western Australia, reworked into rocks that are themselves about 3 billion years old.
--- fact 2
The oldest grains are dated by uranium-lead decay to roughly 4.4 billion years, only about 150 million years after Earth formed.
--- fact 3
Oxygen-isotope ratios in some grains have been interpreted as evidence that their parent magma had interacted with liquid water, though this reading is contested.
--- fact 4
The grains survive because zircon is chemically tough and resists weathering and remelting.
--- fact 5
No rock of comparable age is known on Earth; the grains are the only direct samples of Hadean crust.
--- context
The Jack Hills grains give the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] its only direct mineral witnesses and are used to argue about when oceans and continental crust first formed. They sit alongside the question of [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]]. Jack Hills Zircons sits in the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the planet's earliest testimony consists of a few grains smaller than a pinhead, kept only because they were hard enough to outlast everything around them. What survives is not a fair sample of what existed, a lesson that recurs in every later record.
--- question 1
Did liquid-water oceans exist by 4.3 billion years ago?
--- question 2
How much continental crust existed in the Hadean?

=== place/jamestown
title: Jamestown
type: place
date: 1607 – 1699
themes: trade, war
src: 97af00030c9e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jamestown, on the James River in Virginia, USA, was founded in 1607 as the first permanent English settlement in North America and was the capital of the Virginia colony until 1699.
--- fact 1
Jamestown was founded in May 1607 by about 105 English men and boys sent by the Virginia Company of London, on land within a paramount chiefdom of many Algonquian-speaking communities led by Wahunsenacawh, whom the English called Powhatan.
--- fact 2
In the winter of 1609 to 1610, known as the Starving Time, hunger and disease reduced the colony from a few hundred people (commonly given as about 500) to about 60, and accounts and archaeological evidence record cannibalism.
--- fact 3
John Rolfe's tobacco, first harvested successfully in 1614, gave the colony an export crop and increased the colonists' demand for land and labor.
--- fact 4
In July 1619 the first representative assembly in English America met at Jamestown; in late August an English privateer, the White Lion, landed about 20 Africans at Point Comfort, downriver, who had been taken from a Portuguese slave ship, and they were traded for food, and some were brought to Jamestown; their legal status is debated.
--- fact 5
A Powhatan attack on 22 March 1622 killed more than 300 colonists, about a third of the English population, and years of war followed; Powhatan deaths from war and introduced disease are poorly recorded.
--- fact 6
Jamestown was burned in 1676 during Bacon's Rebellion, and the capital moved to Middle Plantation, renamed Williamsburg, in 1699 after the statehouse burned in 1698.
--- fact 7
Archaeologists found the remains of the 1607 fort in 1996, after it had long been thought lost to river erosion.
--- context
Jamestown was an outpost of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] in North America, sending tobacco to Europe and receiving people, animals and diseases across the Atlantic, and the arrival of 1619 belongs to the history of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Jamestown is a place where two claims to the same land met, an English company's charter and a Powhatan chiefdom's territory, and that the settlement survived only after it found a crop that turned land and labor into exported wealth. The year 1619 is remembered for a representative assembly and for the arrival of enslaved Africans, and the Observer holds that both belong in one account, with the deaths of colonists, Powhatan people and Africans recorded as suffering. The reading would weaken if new records show that Virginia's system of hereditary slavery grew from later laws with little connection to the 1619 arrivals.
--- question 1
What was the legal status of the Africans landed in 1619, and how did Virginia's law of slavery develop afterward?
--- question 2
How many Powhatan people died from war and introduced disease in the first decades of the colony?

=== place/jericho
title: Jericho
type: place
date: c. 9000 BCE
themes: kinship, technology
src: 0bddc95619ba
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Jericho, at Tell es-Sultan in the Jordan Valley, is one of the oldest continuously inhabited settlements, with permanent occupation beginning around 9000 BCE.
--- fact 1
The site lies in the West Bank, about 250 meters below sea level, near a perennial spring.
--- fact 2
Excavations by Kathleen Kenyon in the 1950s found a stone wall and a large stone tower dated to roughly 8000 BCE.
--- fact 3
The purpose of the wall, defense against attack or protection from floods and mudflow, is debated.
--- fact 4
Houses in the later Pre-Pottery Neolithic B period contained skulls with facial features modeled in plaster, dated to around 7000 BCE.
--- fact 5
Jericho is often described as one of the world's oldest towns.
--- context
Jericho illustrates early sedentary life and stone architecture in the Levant during [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], long before the cities of [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]] in [[place/uruk|Uruk]]. Jericho sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a community built a large wall and tower, and also preserved the faces of the dead in plaster, before it had metal or writing. Defense, memory, and grief all seem to have been present at the start of settled life.
--- question 1
Was the wall for defense, flood control, or status display?

=== place/jerusalem
title: Jerusalem
type: place
date: c. 3000 BCE
themes: religion, war
src: 3a4a9a5e0ee9
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jerusalem is a city in the Judean hills of the southern Levant, settled since about the 4th millennium BCE and holy to Jews, Christians and Muslims, whose political status is disputed today.
--- fact 1
Settlement on the spur above the Gihon spring dates from about the 4th millennium BCE, and Jerusalem is named in Egyptian texts of the early 2nd millennium BCE and in the Amarna letters of the 14th century BCE.
--- fact 2
According to the Hebrew Bible, King David captured the city about 1000 BCE and his son Solomon built the First Temple; archaeologists debate the size of Jerusalem in the 10th century BCE, and the city grew markedly by the 8th century BCE.
--- fact 3
Babylonian forces destroyed Jerusalem and the First Temple in 586 BCE and deported part of its population; the Second Temple was dedicated in 516 BCE.
--- fact 4
Roman forces destroyed the city and the Second Temple in 70 CE; after the Bar Kokhba revolt (132 to 135 CE) the Romans refounded it as Aelia Capitolina and barred Jews from entering.
--- fact 5
The Church of the Holy Sepulchre was built in the 4th century under Constantine; Muslim forces took the city in 637 to 638, and the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik completed the Dome of the Rock in 691 to 692.
--- fact 6
Crusaders captured Jerusalem in 1099 and killed many of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, with estimates of the dead varying widely; Saladin retook it in 1187, and it was under Ottoman rule from 1517 to 1917.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Old City of Jerusalem and its walls in 1981. The city was divided between Israel and Jordan in 1948 and has been under Israeli control since 1967, and its status remains disputed between Israelis and Palestinians.
--- context
Jerusalem's history is tied to [[place/babylon|Babylon]] through the destruction of 586 BCE, and to the [[culture/romans|Romans]], the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], the [[event/crusades|Crusades]] and the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] through later conquests. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] (its earliest settlement) and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Jerusalem is a place where three meaning-engines, Jewish, Christian and Muslim, locate some of their most significant sites within a few hundred meters of one another, so one spot can hold the claim-lines of several communities, and builders of each period have reused or replaced earlier structures. The city's long record of sieges, destructions and massacres is recorded here as suffering, whichever community was its victim at the time. The reading would weaken if the sanctity of particular sites proves to have been less continuous over time than each tradition holds.
--- question 1
How large was Jerusalem in the 10th century BCE?
--- question 2
How did the population of Jerusalem change under successive rulers?

=== place/karakorum
title: Karakorum
type: place
date: 1235 – 1388
themes: war, trade, religion
src: db637cc3dbe0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Karakorum was the capital of the Mongol Empire in the Orkhon Valley of central Mongolia, built from 1235 under Ögedei Khan, and it was destroyed by Ming forces in 1388.
--- fact 1
Karakorum stood in the Orkhon Valley; Chinggis Khan is reported to have set up a camp there around 1220, and his son Ögedei ordered a walled city built from 1235.
--- fact 2
Craftsmen and captives from conquered lands, including China, Central Asia and Europe, were settled at Karakorum to work as artisans.
--- fact 3
William of Rubruck, a Flemish Franciscan sent by Louis IX of France, visited Karakorum in 1254 and described temples of several traditions, mosques, a Christian church, and a silver tree that dispensed drinks, made by the French goldsmith Guillaume Boucher.
--- fact 4
After Kublai Khan took power in 1260 and shifted the center of the empire to northern China, Karakorum declined.
--- fact 5
Ming forces destroyed the city in 1388; the Buddhist monastery of Erdene Zuu was built nearby from 1585, partly with stone from the ruins.
--- fact 6
The ruins of Karakorum were identified in the 19th century and excavated by a Soviet-Mongolian expedition in 1948 and 1949; UNESCO inscribed the Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape, which includes the site, in 2004.
--- context
Karakorum was the capital of the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] during the period of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], in a valley that also holds inscriptions of the earlier [[culture/gokturk-khaganate|Göktürk Khaganate]], and it was destroyed by forces of the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Karakorum was a fixed point built by a people whose power rested on mobility, a place to gather tribute, envoys and skilled captives, where the silver tree served as a status-signal aimed at foreign delegations. It was the center of the empire for only a few decades before power moved toward the taxable cities of northern China, which suggests that the capital followed the revenue and not the herds. The forced resettlement of artisans is a reminder that such a capital was built partly through sanctioned harm. The sources, mostly written by outsiders, do not settle how much the city mattered to the Mongol elite compared with the camps they moved between.
--- question 1
How large was Karakorum, and how many people lived there year-round?
--- question 2
How much does Rubruck's account, written by a visiting envoy, reflect the city as its rulers saw it?

=== place/karnak
title: Karnak
type: place
date: c. 1970 BCE
themes: religion, art, war
src: 3db99fd93e38
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Karnak is a vast temple complex at Thebes, near modern Luxor in Egypt, dedicated mainly to the god Amun-Ra and built and enlarged over about 2,000 years from the Middle Kingdom to the Ptolemaic period.
--- fact 1
Karnak's earliest surviving structures date to the reign of Senusret I, around 1970 BCE, and kings added to the complex until the Ptolemaic period.
--- fact 2
The Great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak, begun by Seti I and completed by Ramesses II in the 13th century BCE, has 134 columns in 16 rows, the tallest about 21 meters high.
--- fact 3
The walls of Karnak carry royal inscriptions, including annals of the campaigns of Thutmose III, reliefs of the Battle of Kadesh, and a relief of the campaign of Shoshenq I in Canaan.
--- fact 4
Hatshepsut's obelisk at Karnak stands about 30 meters high; images and inscriptions of Hatshepsut were later removed from many monuments, and the motives are debated.
--- fact 5
Papyri of the 12th century BCE record large estates and tens of thousands of dependents attached to the temples of Amun.
--- fact 6
Kings of the Kushite 25th Dynasty added to Karnak in the 8th and 7th centuries BCE.
--- fact 7
An avenue of sphinxes about 2.7 kilometers long linked Karnak with Luxor Temple, and its restored length was reopened in 2021; UNESCO inscribed Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis, including Karnak, in 1979.
--- context
Karnak was the religious center of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] at Thebes, on the east bank of the [[place/nile-river|Nile River]] across from the tombs of the [[place/valley-of-the-kings|Valley of the Kings]]. Its walls record the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]], and kings of [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]] added to it. Karnak sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Karnak works as an accumulating archive in stone: each ruler added buildings and cut his name and campaigns into the walls, so the temple is both a religious site and a public record of royal claims. The removal of Hatshepsut's images shows the claim-line reaching backward, since later rulers could edit the record of a predecessor, which makes the walls an official memory and not a neutral one. A temple holding large estates and dependents also shows a meaning-engine acting as an economic institution. The reading would weaken if the erasures prove to have been less deliberate or less selective than assumed.
--- question 1
Why were Hatshepsut's images removed, and when?
--- question 2
How large were the temple's estates at their height?

=== place/kilwa-kisiwani
title: Kilwa Kisiwani
type: place
date: c. 800 CE
themes: trade, religion, art
src: 046ad853f984
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kilwa Kisiwani is an island town off the coast of southern Tanzania that was a leading Swahili port from about the 12th to the 15th century, trading gold from the interior for goods carried across the Indian Ocean.
--- fact 1
Kilwa Kisiwani was occupied from about the 9th century CE, and its Great Mosque dates from the 11th or 12th century and was enlarged in the 14th.
--- fact 2
The Kilwa Chronicle, written in the 16th century, traces the ruling dynasty to a founder from Shiraz in Persia; archaeological and linguistic evidence indicates that Kilwa's society was rooted in African coastal communities that took part in Indian Ocean trade.
--- fact 3
From about the 13th century Kilwa controlled the gold trade from Sofala, in the south, whose gold came from the interior of Zimbabwe, and the town minted its own coins, among the earliest in sub-Saharan Africa.
--- fact 4
Sultan al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman built the palace and market complex of Husuni Kubwa in the early 14th century.
--- fact 5
The traveler Ibn Battuta visited Kilwa in about 1331 and described it as one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns.
--- fact 6
Portuguese forces attacked Kilwa in 1505 and built a fort; the town later came under Omani influence and was involved in the trade in enslaved people in the 18th and 19th centuries.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara in 1981.
--- context
Kilwa Kisiwani was one of the [[culture/swahili-coast-city-states|Swahili Coast City-States]] and a hub of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]]; its gold came through the trade that also supplied [[place/great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]]'s region, and the [[event/portuguese-sea-route-to-india|Portuguese Sea Route to India]] led to its attack in 1505. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kilwa's wealth came from standing between two exchange-webs, the gold of the interior and the ships of the Indian Ocean: the town made none of its main goods but profited from the position between them. The founding story of a Persian origin may have served as a status-signal within a Muslim trading world, while the archaeological evidence shows local African roots, so the story and the record point in different directions. The reading would weaken if excavation shows that Kilwa's wealth depended more on local production than on trade in transit.
--- question 1
How much did gold from Sofala contribute to Kilwa's wealth, and how did that trade decline?
--- question 2
How large was Kilwa Kisiwani's population at its height?

=== place/knossos
title: Knossos
type: place
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: art, trade, language
src: 882274382747
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Knossos is an archaeological site near Heraklion on the Greek island of Crete, occupied from about 7000 BCE and the site of the largest palace of the Minoan civilization, built from about 1900 BCE.
--- fact 1
Knossos was occupied by farmers by about 7000 BCE, and the first palace was built about 1900 BCE; an earthquake destroyed it about 1700 BCE and a larger palace was built after it.
--- fact 2
The palace held storerooms for oil, grain and wine, workshops and frescoes, including bull-leaping scenes, and clay tablets in Linear A, a script that has not been deciphered.
--- fact 3
Tablets in Linear B, which was deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952 as an early form of Greek, show that Mycenaean Greeks controlled Knossos by about 1450 BCE.
--- fact 4
The palace was destroyed by fire; the date is debated, with proposals from about 1375 to about 1200 BCE.
--- fact 5
Arthur Evans excavated Knossos from 1900 and rebuilt parts of it with concrete columns and repainted frescoes; the reconstructions are controversial among archaeologists.
--- fact 6
The name "Minoan" is a modern term drawn from the legendary King Minos, and later Greek myth placed the Minotaur and its labyrinth at Knossos; whether the palace inspired the myth is uncertain.
--- fact 7
Estimates of the population of Knossos vary widely.
--- context
Knossos was the largest palace of the [[culture/minoans|Minoans]], came under the [[culture/mycenaean-greeks|Mycenaean Greeks]], and shaped the memory of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]]; its tablets are central to the story of the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] in the Aegean. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (its earliest farming settlement) and the region [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Knossos is understood mainly through counting: tablets of stores and allotments show a palace economy that kept records of oil, grain and wine, memory-outsourcing for storage and not for storytelling, while the Minoans' own script remains unread. What visitors see is also an early 20th-century interpretation, since Evans's concrete and paint present a Bronze Age palace through his own ideas of it. The reading would weaken if new work shows that the reconstructions changed the palace's evidence less than critics claim.
--- question 1
What do the Linear A tablets say, and what language do they record?
--- question 2
How did Knossos come under Mycenaean control, and when was it finally destroyed?

=== place/kolyma
title: Kolyma
type: place
date: c. 1932 – 1957
themes: war, trade
src: 59d0e0387d18
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kolyma is a remote region of northeastern Siberia, Russia, that from 1932 until the 1950s held the Soviet Union's largest system of forced-labor camps, where prisoners mined gold and other metals in extreme cold and many died.
--- fact 1
Kolyma lies along the Kolyma River in the far northeast of Siberia, where winter temperatures fall below minus 40 degrees Celsius.
--- fact 2
Gold was found on the Kolyma River in the late 1920s, and in 1931 the Soviet state created Dalstroy, a state trust that developed the region with prisoners from the Gulag system, the first of whom arrived in 1932.
--- fact 3
Prisoners included people convicted of ordinary crimes and of political offenses, and many were sent during the Great Terror of 1937 to 1938; hundreds of thousands of people passed through the Kolyma camps.
--- fact 4
Estimates of deaths in the Kolyma camps range from more than 100,000 to several hundred thousand, from hunger, cold, disease, overwork and executions; the full toll is uncertain.
--- fact 5
Prisoners built the Kolyma Highway between Magadan and Yakutsk, known as the "Road of Bones" because of the deaths among its builders.
--- fact 6
Varlam Shalamov, who was imprisoned in the Kolyma camps, wrote the short stories known as the Kolyma Tales about them.
--- fact 7
Dalstroy was dissolved in 1957, and a memorial, the Mask of Sorrow, was unveiled in Magadan in 1996.
--- context
Kolyma was a major site of the Gulag under the Soviet state that emerged from the [[event/russian-revolution|Russian Revolution]], in a region that Russia had entered through the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]]. Much of what is known about the camps comes from survivors' writing and from archives opened after the [[event/collapse-of-the-soviet-union|Collapse of the Soviet Union]]. It sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kolyma is a case of sanctioned harm carried out through economic administration: prisoners were counted as labor for gold production, and their deaths were entered in ledgers, which the Observer records as suffering. The record of the place comes from two sources that do not always agree, survivors' writing and, after 1991, opened archives, which is why the death toll is given here as a range. The reading would weaken if fuller archival access shows that the camps served aims other than production more than this account suggests.
--- question 1
How many people died in the Kolyma camps, and how many survived?
--- question 2
How is Kolyma remembered inside Russia today?

The Kuk Early Agricultural Site in the highlands of Papua New Guinea preserves drainage ditches and gardens showing that people cultivated wetlands there for at least 7,000 years.

Kuk Early Agricultural Site sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/technology|Technology]].

=== place/kyoto
title: Kyoto
type: place
date: 794 CE – 1868
themes: art, religion, war
src: c033fcbe568d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kyoto is a city on the Japanese island of Honshu, founded in 794 CE as the imperial capital Heian-kyō and the seat of the emperor until 1868.
--- fact 1
Kyoto was founded in 794 by Emperor Kanmu as Heian-kyō, on a grid plan modeled on Chinese capitals such as Chang'an.
--- fact 2
Under the Heian court (794 to 1185) Kyoto was the center of court culture, including the novel The Tale of Genji, written in the early 11th century by Murasaki Shikibu.
--- fact 3
The Ashikaga shogunate ruled from Kyoto from 1336 to 1573, and the Onin War (1467 to 1477) destroyed much of the city.
--- fact 4
Under the Tokugawa shogunate (1603 to 1868) political power lay in Edo, while Kyoto remained the imperial seat and a center of crafts and Buddhist institutions.
--- fact 5
The emperor moved to Tokyo (formerly Edo) in 1868 to 1869 after the Meiji Restoration, ending Kyoto's role as capital.
--- fact 6
In 1945 Kyoto was removed from the list of possible atomic-bomb targets at the urging of US Secretary of War Henry Stimson, who cited its cultural significance.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed 17 "Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto" in 1994.
--- context
Kyoto's plan followed [[place/chang-an|Chang'an]], and the city succeeded [[place/nara|Nara]] as the imperial capital. It remained the imperial seat under the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] and ceased to be the capital with the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kyoto held an imperial house for more than a thousand years while, for much of that time, armies and administrations answered to shoguns elsewhere, so the story-glue of legitimacy and the practical power to govern sat in different places. A city laid out from a foreign template was maintained through fires and wars as a store of court culture, and its survival in 1945 shows that a city can be spared because of what its buildings hold, in a war that destroyed others. This reading would weaken if the decision not to bomb Kyoto rested mainly on operational reasons and not on cultural ones.
--- question 1
How much of Kyoto's original Heian layout survives in the modern street plan?
--- question 2
How far did cultural reasons, compared with military ones, decide Kyoto's removal from the 1945 target list?

=== place/l-anse-aux-meadows
title: L'Anse aux Meadows
type: place
date: c. 1000 – 1030
themes: trade, technology
src: 3e691fc2e0fe
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
L'Anse aux Meadows is an archaeological site on the northern tip of Newfoundland, Canada, where Norse people from Greenland built a small settlement of sod buildings around 1000 CE, the only widely accepted Norse site in North America.
--- fact 1
L'Anse aux Meadows lies at the northernmost tip of Newfoundland; Helge and Anne Stine Ingstad found it in 1960 with the help of a local resident, George Decker, and excavated it in the 1960s.
--- fact 2
The site has eight buildings of sod over wooden frames, including an iron smithy, a carpentry workshop and a boat-repair area, and more than 800 Norse objects were found.
--- fact 3
Radiocarbon dates place activity between about 990 and 1050 CE; a 2021 study of tree rings, using a solar-storm signal in the year 993, dated felled timber cut with metal tools to 1021 CE.
--- fact 4
The occupation was short, perhaps about 20 years, though this is debated, and no burials or signs of farming were found, so the site appears to have been left in an orderly way; the date_end of 1030 is a rough placeholder.
--- fact 5
Butternuts found at the site grow farther south, so Norse travelers reached or traded with regions beyond Newfoundland.
--- fact 6
The Icelandic sagas, written down in the 13th century, describe voyages to a land called Vinland, and whether L'Anse aux Meadows is that place or a base for voyages farther south is debated.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed L'Anse aux Meadows in 1978.
--- context
L'Anse aux Meadows records a voyage by the [[culture/norse|Norse]] that reached North America about five centuries before the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], long after the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that here a story-glue, the sagas, and a physical record, the sod buildings and iron working, were long treated as separate until the Ingstads matched them. The site shows a link between two hemispheres that did not become an exchange-web, because no lasting settlement, herds or diseases were left to change either side. The sagas describe meetings with the region's Indigenous people, and how those meetings went is known only from the Norse side. The reading would weaken if evidence shows that contact between the Norse and northeastern peoples was more frequent and lasting than a short-lived camp suggests.
--- question 1
Was L'Anse aux Meadows the Vinland of the sagas, or a base for voyages farther south?
--- question 2

=== place/lake-baikal
title: Lake Baikal
type: place
date: c. 5000 BCE
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 3b75af4fa14d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Lake Baikal is a rift lake in southern Siberia, Russia, the deepest and oldest lake on Earth and the largest by volume of fresh water, with people living around it for many thousands of years and a UNESCO listing since 1996.
--- fact 1
Lake Baikal lies in a rift valley, is about 636 kilometers long, has a maximum depth of 1,642 meters, and holds about 20 percent of the world's unfrozen surface fresh water.
--- fact 2
The Baikal rift basin is estimated to be 25 to 30 million years old, which makes the lake the oldest on Earth; this geologic age is far older than any human presence.
--- fact 3
More than 1,700 species of plants and animals live in and around Lake Baikal, about two thirds of them found nowhere else, including the Baikal seal, a freshwater seal.
--- fact 4
Hunter-fisher-gatherers of the Kitoi culture buried their dead around the lake from about the 5th millennium BCE, and much older Paleolithic sites, such as Mal'ta, lie nearby.
--- fact 5
Russian explorers reached Lake Baikal in the 1640s, beginning Russian settlement among the Buryat and Evenki peoples.
--- fact 6
A pulp and paper mill on the southern shore discharged waste into the lake from 1966 until it closed in 2013, and it was one of the earliest targets of organized environmental protest in the Soviet Union.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Lake Baikal in 1996.
--- context
Lake Baikal lies near the Paleolithic site linked to [[culture/mal-ta-and-the-ancient-north-eurasians|Mal'ta and the Ancient North Eurasians]], and its shores were reached by the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] in the 17th century. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (its earliest Holocene burials) and the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Lake Baikal is valued both as a store of an unusual biota and as a resource, that both valuations have shaped policy and the state of the lake, and that the balance between them has shifted with the political era. A lake that took millions of years to form and holds a fifth of the planet's unfrozen fresh water could be altered by one industrial plant within a few decades, which shows the mismatch between geological and human timescales. The reading would weaken if measurements show that the mill's effect on the lake as a whole was small compared with natural variation.
--- question 1
How much lasting change did the pulp mill cause in the lake's ecosystem?
--- question 2
How did people live around Lake Baikal in the Neolithic, and how did they treat their dead?

=== place/london
title: London
type: place
date: c. 47 CE

observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
London is the capital of the United Kingdom, on the Thames River, founded as the Roman town of Londinium in about 47 CE and grown into a leading center of trade, finance and empire and, in the 19th century, the largest city in the world.
--- fact 1
Londinium was founded by the Romans in about 47 CE near a crossing of the Thames; Boudica's revolt destroyed it in 60 or 61, and it later became the capital of Roman Britain, until Roman rule ended around 410.
--- fact 2
William I was crowned at Westminster in 1066 and began the Tower of London in about 1078.
--- fact 3
The Black Death of 1348 to 1349 killed a large share of London's people, and the Great Plague of 1665 killed tens of thousands; the Great Fire of 1666 destroyed about 13,000 houses and 87 parish churches.
--- fact 4
London was probably the largest city in the world from the 1830s to the 1920s, and the world's first underground railway opened there in 1863.
--- fact 5
During the 1854 cholera outbreak in Soho, John Snow mapped deaths by address and linked them to a water pump; the "Great Stink" of 1858 led to the building of a sewer system by Joseph Bazalgette.
--- fact 6
London was a major center of shipping, insurance and finance for the Atlantic slave trade and for British colonial trade, and the capital of the British Empire.
--- fact 7
The German air raids of 1940 to 1941 killed about 20,000 Londoners or more.
--- context
London began as a town of the [[culture/romans|Romans]], lost people to the [[event/black-death|Black Death]], grew with the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], served as the imperial center of the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]] and other territories, and was bombed in [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that London was founded as a bridge town for a foreign state's administration and grew into a hub of an exchange-web that spanned oceans, and that its wealth included profits from the sale and transport of enslaved people, which the Observer records as suffering. The Snow map of 1854 shows a city becoming an object of pattern-taming: tabulating deaths by address led to the identification of a source of disease, a method later used across public health. The reading would weaken if the record shows that London's global role rested more on later trade than on the earlier ones it emphasizes.
