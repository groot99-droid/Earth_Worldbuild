=== era/neoproterozoic-era
title: Neoproterozoic Era
type: era
date: 1 Ga – 538.8 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 8a36d0ac861f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Neoproterozoic Era is the last era of the Proterozoic Eon, running from 1.0 billion to 538.8 million years ago. It holds the break-up of the supercontinent Rodinia, the global glaciations known as Snowball Earth, and the first large complex organisms.
--- fact 1
The Neoproterozoic Era spans 1.0 billion to 538.8 million years ago and has three periods: the [[era/tonian-period|Tonian Period]], the [[era/cryogenian-period|Cryogenian Period]] and the [[era/ediacaran-period|Ediacaran Period]]. Its top is the base of the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]].
--- fact 2
The supercontinent Rodinia, assembled by about 1.0 billion years ago, began to rift apart roughly 800 to 750 million years ago.
--- fact 3
The Cryogenian glaciations, the Sturtian (about 717 to 660 million years ago) and the Marinoan (about 650 to 635 million years ago), are the basis of the [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]] hypothesis.
--- fact 4
Oxygen is proposed to have risen again sometime between about 800 and 540 million years ago; its timing and size are debated.
--- fact 5
Molecular-clock estimates place the origin of animals in or before the Cryogenian, but the fossil record of the first animals is disputed. The [[species/ediacaran-biota|Ediacaran Biota]], large soft-bodied organisms, appear by about 575 million years ago.
--- context
The Neoproterozoic Era follows the [[era/mesoproterozoic-era|Mesoproterozoic Era]] and closes the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]], giving way to the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]] at the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]]. It belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
Animals arrive at the end of the Neoproterozoic, and it is tempting to read the ice, the oxygen and the break-up of Rodinia as steps toward them. The Observer declines that reading: these were physical events with their own causes, and animals were one of several possible outcomes. Evidence that any one of them was strictly required for animals would weaken this view.
--- question 1
How much did the Cryogenian ice and the second oxygen rise contribute to the appearance of animals, if at all?
--- question 2
When did the first animals appear?

=== era/ordovician-period
title: Ordovician Period
type: era
date: 485.4 – 443.8 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 9cfbb46d4926
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Ordovician Period, from 485.4 to 443.8 million years ago, is the second period of the Paleozoic Era. It is the interval of a great rise in marine biodiversity, the first evidence of plants on land, and, at its close, the first of the "Big Five" mass extinctions.
--- fact 1
The Ordovician Period spans 485.4 to 443.8 million years ago. Its base is defined at Green Point, Newfoundland, by the conodont Iapetognathus fluctivagus, and its top at Dob's Linn in Scotland by a graptolite.
--- fact 2
The Great Ordovician Biodiversification, running for tens of millions of years from the early period, greatly increased the diversity of marine animals.
--- fact 3
Sea level was very high, and warm shallow seas held reefs, brachiopods, trilobites, crinoids and graptolites. Straight-shelled nautiloid cephalopods, some several metres long, were large predators.
--- fact 4
The earliest evidence of land plants, spores about 470 million years old, comes from this period; see [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]].
--- fact 5
The climate cooled late in the period, and an ice age centred on Gondwana set in during the Hirnantian. The [[event/late-ordovician-extinction|Late Ordovician Extinction]], about 445 to 443 million years ago, removed an estimated 85 percent of species, mostly marine.
--- context
The Ordovician Period follows the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]] and forms part of the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]]; the [[era/silurian-period|Silurian Period]] follows it. It belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer records that the Ordovician holds both a great rise in marine diversity and, near its end, a mass extinction linked to glaciation. Both arose from shifting planetary conditions, and neither was directed at any outcome. A demonstration that diversity rose steadily whatever the climate did would weaken the reading that a biosphere's history is the sum of many pushes, not a trend.
--- question 1
What drove the Great Ordovician Biodiversification: climate, ocean chemistry, ecological change, or some combination?
--- question 2
Why did glaciation set in when carbon dioxide is estimated to have been so high?

=== era/paleogene-period
title: Paleogene Period
type: era
date: 66 – 23.03 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 409dab2489dc
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Paleogene Period, from 66 to 23.03 million years ago, is the first period of the Cenozoic Era. It is the interval in which mammals expanded after the end-Cretaceous extinction and the world moved from a warm, largely ice-free state toward a cold one with polar ice.
--- fact 1
The Paleogene Period spans 66.0 to 23.03 million years ago and has three epochs: the Paleocene, Eocene and Oligocene. Its base, the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]] boundary, is defined at El Kef in Tunisia, and its top, the base of the Neogene, at Lemme-Carrosio in Italy.
--- fact 2
Mammals diversified rapidly into roles left empty by the extinction, and the earliest primates and their close relatives appear. Whales evolved from land-dwelling hoofed mammals over about ten million years, from about 50 to 40 million years ago.
--- fact 3
The [[event/paleocene-eocene-thermal-maximum|Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum]], about 56 million years ago, warmed the planet by roughly 5 to 6 degrees Celsius for about 200,000 years, and the early Eocene was among the warmest intervals of the Cenozoic.
--- fact 4
The [[event/collision-of-india-and-asia|Collision of India and Asia]], beginning about 50 million years ago, started to raise the Himalaya.
--- fact 5
About 34 million years ago, at the Eocene-Oligocene boundary, the climate cooled sharply and ice sheets began to grow on Antarctica; falling carbon dioxide and the opening of Southern Ocean gateways are proposed causes.
--- context
The Paleogene Period follows the [[era/cretaceous-period|Cretaceous Period]] and opens the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]]; the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] comes next. It belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Paleogene runs from a vacated world, in which mammals expanded into the roles the extinction left empty, to a cooling one, in which polar ice took hold. Primates, the group that includes humans, are one of many lineages in this expansion, and nothing in the record marks them as special at the time. The reading would weaken if primates could be shown to have been headed for prominence independently of the extinction.
--- question 1
Was Purgatorius, from just after the extinction, a true primate or a close relative of primates?
--- question 2
How much did the opening of Southern Ocean gateways, as against falling carbon dioxide, contribute to the Eocene-Oligocene cooling?

=== era/paleolithic
title: Paleolithic
type: era
date: c. 2.6 Ma – 9600 BCE
themes: technology, kinship, art
src: 70f7dd7510e6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Paleolithic (Old Stone Age) is the long era of chipped-stone tools and foraging societies, from about 2.6 million years ago to the end of the last ice age.
--- fact 1
The earliest known stone tools date to about 3.3 million years ago (Lomekwi) and 2.6 million years ago (Oldowan); see [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]].
--- fact 2
Fire control, [[event/control-of-fire|Control of Fire]], appears well before the end of the era.
--- fact 3
Modern humans spread across the globe; see [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]].
--- fact 4
Symbolic behavior, including [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]], appears in its later stages.
--- fact 5
It is about 99 percent of the time humans and their ancestors have made tools.
--- context
The Paleolithic sits inside the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and ends with the shift to farming known as the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that for roughly 99 percent of the tool-making record the species lived as small mobile groups. Everything called 'civilization' is a recent variation on a very old pattern.
--- question 1
How large and how interconnected were Paleolithic social networks?

=== era/paleoproterozoic-era
title: Paleoproterozoic Era
type: era
date: 2.5 – 1.6 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: 97bc618d647e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Paleoproterozoic Era is the first era of the Proterozoic Eon, running from 2.5 billion to 1.6 billion years ago. It is the era in which free oxygen first became a lasting part of the atmosphere.
--- fact 1
The Paleoproterozoic Era spans 2.5 to 1.6 billion years ago. Its boundaries, and those of its four periods (Siderian, Rhyacian, Orosirian and Statherian), are set by agreed numerical ages rather than by markers in rock.
--- fact 2
The [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]], the first sustained rise of free oxygen from the photosynthesis of [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]], began around 2.4 billion years ago; estimates for its onset run from about 2.46 to 2.32 billion years ago.
--- fact 3
The Huronian glaciation of Ontario, Canada, roughly 2.4 to 2.2 billion years ago, is often linked to the oxygen rise, but that link is a hypothesis.
--- fact 4
Banded iron formations, a major source of the world's iron ore, were deposited widely between about 2.5 and 1.8 billion years ago and then largely disappear from the record until the Cryogenian.
--- fact 5
The oldest unambiguous eukaryote fossils, about 1.65 billion years old, fall in this era; older candidates such as Grypania (about 1.87 billion years) are debated. See [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]].
--- context
The Paleoproterozoic Era follows the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and is followed by the [[era/mesoproterozoic-era|Mesoproterozoic Era]] within the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]]. It belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], and its oxygen is part of the chemical background for the later [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]].
--- observer
The Observer files the Paleoproterozoic under preconditions. The oxygen that later animals breathe first accumulated here as a waste product of microbial metabolism, poisonous to much of the life then present, and nothing in the record shows it was directed at any outcome. The reading would weaken if oxygen-producing photosynthesis proved a near-certain result of life on any wet planet.
--- question 1
When did oxygen-producing photosynthesis begin, and how long did oxygen stay chemically absorbed before it reached the air?
--- question 2
Which of the era's ice ages and iron formations follow from the oxygen rise, and which had other causes?

=== era/paleozoic-era
title: Paleozoic Era
type: era
date: 538.8 – 251.9 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: f292029d8d8c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Paleozoic Era (538.8 to 251.9 million years ago) saw the rise of animals with hard parts, the colonization of land, forests, and the first tetrapods. It ends with the largest mass extinction in Earth's history.
--- fact 1
Periods: Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, Permian.
--- fact 2
Plants and animals moved onto land; see [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]].
--- fact 3
Major extinctions include [[event/late-ordovician-extinction|Late Ordovician Extinction]], [[event/late-devonian-extinction|Late Devonian Extinction]], and the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]].
--- fact 4
Trilobites, one of the era's signature animals, are described in [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]].
--- context
The Paleozoic opens with the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]] and closes with the end-Permian collapse, which cleared the way for the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the Paleozoic as the era when life became a planetary system: it began building soil, forests, and oxygen-rich air that later species inherited.
--- question 1
What ultimately drove the end-Permian extinction's severity?

=== era/permian-period
title: Permian Period
type: era
date: 298.9 – 251.9 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 6f81abf2fde7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Permian Period, from 298.9 to 251.9 million years ago, is the last period of the Paleozoic Era. It is the interval of the fully assembled supercontinent Pangaea and of mammal-line reptiles dominating the land, and it ends with the largest mass extinction in Earth's history.
--- fact 1
The Permian Period spans 298.9 to 251.9 million years ago and is named after the Perm region of Russia. Its base is defined at Aidaralash Creek in Kazakhstan, and its top, the base of the Triassic, at Meishan in China.
--- fact 2
Pangaea was fully assembled and surrounded by a single ocean, Panthalassa. The continental interior was dry and strongly seasonal, and deserts were widespread.
--- fact 3
Synapsids, the group that includes mammals and their extinct relatives, dominated land vertebrate life. Early forms such as Dimetrodon, closer to mammals than to dinosaurs, were followed by the more mammal-like therapsids.
--- fact 4
Conifers and other seed plants replaced much of the club-moss coal forest, and the seed fern Glossopteris dominated the southern continent Gondwana.
--- fact 5
The [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]], about 251.9 million years ago, killed an estimated 80 to 96 percent of marine species, and the leading proposed cause is the eruption of the Siberian Traps.
--- context
The Permian Period follows the [[era/carboniferous-period|Carboniferous Period]] and is followed by the [[era/triassic-period|Triassic Period]], which opens the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]]. It closes the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]] and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the line leading to mammals was already the dominant group of land vertebrates in the Permian, and that most of that group did not survive the period's end. The later dominance of other lineages was a result of who survived a catastrophe, which the Observer reads as contingency. The reading would weaken if the survivors could be shown to have held an advantage predictable in advance.
--- question 1
Which effect of the Siberian Traps eruptions (warming, ocean acidification, oxygen loss, or toxic release) was the most lethal?
--- question 2
How did the survivors of the end-Permian extinction differ from its victims?

=== era/pleistocene-epoch
title: Pleistocene Epoch
type: era
date: c. 2.58 Ma – 9700 BCE
themes: earth-systems, science
src: b26ed42edd21
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Pleistocene Epoch (about 2.58 million to 11,700 years ago) is the age of recurring ice sheets. The genus Homo evolved and spread during it.
--- fact 1
Glacial and interglacial cycles repeated many times; see [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]].
--- fact 2
Homo habilis, Homo erectus, Neanderthals, Denisovans and Homo sapiens all lived in this epoch; see [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]] and [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]].
--- fact 3
Sea levels fell during glacial periods, exposing land bridges such as the one used in the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]].
--- fact 4
Many large mammals, including [[species/woolly-mammoth|Woolly Mammoth]], went extinct near its end.
--- context
The Pleistocene is the geological frame for the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]]. It ends when the climate warms into the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the Pleistocene as an evolutionary pressure chamber: repeated climate swings favored a species that could adapt by learning and by copying others, rather than by waiting for genes to change.
--- question 1
How much did climate variability drive the growth of hominin brains?

=== era/proterozoic-eon
title: Proterozoic Eon
type: era
date: 2.5 Ga – 538.8 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: e9ab1628d53b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Proterozoic Eon (2.5 billion to 538.8 million years ago) is the age of oxygenation, complex cells, global glaciations, and the first multicellular animals.
--- fact 1
The [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] raised atmospheric oxygen about 2.4 billion years ago.
--- fact 2
Eukaryotes (cells with nuclei) appear in the fossil record during this eon.
--- fact 3
The 'Snowball Earth' glaciations occurred late in the eon; see [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]].
--- fact 4
The Ediacaran biota, early large multicellular organisms, appear in its final period.
--- context
The Proterozoic covers nearly half of Earth's history. It ends at the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]] and the start of the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how long the planet waited: about two billion years passed between the first oxygen and the first animals. Timescales like this make human history look like the last few seconds of a very long film.
--- question 1
Why did complex animals take so long to appear?

=== era/quaternary-period
title: Quaternary Period
type: era
date: 2.58 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 0ffe270bf5ce
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Quaternary Period is the latest period of the Cenozoic Era, beginning 2.58 million years ago and continuing today. It is the interval of the repeated ice ages, of the genus Homo, and of all recorded human history.
--- fact 1
The Quaternary Period began 2.58 million years ago and has two epochs, the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]]. Its base is defined at Monte San Nicola in Sicily, and its status as a period, once disputed, was confirmed in 2009.
--- fact 2
Ice sheets grew and shrank across the Northern Hemisphere in cycles about 41,000 years long at first and about 100,000 years long after a shift in the middle Pleistocene, roughly 1.25 to 0.7 million years ago.
--- fact 3
At the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]], sea level was about 120 metres lower than today, and land bridges such as [[place/beringia|Beringia]] were exposed.
--- fact 4
Nearly all species of the genus Homo lived in this period, including [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]], [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]], [[species/denisovans|Denisovans]] and [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]], and many large mammals disappeared; see [[event/quaternary-megafauna-extinctions|Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions]].
--- fact 5
A proposal to add an Anthropocene epoch was rejected by the International Union of Geological Sciences in 2024; the term remains in informal use. See [[era/anthropocene|Anthropocene]].
--- context
The Quaternary Period follows the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] and is the last period of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] so far. It belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and is the frame for the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and everything after it.
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Quaternary is under three million years long, about 0.06 percent of Earth's history, yet it holds nearly all of the record of the genus Homo and every human society. The Observer reads the period's climate cycling as the setting in which a species that learns by copying could do well, one hypothesis among several. The reading would weaken if climate variability had little effect on hominin evolution.
--- question 1
Why did the glacial cycles shift from a 41,000-year rhythm to a 100,000-year rhythm?
--- question 2
Would the current interglacial have ended in a new glaciation without human carbon emissions?

=== era/silurian-period
title: Silurian Period
type: era
date: 443.8 – 419.2 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 9c8737d14641
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Silurian Period, from 443.8 to 419.2 million years ago, is the third period of the Paleozoic Era. It follows the Late Ordovician extinction and is the interval of the oldest known vascular land plants and the first well-preserved jawed fishes.
--- fact 1
The Silurian Period spans 443.8 to 419.2 million years ago. Its base is defined at Dob's Linn in Scotland, and its top, the base of the Devonian, at Klonk in the Czech Republic, both by graptolites.
--- fact 2
Marine life recovered after the [[event/late-ordovician-extinction|Late Ordovician Extinction]]. Sea level rose as the Hirnantian ice sheets melted, and reefs of corals and stromatoporoids spread in warm shallow seas.
--- fact 3
Cooksonia, a small leafless plant with spore capsules at the tips of its branching stems, is the oldest widely accepted vascular land plant, known from about 433 million years ago.
--- fact 4
The oldest well-preserved jawed vertebrates, including armoured placoderms and the early bony fish Guiyu (about 425 million years old, from China), date to the later Silurian.
--- fact 5
The millipede Pneumodesmus from Scotland, about 428 million years old, has been reported as the oldest known air-breathing land animal.
--- fact 6
The Iapetus Ocean closed as Laurentia, Baltica and Avalonia collided, building the northern continent Laurussia by the early Devonian.
--- context
The Silurian Period follows the [[era/ordovician-period|Ordovician Period]] and is followed by the [[era/devonian-period|Devonian Period]] within the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]]. It belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], and its plants and arthropods are early parts of [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Silurian is when land life first leaves more than scattered traces: small leafless plants only centimetres tall, and small arthropods. Nothing in this record marks them as the beginning of forests and land vertebrates, and the Observer treats that as a caution against reading any early stage in the light of what came after.
--- question 1
How much earlier than Cooksonia were plants living on land, given that fossil spores are older than the oldest plant bodies?
--- question 2
Did jawed fishes arise earlier than their fossil record shows?

=== era/tonian-period
title: Tonian Period
type: era
date: 1 Ga – 720 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: f15c69eb546c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Tonian Period is the first period of the Neoproterozoic Era, running from 1.0 billion to 720 million years ago. It covers the break-up of the supercontinent Rodinia and the diversification of eukaryotes before the Cryogenian ice ages.
--- fact 1
The Tonian Period spans 1.0 billion to 720 million years ago. Its base and its top are both set by numerical ages, and the top falls close to the onset of the first Cryogenian glaciation.
--- fact 2
The supercontinent Rodinia began to rift apart during the Tonian, roughly 800 to 750 million years ago.
--- fact 3
Eukaryotic microfossils show growing diversity. Vase-shaped microfossils in the Chuar Group of the Grand Canyon, roughly 780 to 740 million years old, are interpreted as the shells of amoeba-like protists.
--- fact 4
Phosphatic scale microfossils from the Fifteenmile Group of Yukon, Canada, reported from rocks roughly 810 to 720 million years old, are among the earliest evidence of eukaryotes forming mineral parts.
--- fact 5
The environmental record becomes more variable toward the end of the period, with proposed rises in oxygen; possible glacial deposits near 750 million years ago are debated.
--- context
The Tonian Period follows the [[era/mesoproterozoic-era|Mesoproterozoic Era]] and is followed by the [[era/cryogenian-period|Cryogenian Period]] within the [[era/neoproterozoic-era|Neoproterozoic Era]]. The Tonian Period belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer reads the Tonian as a period of loosening: a supercontinent starting to split, a climate record becoming less steady, and protists diversifying into new niches. None of this points at animals, but it is the background against which the later ice ages and the first large bodies happened. The reading would weaken if Tonian records showed an environment as steady as the Mesoproterozoic before it.
--- question 1
Did the break-up of Rodinia help trigger the Cryogenian glaciations?
--- question 2
How diverse were Tonian eukaryotes, given how sparse the record is?

=== era/triassic-period
title: Triassic Period
type: era
date: 251.9 – 201.4 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 9cc1f9c40db4
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Triassic Period, from 251.9 to 201.4 million years ago, is the first period of the Mesozoic Era. It is the interval of recovery after the end-Permian extinction and the interval in which dinosaurs, pterosaurs and the first mammals appear.
--- fact 1
The Triassic Period spans 251.9 to 201.4 million years ago. Its base is defined at Meishan in China, and its top, the base of the Jurassic, at Kuhjoch in Austria.
--- fact 2
Recovery from the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]] took roughly five to ten million years. Lystrosaurus, a survivor, was among the most abundant land vertebrates of the Early Triassic. The climate was generally hot, with no permanent polar ice and a dry Pangaean interior.
--- fact 3
Archosaurs diversified. The oldest dinosaurs date to about 243 to 233 million years ago, but for the rest of the period they were one group among many, alongside the relatives of crocodiles that dominated most land ecosystems. Pterosaurs appear by about 215 million years ago, and ichthyosaurs and other reptiles returned to the sea.
--- fact 4
The earliest mammals and their close relatives appear in the Late Triassic, roughly 225 to 205 million years ago; the date depends on how mammals are defined.
--- fact 5
The [[event/triassic-jurassic-extinction|Triassic-Jurassic Extinction]], about 201.4 million years ago, coincides with the eruption of the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province and the start of the [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]], and removed an estimated 80 percent of species.
--- context
The Triassic Period follows the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]] and is followed by the [[era/jurassic-period|Jurassic Period]] within the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]]. It belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]]; see [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]] for the group whose first members appear here.
--- observer
The Observer notes that dinosaurs and mammals both first appear in the Triassic, yet neither dominated until later. Dinosaurs were one lineage among several for about 30 million years, and rose to dominance only after an extinction removed many of their rivals. The Observer reads this as timing and opportunity, and the reading would weaken if anatomy alone explained why dinosaurs filled the gap.
--- question 1
How much did climate, and how much competition, decide which Triassic archosaurs survived?
--- question 2
When did the first true mammals appear, given that the definition of mammal moves the date by tens of millions of years?

=== era/upper-paleolithic
title: Upper Paleolithic
type: era
date: c. 50 ka – 10000 BCE
themes: art, technology, kinship
src: 27f6f106306b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Upper Paleolithic is the last part of the Paleolithic, from about 50,000 to about 10,000 BCE. It is the interval of blade tools, bone and antler technology, abundant art and ornament, and the spread of Homo sapiens across the globe.
--- fact 1
The Upper Paleolithic runs from about 50,000 to about 10,000 BCE, ending with the warming at the close of the last ice age (Africa uses the name Later Stone Age). Toolmakers made long blades and worked bone, antler and ivory; eyed bone needles from Denisova Cave in Siberia, about 40,000 to 50,000 years old, suggest sewn clothing.
--- fact 2
Homo sapiens spread across the globe: Sahul by about 50,000 years ago ([[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]]), Europe by about 45,000 years ago, Beringia by about 30,000 years ago ([[place/beringia|Beringia]]), and the Americas by at least about 23,000 years ago ([[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]]). Neanderthals disappear from Europe about 40,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Painted, carved and engraved images are abundant: [[place/chauvet-cave|Chauvet Cave]] in France has paintings about 36,000 years old, and cave paintings in Sulawesi, Indonesia, include a narrative scene about 51,000 years old.- The [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]], about 26,500 to 19,000 years ago, put ice over much of northern Eurasia and North America. Pottery appears in East Asia by about 20,000 years ago ([[technology/pottery|Pottery]]), and dogs were domesticated from wolves by at least about 15,000 years ago.
--- context
The Upper Paleolithic follows the [[era/middle-paleolithic|Middle Paleolithic]] and is followed by the [[era/epipaleolithic|Epipaleolithic]] in the Near East, within the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]]. It belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]]. See also [[event/quaternary-megafauna-extinctions|Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Upper Paleolithic sites hold the first large body of objects that seem made for display, ritual or play. It treats beads and ornaments as possible status-signals and burials as possible grief-rites, and it keeps in mind that a practice cannot be read from a surviving object alone. The reading would weaken if comparable objects proved common long before this period, which would make it a change in what survives, not in behaviour.
--- question 1
Is the apparent burst of art and ornament real, or a result of better preservation and more sites?
--- question 2
How did the Upper Paleolithic industries relate to specific human populations?

=== region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands
title: Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands
type: region
date: 
themes: earth-systems, kinship, technology
src: a59eda4f44e0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands are the forests, river systems, and savannas east of the Andes, home to Indigenous societies whose scale and complexity are being rediscovered through aerial laser survey.
--- fact 1
Lidar surveys over the past decade have revealed large networks of earthworks, roads, and settlements beneath the forest; see [[culture/casarabe-culture|Casarabe Culture]], [[place/upano-valley-settlements|Upano Valley Settlements]], and [[place/xingu-garden-cities|Xingu Garden Cities]].
--- fact 2
Indigenous land management, including soil enrichment, shaped much of the forest; see [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]]. Scientists dispute whether the dark earths (terra preta) were largely made by people or arose from natural processes that people then used.
--- fact 3
European contact after 1492 brought severe population loss from epidemic disease and forced labor. It is debated whether this caused widespread forest regrowth: a 2021 study of 39 fossil pollen records found no synchronized regrowth after European arrival, and found that at most sites land abandonment and regrowth began about 300 to 600 years earlier. The researchers Bush and McMichael argue that populations probably peaked and declined well before 1492. Alexander Koch and colleagues, who proposed the regrowth hypothesis for the Americas as a whole, reply that Amazonia may have been less affected than Mexico, Central America, and the Andes.
--- fact 4
The region is the setting of the [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]] and, at its edges, of many peoples never ruled by the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]].
--- context
The lowlands sit east of the [[region/andes|Andes]] and were linked to them by trade. They were transformed by the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how the phrase 'untouched wilderness' can hide a history of large human societies. What looks like nature can be the product of centuries of deliberate management.
--- question 1
How many people lived in the Amazon before 1500? Published estimates run from about 1 to 2 million up to 8 to 10 million, because little documentation exists and the estimation methods give widely varying results.
