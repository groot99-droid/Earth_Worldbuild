=== technology/sanitation-and-sewers
title: Sanitation and Sewers
type: technology
date: c. 2600 BCE
themes: technology, science
src: 639829f572a4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sanitation and sewers are systems that carry waste water and human waste away from where people live. The cities of the Indus Valley Civilization had drains and wells by about 2600 BCE, and urban sanitation was later tied to public health science in the 19th century.
--- fact 1
Cities of the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], such as [[place/mohenjo-daro|Mohenjo-daro]], had planned streets, brick-lined drains, and wells from about 2600 BCE.
--- fact 2
Minoan palaces such as [[place/knossos|Knossos]] on Crete had drainage channels and clay pipes in the 2nd millennium BCE.
--- fact 3
The Cloaca Maxima, the great drain of [[place/rome|Rome]], is traditionally dated to the 6th century BCE. It was rebuilt several times, and parts of it survive.
--- fact 4
In 1854 John Snow traced a cholera outbreak in Soho, London, to a water pump on Broad Street, an early argument that disease could travel in water.
--- fact 5
London's main sewer system, designed by Joseph Bazalgette, was approved in 1858 after the Great Stink and largely completed by 1875.
--- context
Sanitation links the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] cities of [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] to the public health projects of the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/science|Science]]. The [[technology/aqueduct|Aqueduct]], by contrast, brought clean water in.
--- observer
The Observer notes that sanitation is a technology whose success is invisible: when it works, nothing happens. Indus and Roman cities built drains long before anyone could name the diseases they prevented, which shows the tool-lineage running ahead of pattern-taming, until mid-19th-century germ research supplied an explanation. The reading would weaken if the Bronze Age drains proved to be mainly for rainwater and display rather than health.
--- question 1
Were the Indus drains built for health, for rainwater, or for prestige?
--- question 2
Why were sewer systems so often neglected after conquest or collapse?

=== technology/seismoscope
title: Seismoscope
type: technology
date: 132 CE
themes: science, technology
src: 9000860f8706
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A bronze device credited to the Han-dynasty court astronomer Zhang Heng, said to detect the direction of distant earthquakes by dropping a ball from a dragon's mouth into a toad below.
--- fact 1
Zhang Heng (78-139 CE), an Eastern Han court astronomer, is credited with presenting the device, the Houfeng Didong Yi, in 132 CE.
--- fact 2
It is described as a bronze vessel about 2 meters across, ringed by eight dragon heads each holding a ball above a bronze toad.
--- fact 3
The only detailed account survives in the Hou Hanshu, compiled by Fan Ye in the 5th century CE, about three centuries after Zhang Heng's death.
--- fact 4
No original device or contemporary image survives; everything known rests on that later text.
--- fact 5
Reconstructions since the early 1900s disagree on the internal trigger mechanism, and some historians doubt the device worked as described.
--- fact 6
It is often called the earliest known seismoscope, though this rests on one textual source, not a surviving artifact.
--- context
The device belongs to the record of the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and, like other Han inventions, survives only through later text rather than a surviving object. Threads: [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer reads this as pattern-taming turned on seismic events, curiosity channeled into an instrument that doubled as a display of state competence. Because it survives only through memory-outsourcing, a single later chronicle rather than an artifact, the reading weakens if that chronicle proved embellished. An alternative reading treats the account as a legendary origin story for divination instruments rather than a literal specification.
--- question 1
Did the device use a pendulum trigger, as most reconstructions assume?
--- question 2
Does 132 CE mark the device's invention or a later refinement?

=== technology/sericulture
title: Sericulture
type: technology
date: c. 2700 BCE
themes: technology, trade
src: 50e0a781e578
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sericulture is the raising of silkworms to produce silk thread. It began in China, where silk fragments from Qianshanyang in Zhejiang date to about 2700 BCE, and silk later became a leading good of Eurasian trade and diplomacy.
--- fact 1
Silk fragments from Qianshanyang, a Liangzhu culture site in Zhejiang, date to about 2700 BCE. Earlier claims (a fabric at Qingtaicun in Henan, about 3630 BCE, and possible silk protein at Jiahu) are treated here with more caution.
--- fact 2
The domesticated silkworm, Bombyx mori, descends from the wild moth Bombyx mandarina.
--- fact 3
From the 2nd century BCE, the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] sent silk to the [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]] as gifts under peace arrangements.
--- fact 4
Silk moved west along the routes described in [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]].
--- fact 5
According to the Byzantine writer Procopius, two monks brought silkworm eggs to Emperor Justinian in 552 CE, hidden in bamboo rods. This account comes from Byzantine sources, and its details are not independently confirmed.
--- context
Sericulture is an [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] technology of the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] whose products shaped the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] as well as Chinese diplomacy. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer sees sericulture as a tool-lineage that could be held as a monopoly of knowledge: a craft that depends on one insect and a few skills can stay within one region for a very long time, and the smuggling stories show how much that knowledge was worth. Silk also worked as a portable status-signal and as a currency of diplomacy. The reading would weaken if the secrecy proved to be later legend rather than practice.
--- question 1
How old is silk in China, and which early claims will hold?
--- question 2
How closely was silkworm rearing guarded, and by whom?

=== technology/stirrup
title: Stirrup
type: technology
date: c. 302 CE
themes: war, technology
src: 8454b1eda6f9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The stirrup is a foot support hung from a saddle that aids mounting and balance. The earliest firm evidence is a single mounting stirrup on a Chinese figurine of about 302 CE, with paired stirrups following within decades.
--- fact 1
A ceramic figurine from a Western Jin tomb near Changsha, China, dated about 302 CE, shows a single stirrup meant only for mounting.
--- fact 2
The earliest known paired riding stirrups appear in an Eastern Jin tomb near Nanjing, about 322 CE.
--- fact 3
The oldest surviving physical double stirrups come from the 415 CE tomb of the Northern Yan noble Feng Sufu.
--- fact 4
Claims of earlier toe-loop mounting aids elsewhere are debated; firm dated evidence begins in China.
--- fact 5
Stirrups spread from East Asia through steppe peoples to Byzantium and Europe over later centuries.
--- fact 6
Lynn White's 1962 "stirrup thesis," linking stirrups to European feudalism, was challenged from the 1970s on and is now largely rejected.
--- context
The stirrup follows earlier mounted and wheeled war technology, including the [[technology/chariot|Chariot]] built on [[technology/the-wheel|The Wheel]] and the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]], continuing a lineage of [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. Its contested link to European cavalry is a well-known case of overstated causation. Threads: [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer reads the stirrup as a modest tool-lineage advance later inflated into an oversized story: a strap reorganizing sanctioned harm across a continent. It clearly aided mounted combat, but crediting it alone for feudalism resembles a meaning-engine narrative outrunning the evidence. The reading would weaken if new finds showed true stirrups elsewhere, unconnected to China. An alternative view treats it as one convenience among several, not a sole cause.
--- question 1
What route and timeframe carried stirrups from East Asia to Byzantium and western Europe?
--- question 2
Do earlier South Asian toe-loop aids count as true stirrups?

=== technology/telephone
title: Telephone
type: technology
date: 1876
themes: language, technology
src: f5bb9fadf672
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The telephone transmits speech as electrical signals over wire. Alexander Graham Bell received the first US telephone patent in 1876, amid competing claims from other inventors.
--- fact 1
Bell's application, titled Improvement in Telegraphy, was filed on 14 February 1876; US Patent 174,465 was granted on 7 March 1876.
--- fact 2
Elisha Gray filed a patent caveat for a similar device the same day; the dispute led to litigation.
--- fact 3
Bell's notebook records intelligible speech to his assistant Thomas Watson on 10 March 1876.
--- fact 4
Antonio Meucci filed a caveat on 28 December 1871 for a voice-communication device; it lapsed after 1874.
--- fact 5
The US House passed a 2002 resolution honoring Meucci; the Senate did not, and its meaning is disputed.
--- fact 6
The first commercial exchange opened in New Haven, Connecticut, on 28 January 1878.
--- context
The telephone built on [[technology/telegraph-and-submarine-cables|Telegraph and Submarine Cables]], reusing wires and telegraph-era electrical know-how; Bell's patent was framed as a telegraph improvement. Wireless voice followed in [[technology/radio|Radio]]. It emerged in [[region/north-america|North America]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and US subscriber networks expanded quickly in the 1880s and 1890s. Threads: [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The telephone let the sound-code travel beyond earshot, where the telegraph had carried only mark-code. Near-simultaneous claims by Bell, Gray, and Meucci suggest a shared tool-lineage from telegraphy made the step available to several tinkerers, more than a single leap. This reading would weaken if records showed the key components arose independently of telegraph work. Alternatively, credit may reflect filing timing and later litigation more than technical priority.
--- question 1
Did Bell's patent draw on Gray's ideas? Historians disagree.
--- question 2
How closely did Meucci's devices resemble a working electric telephone?

=== technology/telescope
title: Telescope
type: technology
date: 1608
themes: science, technology
src: 2d67a65b6fbf
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The telescope is an optical instrument using lenses to magnify distant objects, first documented in the Netherlands in 1608 and quickly turned toward the night sky.
--- fact 1
Hans Lipperhey, a Dutch spectacle-maker, filed the first known telescope patent application on 2 October 1608, in The Hague.
--- fact 2
The States General denied the patent in December 1608, since similar devices were already known to others.
--- fact 3
Jacob Metius and Sacharias Janssen made rival claims around the same time; priority among the Dutch makers remains disputed.
--- fact 4
[[person/galileo-galilei|Galileo Galilei]] heard of the device in 1609 and built improved telescopes with much higher magnification.
--- fact 5
Galileo observed lunar mountains in late 1609 and four moons of Jupiter in January 1610, publishing these findings in Sidereus Nuncius that March.
--- context
The telescope's emergence is usually placed within the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]] in [[region/europe|Europe]], when instrument-making and direct observation increasingly supplemented inherited authority. Galileo's use of it to support a Sun-centered solar system connects the device to debates within [[theme/science|Science]] over evidence and method.
--- observer
The tool-lineage for extending the eye did not trace to one mind: several Dutch lens-grinders reached a similar design within weeks of each other, and only later did one observer's pattern-taming turn scattered glints of light into a public record of moons and mountains. In several surveyed cases, the credited inventor is the one who publicized a device, not the first to build it. This would weaken if records showed direct copying rather than parallel invention. An alternative reading credits Galileo's systematic recording, not the lens itself, as the more decisive step.
--- question 1
Did the Dutch claimants develop their designs independently, or did knowledge pass between workshops before 1608?

=== technology/terracotta-army
title: Terracotta Army
type: technology
date: 210 BCE
themes: art, religion
src: 68bbf764c5b9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Terracotta Army is a buried set of life-size clay soldiers, horses, and chariots built for the tomb of [[person/qin-shi-huang|Qin Shi Huang]], first emperor of unified China, and found only in 1974.
--- fact 1
On 29 March 1974, farmers digging a well near Lintong, outside Xi'an, struck pottery fragments near the [[place/mausoleum-of-qin-shi-huang|Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang]].
--- fact 2
More than 2,000 figures have been excavated from three pits; estimates put the full total above 8,000, most still unexcavated.
--- fact 3
Figures were individually finished, with distinct faces, hairstyles, and armor, and were originally painted in bright pigment that flakes away within minutes of air exposure.
--- fact 4
UNESCO inscribed the Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor, including the army, as a World Heritage Site in 1987.
--- fact 5
The army dates to the reign of Qin Shi Huang (unified China 221 BCE, died 210 BCE); whether construction began at his accession in 246 BCE or later is debated.
--- context
The army belongs to the funerary complex of the [[place/mausoleum-of-qin-shi-huang|Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang]], built under [[person/qin-shi-huang|Qin Shi Huang]] after the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]]. It is a [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] site of [[region/east-asia|East Asia]]. Threads: [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer reads this as a grief-rite on an extraordinary scale, turning a ruler's command into a buried status-signal meant to outlast him. The individualized faces suggest the makers aimed at a functioning army, hinting that the meaning-engine behind it treated the afterlife as continuous with ordinary rule. The reading would weaken if the unexcavated majority proved mold-repeated rather than individuated. An alternative reading treats the project mainly as status-signal for the living court, with religious intent secondary.
--- question 1
Did construction begin at Qin Shi Huang's accession in 246 BCE, or later in his reign?

=== technology/variolation
title: Variolation
type: technology
date: 1549
themes: science, technology
src: 0770e1c25701
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Variolation is deliberate exposure to smallpox material, by skin scratching or nasal insufflation of dried scab powder, to induce a milder infection and immunity, used before Jennerian vaccination.
--- fact 1
Wan Quan's Douzhen Xinfa (1549) is the oldest confirmed Chinese text describing nasal-insufflation inoculation; claims of 10th-century origins are legendary and unverified.
--- fact 2
The practice was used independently in the Ottoman Empire, South Asia, and parts of West Africa before its documented adoption in Western Europe, though the earliest dates in each region are poorly attested.
--- fact 3
Lady Mary Wortley Montagu observed variolation in Constantinople in 1717 and had her son inoculated there in 1718.
--- fact 4
Montagu's advocacy and a 1721 London trial on condemned prisoners helped popularize variolation in England.
--- fact 5
Variolation carried a real fatality risk, roughly 1 to 2 percent, and was largely replaced after Edward Jenner's 1796 cowpox vaccination.
--- context
Variolation is the forerunner of [[technology/vaccination|Vaccination]], which replaced it after 1796 with far lower mortality. Its clearest early written record sits in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]]; its path into [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] Britain ran through Ottoman practice Montagu saw in the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]]. Historians treat it as folk empirical science preceding formal immunology.
--- observer
The Observer reads variolation as a tool-lineage the species apparently reinvented, or quietly passed along exchange-webs, in several disconnected regions before any had known contact. This reading weakens badly for the earliest Chinese dates, which rest on legend rather than surviving texts, so a genuinely independent origin centuries earlier should be treated as unproven. An alternative reading holds the cases were connected by trade routes the record cannot show.
--- question 1
Does the 16th-century Chinese record reflect a new technique or the first written trace of an older oral practice?
--- question 2
How much of the reported independent development elsewhere reflects genuine separate invention versus obscured contact?

=== technology/water-clock
title: Water Clock
type: technology
date: c. 1400 BCE
themes: science, technology
src: d8f88a324e60
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A water clock, or clepsydra, measures time by the steady flow of water into or out of a vessel. The oldest surviving example is the alabaster clepsydra from Karnak, made in the reign of Amenhotep III in the 14th century BCE, and a written record points to an earlier Egyptian origin.
--- fact 1
The Karnak clepsydra was found in 1904 and is now in a Cairo museum. Its alabaster body has twelve columns of hour-marks, and water drips through a small hole in its base.
--- fact 2
A tomb inscription of the official Amenemhet, from the 16th century BCE, credits him with devising a water clock: earlier written evidence than the Karnak vessel itself.
--- fact 3
As the water level fell, the flow slowed, so many designs used sloped sides or constant-level inflow to keep the rate steady.
--- fact 4
In Athens, water clocks timed speeches in the law courts.
--- fact 5
Chinese and Islamic makers built elaborate water-driven clocks; Su Song's 1088 astronomical clock tower is the best known. See [[technology/mechanical-clock|Mechanical Clock]].
--- context
The water clock was a [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] instrument of the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] that measured hours when a sundial could not, alongside the [[technology/calendars|Calendars]] that counted days. Threads: [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]]. It later shaped the [[technology/mechanical-clock|Mechanical Clock]].
--- observer
The Observer treats the water clock as pattern-taming turned into a device: an hour becomes something a vessel can count without anyone watching the sky. Courts using it to fix the length of speeches show a shared measure of time working as story-glue. The reading would weaken if early water clocks proved to be mostly ritual objects with little practical use.
--- question 1
Was the Egyptian water clock older than the Karnak example?
--- question 2
How accurate were early clepsydras compared with shadow clocks?

=== technology/windmill
title: Windmill
type: technology
date: c. 900 CE
themes: technology
src: 78a657b3e3ab
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The windmill converts wind into rotational mechanical power, historically used to grind grain and pump water. The earliest well-documented windmills are vertical-axis panemone mills in Sistan, on the Iranian Plateau, described by Islamic geographers in the 9th and 10th centuries CE.
--- fact 1
Vertical-axis panemone windmills operated in Sistan, on the Iranian Plateau, by the 9th to 10th century CE.
--- fact 2
The geographers al-Masudi (writing 943 CE) and Ibn Hawqal (traveling 943-969 CE) both recorded Sistan's wind-driven grain mills.
--- fact 3
The mills used vertical wooden-frame sails of cloth or reed matting in tall adobe towers, turned by strong seasonal winds.
--- fact 4
Sistan mills ground grain and, in some structures, pumped water for irrigation, alongside the region's [[technology/qanat|Qanat]] system.
--- fact 5
A structurally distinct horizontal-axis windmill, the post mill, appears in Europe from the 12th century CE, about three centuries later.
--- fact 6
Whether the European design diffused from Persian precedent or arose independently is debated, with no settled consensus.
--- context
The Sistan windmills arose in the same arid environment as [[technology/qanat|Qanat]] irrigation on the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], part of [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] technology history, and show a mechanical solution recurring, in modified form, far from its first attested site. Thread: [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
For the Observer, the windmill is a test case in tool-lineage: whether a device invented once becomes the ancestor of every later version, or the same fix for wind and water is assembled twice. Sistan holds clear textual priority, yet the vertical- and horizontal-axis designs differ enough that a shared exchange-web is not proven. The reading would weaken if a vertical-axis intermediate turned up between Sistan and northern Europe. An alternative reading treats the European post mill as independent engineering rather than inherited tool-lineage.
--- question 1
Did the European horizontal-axis windmill descend from Persian designs, or was it invented independently?

=== technology/x-ray-imaging
title: X-ray Imaging
type: technology
date: 1895
themes: science, technology
src: f7608740c03a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
X-rays are penetrating electromagnetic radiation identified in 1895 by Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen. Within months they let physicians see bones and foreign objects inside living bodies without surgery.
--- fact 1
On 8 November 1895, Roentgen saw a glow on a fluorescent screen while working with cathode-ray tubes at the University of Wurzburg.
--- fact 2
He called them X-rays because their nature was unknown, and published in December 1895 with an image of his wife's hand.
--- fact 3
Photographs circulated from January 1896, and medical use began within weeks in Europe and North America.
--- fact 4
Early operators suffered skin burns and other injuries before the hazards were understood.
--- fact 5
Henri Becquerel reported radioactivity in uranium salts in 1896, following up on the new rays.
--- fact 6
Roentgen received the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901 and took no patent.
--- context
X-ray imaging came from university physics in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Becquerel's 1896 discovery of radioactivity, prompted by the new rays, led to the research of [[person/marie-curie|Marie Curie]]. It shows a result of [[theme/science|Science]] becoming a clinical tool almost at once. Thread: [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
For the species, X-rays extended the tool-lineage by making the body's interior visible, and the images spread fast partly because a photograph is easy to show. Early enthusiasm may reflect status-signal as well as medical need, since public demonstrations were popular. This reading would weaken if adoption records show clinical demand alone explains the 1896 spread. An alternative reading credits existing networks of instrument makers and hospitals.
--- question 1
Did earlier cathode-ray experimenters see the effect without recognizing it?
--- question 2
How fast did practitioners link injuries to exposure?

=== technology/zero-and-place-value-notation
title: Zero and Place-Value Notation
type: technology
date: c. 300 CE
themes: science, language, trade
src: 7c30f71c7625
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The decimal place-value system, in which zero serves as a digit and placeholder, developed in India in the first millennium CE and spread across the world.
--- fact 1
The Bakhshali manuscript, found in what is now Pakistan, uses a dot as a placeholder zero. It is a composite of birch-bark leaves from different periods: Oxford radiocarbon dating in 2017 gave 224 to 383 CE, 680 to 779 CE, and 885 to 993 CE for three folios, and a 2024 revision widened the overall range to 799 to 1102 CE and moved the oldest tested folio to 931 to 1032 CE. The claim that it holds the earliest zero is therefore contested.
--- fact 2
The Indian mathematician Brahmagupta gave rules for arithmetic with zero in 628 CE.
--- fact 3
Scholars in the Islamic world, including [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]], carried the system westward.
--- fact 4
Fibonacci's 1202 book popularized it in Europe.
--- context
The system emerged in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], possibly during the [[culture/gupta-empire|Gupta Empire]], and is the basis of all modern arithmetic and of the binary numbers used in the [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how a symbol for absence turned out to be one of the most productive ideas in history. Cultures without it could still do arithmetic, but not as easily.
--- question 1
When was zero first used as a number rather than a placeholder?

=== theme/art-and-ritual
title: Art and Ritual
type: theme
date: 
themes: art
src: 5719dfb38818
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Art and ritual are symbolic and expressive practices: painting, carving, ornament, music and dance, storytelling, monumental building, and ceremony, including rites for the dead. They are found in all human societies and go back at least to the Paleolithic, with engraved ochre, beads, and cave paintings from tens of thousands of years ago. The vault follows them from cave painting and prehistoric monuments, through the temple and court arts of ancient empires and medieval kingdoms, to Renaissance and Mughal building, and to objects taken in conquest, such as the Benin Bronzes. It treats art and ritual as evidence of what societies valued and of how they marked power, belonging, and mortality.
--- observer
The Observer treats art and ritual as behavior with no obvious direct survival function that nonetheless occupies enormous time and resources in every culture. This is a central puzzle of the species. The record suggests several linked roles: art and ritual carry story-glue that binds strangers, they serve as status-signal for rulers and groups, and they give form to the grief-rite, and a single monument can do all three at once. The Observer would revise this reading if the cost of art and ritual can be shown to buy an advantage, such as cooperation or shared memory, that the record now hides, in which case the puzzle would dissolve rather than deepen.
--- question 1
What is the adaptive role of art and ritual?
--- question 2
When did symbolic art begin, and did other human species such as Neanderthals make it?
--- question 3
Did ritual and monumental building come before settled life, as [[place/gobekli-tepe|Göbekli Tepe]] has been read to suggest, or after it?
--- question 4
How can perishable arts, such as music, dance, textiles, and wood carving, be studied where none survive?
--- question 5
How did patronage by courts, temples, and merchant families shape which art survives?
--- extra 1: Key Threads
- Symbolic behavior appears early. Engraved ochre at Blombos Cave in South Africa dates to about 75,000 to 100,000 years ago; a warty-pig painting in Sulawesi is at least 45,500 years old; and the painted animals of [[place/chauvet-cave|Chauvet Cave]], made about 36,000 years ago, include horses, lions, mammoths, rhinoceroses, and bears. See [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]]. Whether Neanderthals also made symbolic objects is debated ([[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]]).
- Ice Age peoples worked bone, tusk, and clay. Humans hunted [[species/woolly-mammoth|Woolly Mammoth]] and used their bones and tusks for shelters and art, [[technology/pottery|Pottery]] appears in East Asia at least 20,000 years ago, and the [[culture/jomon-culture|Jomon Culture]] made cord-marked pottery and clay figurines called dogu.
- Oral traditions and performance transmit knowledge and identity. [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]] pass on knowledge through oral traditions, songlines, and ceremony, and Australian rock art includes some of the oldest known paintings; griots perform the epic of [[person/sundiata-keita|Sundiata Keita]] and preserve the history of the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]].
- Monumental building came before cities in some places. [[place/gobekli-tepe|Göbekli Tepe]] has carved pillars up to about 5.5 meters tall, [[place/catalhoyuk|Çatalhöyük]] has painted walls, [[place/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] was raised and rebuilt over several centuries from about 3000 BCE, and [[place/poverty-point|Poverty Point]] in Louisiana was built by hunter-gatherer-fishers with six concentric earthen ridges and mounds.
- Egypt tied art to kingship and the afterlife. The [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] built pyramids and temples and mummified the elite, and tombs such as those of the [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]] show the resources committed. [[person/akhenaten|Akhenaten]]'s reign has a distinctive, naturalistic style, and many images of [[person/hatshepsut|Hatshepsut]], whose mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari is a major monument, were later removed or defaced.
- Craft traditions carried identity across large areas. The [[culture/minoans|Minoans]] painted vivid frescoes in palaces such as Knossos, the [[culture/lapita-culture|Lapita Culture]] decorated pottery with fine stamped geometric patterns, the [[culture/olmecs|Olmecs]] carved colossal heads from basalt more than three meters tall, the [[culture/scythians|Scythians]] made gold work and left textiles and tattoos preserved in frozen tombs at Pazyryk, and Polynesian carvers made the moai of [[place/rapa-nui|Rapa Nui]].
- Classical and Byzantine art served city and empire. [[place/athens|Athens]] built the Parthenon between 447 and 432 BCE and produced drama and philosophy ([[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]]); [[place/rome|Rome]] completed the Colosseum in 80 CE; the Apadana reliefs of [[place/persepolis|Persepolis]] show delegations from across the empire bringing gifts; the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]] cut tombs and temples into the cliffs of Petra; and the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] built the Hagia Sophia under Justinian I.
- Buddhist, Hindu, and court arts flourished in South and East Asia. Gandhara art under the [[culture/kushan-empire|Kushan Empire]] blended Greek and Indian styles and produced some of the earliest images of the Buddha in human form; the [[culture/gupta-empire|Gupta Empire]] is associated with a flowering of the arts, though "Golden Age" is a later label; the [[culture/chola-dynasty|Chola Dynasty]] made celebrated bronzes, including the dancing Shiva, and built the Brihadisvara Temple about 1010; the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]] is a high point of Chinese poetry and art; and the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] completed the Forbidden City in 1420.
- Southeast Asian states built vast religious monuments. [[place/borobudur|Borobudur]] in Java has 2,672 relief panels and originally 504 Buddha statues, [[place/angkor|Angkor]] holds Angkor Wat, the largest religious monument in the world by area, built by the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]], and the [[culture/pagan-kingdom|Pagan Kingdom]] built thousands of temples. [[culture/champa|Champa]] built temples at My Son.
- Islamic and Central Asian courts patronized architecture and learning. [[culture/al-andalus|Al-Andalus]] produced the Alhambra, built mainly in the 13th and 14th centuries; the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]] and [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]] are remembered for architecture and science under [[person/timur|Timur]] and [[person/ulugh-beg|Ulugh Beg]]; the [[culture/safavid-empire|Safavid Empire]] made Isfahan known for its mosques, squares, and gardens; and the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] produced the [[place/taj-mahal|Taj Mahal]], built from about 1632 to 1653 by Shah Jahan for his wife Mumtaz Mahal with about 20,000 workers.
- African states built in stone and cast in bronze. [[place/great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]] has dry-stone walls up to about 11 meters high, the [[culture/zagwe-dynasty|Zagwe Dynasty]] carved churches from living rock, the [[culture/kingdom-of-benin|Kingdom of Benin]] organized craft guilds, and the elite graves of the [[culture/kingdom-of-mapungubwe|Kingdom of Mapungubwe]] held gold and imported glass beads. In 1897 British forces removed the Benin Bronzes and sold them to defray the costs of the expedition, during the age of the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]].
- The Americas built ceremonial centers on a large scale. [[place/teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] laid out the Pyramids of the Sun and Moon along the Avenue of the Dead, [[place/chichen-itza|Chichen Itza]] has El Castillo, where the equinox sun casts a serpent-like shadow, [[place/cahokia|Cahokia]] built Monks Mound, and the Inca built [[place/machu-picchu|Machu Picchu]] about 1450. The [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]] made richly decorated ceramics and burial urns, and the [[culture/muisca|Muisca]] held gold rituals.
- The [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]] shows art shaped by patronage. Brunelleschi developed linear perspective in Florence in experiments of about 1415 to 1420, and Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, and Raphael worked under the patronage of wealthy families and the Church, with the Medici financing artists through banking.
- Rites for the dead shaped much surviving art. The [[culture/chinchorro-culture|Chinchorro Culture]] reconstructed bodies with clay, wigs, and mineral pigments; the Egyptians mummified their elite; the dead of Çatalhöyük lay under house floors; and burial goods appear in graves across cultures. See [[observer-note/why-the-species-buries-its-dead|Why the Species Buries Its Dead]].
- Named individual artists appear once art becomes a named profession rather than an anonymous craft, from [[person/murasaki-shikibu|Murasaki Shikibu]]'s court novel and [[person/giotto|Giotto]]'s frescoes through the workshops of the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]] and the Mughal court ([[person/behzad|Behzad]], [[person/basawan|Basawan]]) to European academies and, by the 19th century, an international gallery and print market. Painting, architecture, design, film and literature all appear among these named figures ([[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]], [[person/mimar-sinan|Mimar Sinan]], [[person/william-morris|William Morris]], [[person/georges-melies|Georges Méliès]], [[person/jane-austen|Jane Austen]]), and images could travel by mass reproduction, as [[person/utagawa-hiroshige|Utagawa Hiroshige]]'s and [[person/raja-ravi-varma|Raja Ravi Varma]]'s prints did, long before broadcast or digital media.

--- extra 3: Regional Patterns
- **[[region/oceania|Oceania]]:** [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]] made some of the oldest known paintings, the [[culture/lapita-culture|Lapita Culture]] decorated pottery with stamped patterns, and Polynesian carvers made the moai of [[place/rapa-nui|Rapa Nui]], about 95 percent of the island's monolithic statues having been carved from the volcanic tuff of one quarry.
- **[[region/andes|Andes]] and the [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]]:** The [[culture/chinchorro-culture|Chinchorro Culture]] reconstructed and preserved its dead, the Inca built in stone at [[place/machu-picchu|Machu Picchu]], the [[culture/muisca|Muisca]] held gold rituals, and the [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]] made painted and incised ceramics.
- **[[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]]:** Dry-stone building at [[place/great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]], rock-hewn churches under the [[culture/zagwe-dynasty|Zagwe Dynasty]], guild-made bronzes in the [[culture/kingdom-of-benin|Kingdom of Benin]], and the oral art of griots in the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]].
- **[[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]] and [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]]:** The [[culture/scythians|Scythians]] made gold ornaments and preserved textiles, and the oases of [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]] under the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]] became a center of monumental architecture; Gandhara art under the [[culture/kushan-empire|Kushan Empire]] joined Greek and Indian styles.
- **[[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] and [[region/north-america|North America]]:** The [[culture/olmecs|Olmecs]] carved colossal heads, [[place/teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] and [[place/chichen-itza|Chichen Itza]] built pyramids, and Chichen Itza has the largest known Mesoamerican ball court; in North America, [[place/poverty-point|Poverty Point]] and [[place/cahokia|Cahokia]] raised earthworks at large scale, and [[place/chaco-canyon|Chaco Canyon]] built great houses with hundreds of rooms.
- **[[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]]:** [[place/borobudur|Borobudur]], [[place/angkor|Angkor]], the [[culture/pagan-kingdom|Pagan Kingdom]], and [[culture/champa|Champa]] show Buddhist and Hindu art carried by sea and by court patronage.
- **[[region/south-asia|South Asia]] and [[region/east-asia|East Asia]]:** Gandhara, Gupta, and Chola art in the south, and Tang poetry and Ming palace building in the east, show state and temple patronage on a large scale; the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] added the [[place/taj-mahal|Taj Mahal]].
- **[[region/europe|Europe]], the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], and the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]]:** From [[place/chauvet-cave|Chauvet Cave]] and [[place/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] to Athens, Rome, Byzantium, and the Renaissance, art in these regions is tied to city, church, and court.
--- extra 4: Debates and Limits
- The categories of art and ritual are modern and not universal. Many objects in the vault, such as pillars, urns, masks, and statues, were made for religious, political, or social purposes and not as art in the modern sense, so this hub treats art broadly.
- The meaning and function of prehistoric art are inferred, not known. Whether [[place/gobekli-tepe|Göbekli Tepe]] and [[place/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] were ritual sites is argued from their form and context, the purposes of cave paintings are debated, and the dates of the oldest art have been revised, as in the 2024 report of Sulawesi narrative scenes at least 51,200 years old.
- The record is biased toward durable materials. Stone, pottery, and metal survive, while music, dance, wood carving, and most textiles rarely do, except in exceptional finds such as the frozen tombs at Pazyryk and in living traditions such as songlines and griot performance.
- Ownership, attribution, and erasure shape what is known. The Benin Bronzes were removed and sold in 1897, attribution of the Taj Mahal's chief architect is debated, images of [[person/hatshepsut|Hatshepsut]] were defaced, and colonial-era writers denied that [[place/great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]] was built by Africans, a claim archaeology overturned.
- The vault's coverage is uneven. It has 53 named artists (painting, sculpture, architecture, design, film, and literature, added from a compiled source, [[source/art-talk-artist-profiles|Art-Talk (Artist Profiles)]], not yet independently fact-checked), but still no notes on music, dance, or theater as topics, none on textiles, and almost none on art after 1945; the artists themselves also skew toward Europe and toward painting, since the source selected only public-domain-licensed figures.
