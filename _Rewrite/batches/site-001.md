=== era/hadean-eon
title: Hadean Eon
type: era
date: 4.567 – 4 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: 16c4efaf8e5a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Hadean is the informal first eon of Earth's history, running from the planet's formation about 4.54 billion years ago to roughly 4.0 billion years ago. Earth was molten, then cooling, and heavily bombarded.
--- fact 1
Earth and the Moon formed in the first tens of millions of years; see [[event/formation-of-the-solar-system-and-earth|Formation of the Solar System and Earth]] and [[event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon|Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon]].
--- fact 2
The oldest known Earth material is zircon crystals from the Jack Hills in Western Australia, dated to about 4.4 billion years ago.
--- fact 3
Those zircons suggest liquid water and some crust existed very early.
--- fact 4
The Hadean is not an official ICS unit; it is an informal name for the pre-rock-record interval.
--- context
The Hadean sets up everything later: the Moon stabilizes Earth's tilt, and volatile-rich material delivered to the young planet gave it oceans. The eon ends where the surviving rock record begins, at the start of the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]].
--- observer
The Observer files the Hadean under preconditions. Nothing here is a subject of study, but every later fact leans on the planet's composition, its Moon, and its water.
--- question 1
How early did stable oceans and continents exist?
--- question 2
Was there a Late Heavy Bombardment, or a gradual decline in impacts?

=== species/archaeopteryx
title: Archaeopteryx
type: species
date: c. 150 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c5cae12f8a6b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Archaeopteryx was a feathered, winged dinosaur about the size of a raven, known from Late Jurassic limestone in Bavaria, Germany, about 150 million years old.
--- fact 1
Fossils come from the Solnhofen limestone of Bavaria, Germany.
--- fact 2
It combined bird-like features, such as feathers and wings, with reptile-like ones, including teeth, clawed fingers, and a long bony tail.
--- fact 3
The first skeleton was found in 1861, two years after Darwin published On the Origin of Species, and was quickly cited as a transitional form.
--- fact 4
Feathered non-avian dinosaurs found in China from the 1990s onward strengthened the link between birds and theropod dinosaurs.
--- fact 5
Birds are the living descendants of theropod dinosaurs.
--- context
Archaeopteryx documents the origin of birds within the [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]]; birds were the dinosaur lineage that survived the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]. It was an early prop for the evolutionary ideas of [[person/charles-darwin|Charles Darwin]]. Archaeopteryx sits in the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the dinosaurs did not fully end; they are still on every continent as birds. The species that studies extinctions overhead lives among survivors of one of them.
--- question 1
Was Archaeopteryx capable of powered flight or only gliding?

observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Angkor was the capital region of the Khmer Empire in present-day Cambodia, including the temple of Angkor Wat, built in the early 12th century.
--- fact 1
Angkor Wat is the largest religious monument in the world by area.
--- fact 2
Aerial laser surveys reveal a vast urban area of roughly a thousand square kilometers.
--- fact 3
A network of reservoirs (barays) and canals managed water.
--- fact 4
It was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992. The traditional date of 1431 for the sack of Angkor by Ayutthaya and its abandonment is now disputed, and Angkor Wat itself was never abandoned.
--- context
Angkor was the center of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]]. See [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Angkor was a 'low-density' city, spread over a landscape of water systems and temples, and that it challenges a picture of cities as compact clusters.

=== place/auschwitz-birkenau
title: Auschwitz-Birkenau
type: place
date: 1940 – 1945
themes: war, technology
src: 8812f1aa4062
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Auschwitz-Birkenau was a complex of Nazi German concentration and extermination camps near the Polish town of Oświęcim, operated from 1940 to January 1945, where at least 1.1 million people, about 960,000 of them Jews, were murdered.
--- fact 1
Auschwitz was established in 1940 near Oświęcim, in Polish territory annexed by Germany, first for Polish political prisoners; the camp at Birkenau (Auschwitz II) was built from 1941 and became a center of mass murder in gas chambers from 1942, and the Monowitz camp (Auschwitz III) supplied forced labor to a chemical works of IG Farben.
--- fact 2
About 1.3 million people were deported to Auschwitz and at least 1.1 million of them died; about 960,000 were Jews from across occupied Europe, and other victims included about 74,000 non-Jewish Poles, about 21,000 Roma and Sinti, and about 15,000 Soviet prisoners of war.
--- fact 3
Most victims were killed on arrival after selection on the rail platform; others died of forced labor, starvation, disease, shootings and medical experiments.
--- fact 4
Prisoners resisted: in October 1944 members of the Sonderkommando revolted and destroyed a crematorium, and escapees, including Witold Pilecki in 1943 and Rudolf Vrba and Alfred Wetzler in 1944, carried reports of the killings to the outside.
--- fact 5
As Soviet forces approached in January 1945, the SS forced tens of thousands of prisoners on death marches, and Soviet troops liberated the camp on 27 January 1945, finding about 7,000 prisoners still there.
--- fact 6
Auschwitz-Birkenau became a state museum in 1947, and UNESCO inscribed the site in 1979; 27 January is observed as International Holocaust Remembrance Day.
--- context
Auschwitz-Birkenau was the largest site of the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]], carried out during [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. It sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer records Auschwitz-Birkenau as a place where a state murdered more than a million people, most of them Jews, on the basis of an ideology about descent, and records those deaths as suffering and atrocity. The killing was carried out with the methods of administration and industry, rail schedules, selection lists, factories and gas, which shows that tool-lineage and bureaucracy can be turned to sanctioned harm on a very large scale. The preserved site, survivors' testimony and the perpetrators' own records now serve as memory-outsourcing against denial and forgetting, and the resistance of prisoners is part of that record. A historian could challenge the emphasis on administrative method by pointing to the role of ideology and of popular participation, which this account does not explain.
--- question 1
How were the figures for the dead reconstructed, given that the perpetrators destroyed records?
--- question 2
How are survivors' testimony and the physical site being preserved as the last survivors die?

=== event/armenian-genocide
title: Armenian Genocide
type: event
date: 1915 – 1923
themes: war, kinship, religion
src: d6c90e3781d8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Armenian Genocide was the deliberate destruction of most of the Ottoman Empire's Armenian population during World War I, through mass killings, forced deportations, and death from starvation and exposure. It is recognized as genocide by many countries and historians and is denied by the government of Turkey.
--- fact 1
On 24 April 1915 Ottoman authorities arrested about 240 Armenian leaders in Constantinople, including writers, doctors, religious leaders, and political figures, and deported them eastward. The date is now used to commemorate the victims.
--- fact 2
In spring 1915 the Ottoman government began deporting Armenians from the northeastern border regions and then extended deportations to almost every province, including regions far from combat.
--- fact 3
Victims died in local massacres, and on forced marches from starvation, dehydration, exposure, and disease, and in or on the way to the deserts of the southern empire.
--- fact 4
Estimates of the number killed run from at least 600,000 to more than 1,000,000; the exact number is not settled.
--- fact 5
The Turkish state has long denied that the events were genocide and has worked against official recognition abroad. More than twenty countries formally recognize the genocide; Uruguay did so first, in 1965.
--- context
The genocide took place inside the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] during [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. It is often studied alongside the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]], and the vault's treatment of organized killing appears in [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer records this as an atrocity: a state deliberately destroyed much of one of its own peoples. It also records that the denial has itself lasted a century, so that the question of what happened has become bound up with the question of who is permitted to say it.
--- question 1
How many people died, and how should deaths on the deportation routes be counted?
--- question 2
How have the surviving communities and their descendants preserved memory under conditions of official denial?

=== event/permian-triassic-extinction
title: Permian-Triassic Extinction
type: event
date: 251.9 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 24312436bf48
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Permian-Triassic extinction, about 251.9 million years ago, was the largest mass extinction in Earth's history. It killed an estimated 80 to 96 percent of marine species.
--- fact 1
The leading cause is enormous volcanic eruptions in Siberia (the Siberian Traps).
--- fact 2
Consequences included rapid warming, ocean acidification, and oxygen loss in the seas.
--- fact 3
It ended the dominance of trilobites, which disappeared.
--- fact 4
Full ecological recovery took roughly ten million years.
--- context
It marks the end of the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]] and the start of the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]]. See [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]].
--- observer
The Observer regards this as the nearest the biosphere has come to ending. Its cause, a carbon-releasing planetary change, has an uncomfortable resemblance to a process the species is now carrying out.
--- question 1
How fast did the warming and acidification occur?

=== person/ada-lovelace
title: Ada Lovelace
type: person
date: 1815 – 1852
themes: science, technology
src: a4b9590aed81
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ada Lovelace (1815 to 1852) was an English mathematician who wrote extensive notes on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine, including what is often regarded as the first published computer algorithm.
--- fact 1
She was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron.
--- fact 2
Her 1843 notes described how the engine could compute a sequence of Bernoulli numbers.
--- fact 3
She suggested that such a machine might manipulate symbols beyond numbers, such as music.
--- fact 4
The extent of her original contribution versus Babbage's is debated.
--- context
Lovelace's ideas anticipate the [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]] and the [[event/digital-revolution|Digital Revolution]]. Compare [[person/alan-turing|Alan Turing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the concept of programmable machines appeared a century before the machines themselves. Ideas can outrun their tools.
--- question 1
What was her role in the design of the algorithm?

=== observer-note/fire-then-everything
title: Fire, Then Everything
type: observer-note
date: 
themes: technology, kinship, earth-systems
src: a790fb8494e5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Is there a single change that best explains the species' rapid rise in power over other animals?
--- observer
The Observer calls the control of fire heat-domestication. It is the first time the species used an energy source outside its own body. Every later step in its power, from smelting to steam to the power grid, repeats this pattern: find a stored source of energy and learn to release it on demand. The consequence is both a triumph and the root of a modern problem, because the same principle now warms the planet.
--- extra 1: Question
Is there a single change that best explains the species' rapid rise in power over other animals?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Fire control is evidenced from at least a million years ago; see [[event/control-of-fire|Control of Fire]].
- Cooking makes food safer and easier to digest and may have supported larger brains.
- Fire extends the day, provides protection, and gathers people.
- Later technologies, from pottery to metallurgy, depend on heat.
- The [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] scaled up the same principle with coal and the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]].
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The essay's framing is a simplification. Language, cooperation, and cumulative learning are also candidates for the deepest cause of human power.
