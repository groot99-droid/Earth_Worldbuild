=== species/black-rat
title: Black Rat
type: species
date: c. 100 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade
src: e1466fe1a779
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The black rat (Rattus rattus) is a rodent native to South Asia and Southeast Asia that became one of the most widespread commensals of humans, spreading with trade and shipping across Eurasia and later the world, and acting as a major vector of plague.
--- fact 1
Genetic studies find well-differentiated black rat lineages native to South Asia, the Himalayan region and Indochina, with diversification beginning long before human involvement; exactly when black rats first became commensal with people in that homeland is not precisely dated.
--- fact 2
Black rats spread out of South Asia along trade routes to the Near East and Egypt in antiquity, and documented Roman-era shipping and trade carried them across the Mediterranean and into the wider Roman Empire, reaching as far as Britain by about the 1st century CE.
--- fact 3
As a commensal species, the black rat thrives in and around human granaries, ships and settlements, feeding on stored grain and waste, which let it travel wherever people and cargo moved.
--- fact 4
Fleas carried on black rats are considered a major vector of Yersinia pestis, the bacterium behind the Plague of Justinian and the Black Death, though the disease's spread beyond rat-infested areas suggests other means of transmission, including human fleas and lice, also mattered.
--- fact 5
The black rat was later displaced from much of Europe and North America by the larger brown rat (Rattus norvegicus), which spread from Asia in the 18th century, though black rats remain widespread in warmer regions and on many islands.
--- fact 6
As an invasive species on islands without native rodents, black rats have caused the extinction or decline of ground-nesting birds and other native species, a continuing ecological effect of their spread.
--- context
The black rat traveled the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]] and Mediterranean shipping lanes of the [[culture/romans|Romans]], and its fleas are implicated in the transmission of the [[event/black-death|Black Death]]. Black Rat sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] (its documented Roman-era spread) and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: the black rat shows that an exchange-web built for grain and cloth can carry unintended passengers, so that trade and sanctioned harm are connected through a rodent that neither side of any transaction intended to move. Wherever ships and granaries created new habitat, this one species followed without domestication, a hitchhiker riding humanity's own tool-lineage of storage and transport. The reading would weaken if genetic dating eventually shows the rat's major dispersals tracking climate and rodent competition more than human shipping.
--- question 1
When did black rats first become commensal with people in South Asia, and how long before their Old World spread?
--- question 2
How much of the Black Death's transmission depended on black rats specifically, as against human ectoparasites?

=== species/honeybee
title: Honeybee
type: species
date: c. 2450 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade
src: 39e67874c54c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The honeybee (Apis mellifera) is a social insect kept by humans for honey, wax and pollination since at least the mid-3rd millennium BCE in Egypt, after millennia of unmanaged honey hunting.
--- fact 1
The oldest evidence of humans exploiting honeybees is a rock painting at Cuevas de la Araña in Spain, showing a figure climbing to raid a wild hive, dated to about the 6th millennium BCE; this was honey hunting, not management of colonies.
--- fact 2
The earliest known depiction of organized beekeeping, with hives and smoke used to calm bees, comes from a relief in the sun temple of the Egyptian pharaoh Nyuserre Ini, dated to about 2450 BCE; honeybee hieroglyphs appear even earlier, from the First Dynasty.
--- fact 3
Egyptian beekeepers used cylindrical hives, often of clay or woven reed and mud, and moved hives along the Nile by boat to follow flowering seasons, a practice recorded in later texts.
--- fact 4
Honey served as a sweetener, a preservative, an offering in temple ritual and a component of medical remedies in Egypt and other ancient societies; beeswax was used for sealing, casting and mummification.
--- fact 5
European colonists brought Apis mellifera to the Americas in the 17th century, where it was not native, as part of the broader exchange of organisms called the Columbian Exchange; some Indigenous peoples came to call the honeybee "the white man's fly" because its spread often preceded settler expansion.
--- fact 6
Beekeeping later became agriculturally essential for pollinating commercial crops, and from the late 20th century colony collapse disorder and pathogens raised concern about honeybee populations worldwide.
--- context
The honeybee was kept and depicted by the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]], one of the domesticated organisms folded into the wider suite of managed species that followed [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], and it was carried to a new hemisphere during the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. Honeybee sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] (its earliest documented management) and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: managing bees required humans to learn to work with, rather than against, an insect society organized on its own terms, so beekeeping is one of the earliest tool-lineages built on cooperating with another species' social order instead of simply killing or caging it. That a colonizing population could nickname an insect after the settlers who unintentionally introduced it shows how an ordinary exchange-web of trade and settlement leaves ecological footprints that outrun any single claim-line. The reading would weaken if genetic evidence shows honeybees dispersing into new regions chiefly on their own rather than through deliberate human transport.
--- question 1
How much earlier than 2450 BCE did organized beekeeping begin, and where?
--- question 2
How much has managed pollination reshaped agricultural landscapes compared with wild pollinators?

=== species/influenza-a-virus
title: Influenza A Virus
type: species
date: 1580
themes: earth-systems, trade, war
src: 71bab881e738
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Influenza A virus is a family of respiratory viruses whose natural reservoir is wild aquatic birds, and which has repeatedly crossed into humans to cause pandemics, the first clearly documented in 1580 and the most severe in 1918.
--- fact 1
Influenza A viruses of all recognized subtypes occur naturally in wild aquatic birds, which are considered the virus's ultimate reservoir; only a smaller number of subtypes have adapted to infect humans, pigs, horses and other mammals.
--- fact 2
The first pandemic clearly matching the description of influenza is generally dated to 1580, which is thought to have originated in Asia and spread along the Silk Road and new maritime trade routes through the Middle East into Europe and Africa within about six months, later reaching the Americas.
--- fact 3
Historians have documented roughly thirty further influenza pandemics since 1580, though descriptions from before reliable virology make some identifications uncertain.
--- fact 4
The 1918 to 1919 influenza pandemic, caused by an H1N1 strain, killed tens of millions of people worldwide, more than the First World War with which it coincided, and its spread was worsened by wartime troop movements and censorship of news about it in combatant countries.
--- fact 5
Influenza A virus was first isolated in the laboratory in 1933, using ferrets, which established the modern scientific study of the virus and its distinct subtypes defined by combinations of hemagglutinin and neuraminidase surface proteins.
--- fact 6
Because influenza A viruses mutate quickly and can exchange genetic material when two strains infect the same host, new pandemic strains continue to emerge from animal reservoirs, including the H1N1 "swine flu" of 2009 and various avian influenza strains monitored for pandemic potential.
--- context
Influenza A virus caused the [[event/1918-influenza-pandemic|1918 Influenza Pandemic]], and its pattern of periodic pandemic emergence is compared by epidemiologists to the more recent [[event/covid-19-pandemic|COVID-19 Pandemic]]; its first documented pandemic spread along the corridors of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. Influenza A Virus sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer records the deaths of 1918 and of every other severe influenza pandemic as suffering compounded, in that instance, by a war that moved young, densely packed populations across the world at the same time the virus needed to spread. That a virus native to wild birds can repeatedly leap into a species with no acquired immunity shows the exchange-web of trade, travel and animal contact working in the pathogen's favor as readily as in humanity's. Wartime censorship of the 1918 pandemic, which let neutral Spain's uncensored press give the pandemic its common name, is itself a case of a meaning-engine, in the form of state secrecy, shaping which suffering gets recorded and which is hidden. The reading would weaken if genetic studies eventually trace 1918's H1N1 strain to a much earlier, more gradual process of adaptation rather than a fast pandemic emergence.
--- question 1
How reliably can pre-19th-century epidemics be identified as influenza rather than another respiratory disease?
--- question 2
What conditions determine whether a new avian-origin influenza strain becomes a global pandemic rather than a contained outbreak?

=== species/mycobacterium-tuberculosis
title: Mycobacterium tuberculosis
type: species
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade
src: f1ac8a00ab94
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Mycobacterium tuberculosis is the bacterium behind tuberculosis, confirmed by DNA analysis in a 9,000-year-old mother and infant buried together at the submerged Neolithic settlement of Atlit-Yam, off the coast of modern Israel, though how much older the disease truly is remains disputed among specialists.
--- fact 1
At Atlit-Yam, a Neolithic village now under the Mediterranean near Haifa, archaeologists found a roughly 25-year-old woman buried with a one-year-old child, both showing skeletal lesions characteristic of tuberculosis; DNA and cell-wall fat analysis confirmed infection with the human-adapted strain, and the remains date to about 9,250 to 8,160 years ago, making this the oldest molecularly confirmed case known.
--- fact 2
Specialists disagree sharply on how old the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex is: one genomic analysis calibrated against ancient human migrations estimated a common ancestor about 70,000 years old, tracking the species' spread out of Africa, while other studies calibrated directly against ancient DNA, including a 17th-century European genome and pre-Columbian genomes from Peru, estimate the most recent common ancestor of modern strains at under 6,000 years, more consistent with a Neolithic emergence tied to farming and denser settlements.
--- fact 3
Ancient DNA from tuberculosis lesions in 1,000-year-old Peruvian skeletons surprisingly proved to be Mycobacterium pinnipedii, a strain associated with seals, suggesting marine mammals carried a form of the disease across the Atlantic to the Americas before European contact, independent of the strains Europeans later introduced.
--- fact 4
Tuberculosis in its classic pulmonary form typically spreads between people through the air in crowded conditions, and it became strongly associated with dense urban settlement, a link that intensified with the growth of industrial cities in the 18th and 19th centuries.
--- fact 5
Because tuberculosis often progresses slowly and can remain latent for years before becoming active or fatal, its true prevalence in any historical period is hard to establish from documentary sources alone, and confirmed cases depend on skeletal or genetic evidence.
--- fact 6
Tuberculosis remains a leading infectious cause of death worldwide, complicated in recent decades by strains resistant to standard antibiotics and by its interaction with HIV infection.
--- context
Tuberculosis is closely tied to the denser, settled communities that followed [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], and separate lineages reached the Americas by different routes before and after the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. Mycobacterium tuberculosis sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (its oldest confirmed case, at Atlit-Yam) and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer records the Atlit-Yam grief-rite, a mother and child buried together after tuberculosis killed them nine thousand years ago, as an early instance of suffering this bacterium has caused across every era of settled life since. That two entirely different scientific methods for dating the same organism's origin disagree by an order of magnitude, sixty-odd thousand years apart, is itself a caution about how far pattern-taming can outrun the certainty of its own conclusions. The seal-associated strain reaching Peru independently of Europeans suggests the exchange-web that carried this disease was wider and older than any single conquest narrative captures. The reading would weaken if future ancient-genome sampling closes the gap between the two dating methods in favor of one over the other.
--- question 1
Which dating method for the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex's age will hold up as more ancient genomes are sequenced?
--- question 2
How did a seal-associated tuberculosis strain reach human populations in South America before European contact?

=== species/plasmodium-falciparum
title: Plasmodium falciparum
type: species
date: c. 8000 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade
src: d930678e3a59
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Plasmodium falciparum is the parasite responsible for the deadliest form of human malaria, which genetic evidence traces to a single transmission event from western gorillas in Central Africa, probably within the last 10,000 years.
--- fact 1
Genomic studies of Plasmodium species carried by wild chimpanzees, gorillas and bonobos across central Africa found that Plasmodium falciparum is most closely related to a parasite of western gorillas, and that essentially all human strains descend from a single cross-species transmission event rather than long co-evolution with hominins.
--- fact 2
The greatly reduced genetic diversity of Plasmodium falciparum compared with its ape-parasite relatives points to a severe population bottleneck consistent with a relatively recent jump into humans, estimated at within roughly the last 10,000 years, revising an older view that the parasite had co-evolved with humans for millions of years.
--- fact 3
Once established in humans, Plasmodium falciparum is transmitted between people by Anopheles mosquitoes and became endemic across much of Sub-Saharan Africa, spreading later into the Mediterranean, South and Southeast Asia and, through the Atlantic slave trade and colonization, into the Americas.
--- fact 4
Malaria caused by this species produces severe fever, anemia and, in many cases, death, especially in young children, and it has shaped human genetics: the sickle-cell trait and several other blood disorders persist in malaria-endemic regions partly because they offer some protection against severe infection.
--- fact 5
Historically, malaria's toll among unacclimatized European soldiers, traders and colonists in West Africa contributed to the region's reputation among Europeans as dangerous to outsiders, while some enslaved and colonized African populations carried genetic and acquired resistance that shaped patterns of labor and disease in the plantation societies of the Americas.
--- fact 6
Malaria remains one of the leading causes of death from infectious disease worldwide, concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, and control efforts include insecticide-treated bed nets, antimalarial drugs and, since 2021, the first licensed malaria vaccines.
--- context
Plasmodium falciparum's history is entangled with the coerced movement of people in the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and the wider biological exchange of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], both of which carried the parasite, its mosquito vectors and human genetic responses to new regions. Plasmodium falciparum sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (the approximate window of its jump into humans) and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer records malaria's death toll, concentrated for centuries among the young and the newly arrived, as suffering rather than as a fixed feature of any one place. That a single cross-species transmission event, probably within the last ten thousand years, could produce a parasite responsible for so much of the species' recorded mortality is a reminder that ancestor-weight in disease history need not be deep to be heavy. The parasite's shaping of human blood chemistry, in the sickle-cell trait and related conditions, shows heat-domestication's opposite: an organism that reshaped the species' own biology in the act of persisting within it. The reading would weaken if further ape-parasite surveys find evidence of older or repeated transmission events rather than one bottleneck.
--- question 1
Can the cross-species transmission event that produced Plasmodium falciparum be dated more precisely than "within the last 10,000 years"?
--- question 2
How much of the historical pattern of disease resistance among enslaved and colonized African populations reflects malaria specifically, as against other endemic diseases?

=== species/variola-virus
title: Variola Virus
type: species
date: c. 1157 BCE
themes: earth-systems, war
src: ad1a21c46900
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Variola virus is the pathogen that causes smallpox, a disease suspected in Egyptian mummies of the 12th century BCE and confirmed by ancient DNA in later remains, that killed and disfigured people for millennia until its eradication in 1980.
--- fact 1
Pockmark-like scarring on Egyptian mummies, including that of Pharaoh Ramesses V (traditionally dated to about 1157 BCE), has long been cited as possible evidence of ancient smallpox, though this identification from skin lesions alone is not certain.
--- fact 2
Genetic studies of variola virus have produced conflicting age estimates: analyses that account for how a virus's mutation rate can appear to change over different timescales estimate the virus emerged more than 3,800 years ago, consistent with the Egyptian mummy evidence, while earlier, simpler molecular-clock estimates had suggested a much more recent origin.
--- fact 3
Ancient DNA recovered from Viking-age skeletons in 2020 confirmed multiple extinct strains of variola circulating in northern Europe around a thousand years ago, pushing back the confirmed genetic record beyond the previously oldest known case, a 17th-century mummy.
--- fact 4
Smallpox killed a large share of those it infected and left many survivors permanently scarred or blind; it played a documented role in demographic collapse among Indigenous peoples of the Americas after European contact, spread as part of the Columbian Exchange, though the death tolls attributed to it are difficult to separate from other introduced diseases.
--- fact 5
Edward Jenner's 1796 demonstration that cowpox exposure protected against smallpox led to the first vaccine, and a coordinated global vaccination campaign led by the World Health Organization eradicated smallpox in the wild by 1980, the only human disease eliminated this way to date.
--- fact 6
Two declared stocks of the virus remain in secure laboratories in the United States and Russia for research purposes, and the question of destroying them remains debated.
--- context
Variola virus is implicated in the demographic devastation that followed the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] and, by some accounts, in the coercive labor systems tied to the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] era. Variola Virus sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] (the traditionally dated Ramesses V case) and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer records smallpox's toll, across three thousand years and every continent it reached, as suffering, and notes that this pathogen is the one case in the record where the species deliberately erased another species from the wild, a form of sanctioned harm turned against a disease rather than a rival group. The gap between the confident-sounding pharaoh's scars and the genuinely unresolved genetic dating shows how a claim widely repeated in popular accounts can rest on evidence the specialists themselves still argue over. The reading would weaken if the Ramesses V lesions are shown by future study to have an unrelated cause.
--- question 1
Do the Ramesses V mummy lesions in fact represent smallpox, and if not, what is the true oldest confirmed case?
--- question 2
How much of the population collapse of Indigenous American peoples after 1492 can be attributed specifically to smallpox rather than to other introduced diseases and violence?

=== species/vibrio-cholerae
title: Vibrio cholerae
type: species
date: 1817
themes: earth-systems, trade
src: 05a667c2be37
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Vibrio cholerae is the bacterium that causes cholera, likely long endemic to the Ganges Delta of Bengal, which broke out as the first of seven documented global pandemics in 1817 and reshaped 19th-century ideas about disease and sanitation.
--- fact 1
The first cholera pandemic began in 1817 near Jessore in the Ganges Delta of Bengal, apparently linked to contaminated rice, and spread along trade and military routes through South and Southeast Asia to the Middle East, eastern Africa and the Mediterranean coast before dying down by 1824, possibly checked by a severe winter that killed the bacteria in water supplies.
--- fact 2
Six further cholera pandemics followed between the 1820s and the mid-20th century, and a seventh, caused by the El Tor biotype, began in 1961 and continues to cause outbreaks in parts of Africa, Asia and the Caribbean.
--- fact 3
Vibrio cholerae spreads mainly through water or food contaminated with the feces of infected people, causing severe watery diarrhea that can kill through dehydration within hours if untreated.
--- fact 4
During the 1854 cholera outbreak in London's Soho district, the physician John Snow mapped cases by address and traced them to a single contaminated water pump, evidence that helped establish waterborne transmission before the germ theory of disease was widely accepted.
--- fact 5
Oral rehydration therapy, developed and proven effective against cholera in the 1960s, dramatically reduced mortality from the disease by replacing fluids and salts faster than the body loses them.
--- fact 6
Cholera remains endemic in parts of South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa and Haiti, where outbreaks are strongly associated with disrupted sanitation and water systems after conflict or natural disaster.
--- context
Cholera's first pandemic spread through the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] and the colonial administrative and trade routes of the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]]. Vibrio cholerae sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer records the deaths of each cholera pandemic, particularly among the poor whose water supplies carried the greatest risk, as suffering shaped as much by infrastructure and inequality as by the bacterium itself. Snow's map of the Soho outbreak shows pattern-taming applied directly to a claim-line as narrow as a single street pump, a case where careful record-keeping preceded, rather than followed, an understanding of the underlying mechanism. That the disease can be effectively treated with little more than sugar, salt and clean water reveals how unevenly memory-outsourcing, in this case medical knowledge, has been distributed to the places that need it most. The reading would weaken if evidence shows earlier undocumented pandemics of comparable scale that the historical record has simply lost.
--- question 1
How long was cholera endemic in the Ganges Delta before the trade and troop movements of 1817 turned it into a global pandemic?
--- question 2
Why has access to a cheap, effective treatment not eliminated cholera mortality in the regions where it remains endemic?

=== species/yersinia-pestis
title: Yersinia pestis
type: species
date: c. 3500 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade
src: 6fc6debf1e59
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Yersinia pestis is the bacterium that causes plague, first documented in human remains from Eurasia around 5,000 years ago and responsible for the Plague of Justinian, the Black Death and repeated later outbreaks.
--- fact 1
Ancient DNA recovered from hunter-gatherer cemeteries in southeastern Siberia shows Yersinia pestis infection dating to roughly 5,500 years ago, among the oldest confirmed human cases so far identified; other Eurasian sites, including in Sweden, place infection at roughly 4,900 to 5,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
These early strains differed genetically from later pandemic forms; one line of evidence suggests an early strain could not yet infect the digestive tract of fleas the way later strains did, which would have limited how efficiently it spread.
--- fact 3
Yersinia pestis caused the Plague of Justinian, beginning in 541 CE, and the Black Death, which swept Eurasia and North Africa from 1346 to 1353; further waves of plague recurred for centuries afterward.
--- fact 4
The bacterium is usually transmitted to humans through the bite of infected fleas that have fed on rodents, or directly between people in its pneumonic form; historically, black rats and other rodents served as reservoirs and amplifying hosts.
--- fact 5
Modern genomic studies of ancient and modern strains suggest at least two genetically distinct early lineages spread across large distances in prehistory, occupying different ecological niches before later strains became the ancestors of historical pandemics.
--- fact 6
Plague still occurs in isolated outbreaks today, chiefly in parts of Africa, Asia and the Americas, and is treatable with antibiotics when caught early.
--- context
Yersinia pestis caused the [[event/black-death|Black Death]], spread in part along the trade corridors of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], and some genetic evidence connects a later plague lineage to the era of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]]. Yersinia pestis sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (its earliest confirmed ancient cases) and the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer records the deaths this bacterium has caused, across five millennia and several pandemics, as suffering rather than as a neutral statistic. Yersinia pestis is a case where a single organism has repeatedly ridden the species' own exchange-webs, from Neolithic contact between distant groups to the caravan and shipping routes of later empires, turning connection itself into a hazard. That the earliest known strains could not yet spread efficiently through fleas suggests the pathogen's own tool-lineage of transmission evolved over thousands of years alongside its hosts. The reading would weaken if the early Eurasian cases prove to have been isolated events with no lasting lineage connection to later pandemic strains.
--- question 1
How closely related are the Stone Age Eurasian strains to the lineages behind the Justinianic Plague and the Black Death?
--- question 2
How did Yersinia pestis's ability to infect fleas evolve, and when did it become an efficient epidemic pathogen?

=== place/aksum
title: Aksum
type: place
date: c. 50 CE
themes: trade, religion, art
src: 461066d5f040
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Aksum is a town in the Tigray region of northern Ethiopia that was the capital of the Kingdom of Aksum, a trading state active from about the 1st century CE to the 7th century and later, known for its stone stelae, coinage and early adoption of Christianity.
--- fact 1
Aksum lies in the highlands of northern Ethiopia, and the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek trade guide of about the 1st century CE, mentions Aksum and its port Adulis on the Red Sea.
--- fact 2
Aksumite rulers minted gold, silver and bronze coins from about the late 3rd century CE, and King Ezana adopted Christianity at a date placed variously between about 325 and 340 CE; coins struck afterward bear the cross.
--- fact 3
Ezana's inscriptions were written in Ge'ez, Sabaean and Greek.
--- fact 4
Aksum's monolithic stelae mark burial sites; the largest, about 33 meters high, fell and broke, and a 24-meter stele taken to Rome by Italian forces in 1937 was returned in 2005 and re-erected in 2008.
--- fact 5
Ethiopian Christian tradition holds that the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion at Aksum houses the Ark of the Covenant; the claim cannot be examined.
--- fact 6
Aksum declined from the 7th century, and proposed causes include changes in Red Sea trade after the rise of Islam, environmental stress and internal conflict; the causes are debated.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Aksum in 1980.
--- context
Aksum was the capital of the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]], which had contacts with [[culture/kingdom-of-saba|Kingdom of Saba]] across the Red Sea; its stele was taken during the [[event/second-italo-ethiopian-war|Second Italo-Ethiopian War]]'s Italian occupation. Later Ethiopian rulers built the [[place/rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela|Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela]]. Aksum sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Aksum shows a state announcing its meaning-engine through stone and metal: giant stelae over graves, and coins that carried a cross after Ezana's conversion, so that a portable object spread the ruler's story-glue across the trading network. The taking and return of the stele show that monuments become claim-lines of another kind when moved. The Ark tradition is a belief held by a community that cannot be tested, and the Observer records it as belief, neither endorsing nor dismissing it. The reading would weaken if the coin evidence proves too sparse to date Ezana's change of religion.
--- question 1
Why did Aksum decline from the 7th century?
--- question 2
How large was Aksum's population at its height?

=== place/ayutthaya
title: Ayutthaya
type: place
date: 1351 – 1767
themes: trade, war, religion
src: ffce8cfcb2eb
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ayutthaya is a city on an island at the meeting of three rivers in central Thailand, traditionally founded in 1351, the capital of the Ayutthaya Kingdom until the Burmese army destroyed it in 1767, and a major trading port of Southeast Asia.
--- fact 1
Ayutthaya lies on an island at the confluence of the Chao Phraya, Lopburi and Pa Sak rivers, and is traditionally said to have been founded in 1351 (by convention, 4 March 1351) by King U Thong (Ramathibodi I), though the settlement may be older.
--- fact 2
In the 1430s Ayutthaya's forces attacked Angkor and installed a short-lived puppet ruler there, though they did not sack the city.
--- fact 3
Chinese, Japanese, Persian, Indian and Malay merchants traded at Ayutthaya; the Portuguese, in 1511, were the first Europeans to reach it, the Dutch East India Company opened a trading post in 1608, and King Narai exchanged embassies with the French court in the 1680s.
--- fact 4
European visitors in the 17th century described a city of canals, palaces and many temples, and some estimates put its population at several hundred thousand at its height.
--- fact 5
A Burmese army besieged Ayutthaya for about 14 months and took it in April 1767, burning much of the city; many thousands of captives were taken to Burma, and much of the kingdom's written record was lost.
--- fact 6
Taksin re-established a Siamese state after 1767 and the capital moved to Thonburi and then to Bangkok in 1782.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Historic City of Ayutthaya in 1991.
--- context

--- observer
The Observer notes that Ayutthaya was a trading capital built on an island where rivers and canals served as moat, road and marketplace at once, and where merchants of many origins met under one ruler. The destruction of 1767 removed much of the kingdom's own written record, so a large part of what is known about it comes from foreign visitors' accounts, a loss of memory-outsourcing that shapes how the city is understood. The Observer records the taking of captives as suffering as well as loss of skill and labor. The reading would weaken if surviving Thai and Burmese records prove richer than assumed.

=== place/baghdad
title: Baghdad
type: place
date: 762 CE
themes: science, trade, war
src: b2782d525633
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Baghdad is a city on the Tigris River in Iraq, founded in 762 CE by the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur as a round capital and, for about five centuries, the seat of the Abbasid caliphs and a leading center of learning.
--- fact 1
Baghdad was founded in 762 by the caliph al-Mansur as a round city called Madinat al-Salam ("City of Peace"), near the older Sasanian capital Ctesiphon; no part of the Round City survives above ground.
--- fact 2
Baghdad became the center of the Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom, and paper mills were operating there by the 790s.
--- fact 3
Population estimates for Baghdad in the 9th and 10th centuries run from several hundred thousand to over a million, which would have made it one of the largest cities in the world.
--- fact 4
Baghdad remained the seat of the Abbasid caliphs until 1258, when Mongol forces under Hulagu took the city after a siege and killed the caliph al-Musta'sim; chronicles report figures ranging from 200,000 (Hulagu's own estimate) to as much as 2,000,000 (in Muslim sources), figures modern historians regard as unreliable and likely inflated by later epidemics, though the massacre and destruction were severe.
--- fact 5
Baghdad was under Ottoman rule from 1534, with Safavid periods before and after, until British forces captured it in 1917; it became the capital of the Kingdom of Iraq in 1921.
--- fact 6
In April 2003, after the US-led invasion, looting of the Iraq National Museum removed about 15,000 objects, some of which have since been recovered.
--- context
Baghdad was the capital of the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] and the home of the [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]]; it was taken in the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]] and later ruled by the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Baghdad was a planned city that became a knot in an exchange-web: books, translators, paper and scholars from several languages made it a place where Greek, Persian and Indian sources were gathered and rewritten, memory-outsourcing at scale. The Round City itself is now known only from texts, because later buildings covered it, while the losses of 1258 and 2003 show that stored knowledge is exposed to political violence. Historians debate how far the court and how far private patrons drove the translation work, and the reading would weaken if private patronage proves to have been the larger force.

=== place/bukhara
title: Bukhara
type: place
date: c. 500 BCE
themes: trade, religion, science
src: 85e2bea3a983
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Bukhara is a city in the Zarafshan oasis of Uzbekistan, a Sogdian trading city, capital of the Samanid dynasty, and for more than a thousand years a center of Islamic learning on the Silk Roads.
--- fact 1
Bukhara has been settled for a long time; the city celebrated 2,500 years in 1997 on a traditional dating, while archaeological evidence places its walled Sogdian settlement by about the 6th century BCE.
--- fact 2
Bukhara was a Sogdian city when Arab armies took it in 709 to 710, and the scholar Muhammad al-Bukhari, compiler of a major collection of hadith, was born there in 810.
--- fact 3
Under the Samanid dynasty (819 to 999), Bukhara was a capital of learning and of Persian literature; the physician and philosopher Ibn Sina was born near it in 980, and the Samanid mausoleum was built in the late 9th to 10th century.
--- fact 4
The Mongol army under Chinggis Khan took Bukhara in 1220; the city was burned and many of its people were killed or enslaved, though the number is unknown.
--- fact 5
The Kalyan Minaret, built in 1127, still stands, and Bukhara was the capital of the Emirate of Bukhara (1785 to 1920), which became a Russian protectorate by 1873 after Russia conquered key Bukharan territories in 1868.
--- fact 6
The Red Army took Bukhara in 1920, and the citadel (Ark) was destroyed after four days of fighting.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Historic Centre of Bukhara in 1993.
--- context
Bukhara was a [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] city, paired in later history with [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]], and it fell to the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]] after coming under the world of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Bukhara was taken by a long series of powers, Sogdian, Arab, Samanid, Mongol, Uzbek, Russian and Soviet, yet regained its role as a center of learning after most of them, which suggests that scholars, patrons and trade routes were rebuilt faster than the buildings. Its standing as a place of Islamic scholarship was a story-glue that outlasted each ruler. The reading would weaken if the record shows long gaps in scholarly output after the sack of 1220.
