=== event/origin-of-birds
title: Origin of Birds
type: event
date: 160 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 2c5b97910207
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Birds evolved from small feathered theropod dinosaurs in the Jurassic, with the earliest bird-like fossils about 160 to 150 million years old. Birds are the only dinosaur lineage that survived to the present.
--- fact 1
Birds share many features with theropod dinosaurs, including hollow bones, a wishbone and a three-fingered hand. Feathered non-avian dinosaurs found in China from the 1990s onward strengthened the link.
--- fact 2
Feathers arose before flight, probably for insulation or display. The theropod Anchiornis, about 160 million years old, and [[species/archaeopteryx|Archaeopteryx]], about 150 million years old, both had feathered wings.
--- fact 3
Early Cretaceous fossils from China, about 125 million years old, show many forms of early birds, including toothless-beaked Confuciusornis.
--- fact 4
How flight began, from the ground up or from the trees down, is debated, and some small feathered dinosaurs had four wings.
--- fact 5
Only the lineage of modern birds survived the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], and living birds diversified after it.
--- context
The Origin of Birds lies within the [[era/jurassic-period|Jurassic Period]] and the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]] and belongs to the story of the [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a category as familiar as bird sits inside a category as remote as dinosaur, a reminder that names for kinds of animals are cuts across a continuous line of descent. The reading concerns naming only, and it would not be affected by the debate over where flight began.
--- question 1
Where in theropod evolution did powered flight first arise?
--- question 2
Which Jurassic fossils count as birds and which as close relatives?

=== event/origin-of-eukaryotes
title: Origin of Eukaryotes
type: event
date: 2 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: dfb56e57b875
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Eukaryotes, the cells with nuclei and internal organelles from which all animals, plants, and fungi are built, arose roughly 2 billion years ago, with very wide uncertainty about the date.
--- fact 1
Mitochondria descend from bacteria taken into a host cell, a process called endosymbiosis, which is widely accepted.
--- fact 2
Chloroplasts likewise descend from cyanobacteria that became resident in some eukaryotic cells.
--- fact 3
Genomic studies place the host cell within the Archaea, with the Asgard archaea often named as the closest relatives.
--- fact 4
The oldest unambiguous eukaryote fossils, large organic-walled microfossils, are more than 1.65 billion years old; older candidates such as Grypania (about 1.87 billion years) are less convincing than younger, larger forms.
--- fact 5
Molecular-clock estimates for the last common ancestor of living eukaryotes vary widely.
--- context
The origin of eukaryotes followed the oxygen rise of the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] and made large multicellular bodies possible, leading eventually to the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]]. Origin of Eukaryotes sits in the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that complex life arose through a merger rather than a single lineage inventing new parts alone. Cooperation between unlike partners sits at the base of every body, including the human one. A different origin would be indicated if strong evidence showed the organelles were built internally without symbiosis.
--- question 1
Did the host cell acquire the mitochondrion before or after gaining complexity?
--- question 2
How old is the last eukaryotic common ancestor?

=== event/origin-of-homo-sapiens
title: Origin of Homo sapiens
type: event
date: c. 300 ka
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 584204583308
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anatomically modern humans, Homo sapiens, emerged in Africa by about 300,000 years ago through a gradual, continent-wide process.
--- fact 1
The Jebel Irhoud fossils in Morocco are about 315,000 years old (315 plus or minus 34 thousand years) and show a modern face and teeth with a more archaic braincase.
--- fact 2
Fossils from Omo Kibish (Ethiopia) are at least 233,000 years old, dated against a volcanic ash layer.
--- fact 3
Some scholars describe the process as 'African multiregionalism', with several connected populations contributing.
--- fact 4
The regional tag here is approximate: Jebel Irhoud is in North Africa, while the other main sites are in East and southern Africa.
--- context
The origin belongs to the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and precedes the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]]. See [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' origin is not a single place or moment. It is a widespread, interbreeding population that became recognizably modern over tens of thousands of years.
--- question 1
Was there one core population, or many linked ones?

=== event/origin-of-language
title: Origin of Language
type: event
date: 150 ka
themes: language, kinship
src: 7c950eaae89c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
When spoken language began is unknown, because neither speech nor the brain circuitry behind grammar fossilizes. Published estimates for when the capacity existed span from about 2 million to 50,000 years ago, depending on whether the evidence is genetic, anatomical, or archaeological.
--- fact 1
Neanderthals share two speech-linked changes to the FOXP2 gene with modern humans, once read as pushing language capacity back 300,000 to 400,000 years; a larger 2018 study found no sign of recent human-specific selection there, and FOXP2 is now seen as one gene among many.
--- fact 2
A Neanderthal hyoid bone from Kebara Cave, about 60,000 years old, closely resembles a modern one, cited as evidence of speech-capable anatomy; others argue vocal-tract proportions still limited the range of sounds.
--- fact 3
Skull-base evidence shows a gradual trend toward a modern vocal tract, from no such change in Australopithecus to a near-modern shape in Homo heidelbergensis about 600,000 years ago, cited for slow change.
--- fact 4
A 2025 genetic synthesis argued the capacity for language existed by the earliest known split among Homo sapiens populations, about 135,000 years ago, with cultural use possibly beginning later, once symbolic material such as engraved ochre appears.
--- fact 5
Researchers are divided between a gradualist view, where language built up over a million years or more, and a saltational view where syntax arose abruptly; a separate debate concerns gesture versus vocalization as the original channel.
--- context
The question overlaps the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and the biology of [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] and [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]]. The [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]] and the [[event/control-of-fire|Control of Fire]] are often cited as indirect evidence without settling when language began. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer treats every date proposed here as a guess dressed as a measurement, since sound-code leaves nothing behind for excavation. The gap between the earliest proposed biological readiness and the earliest confident signs of symbolic behavior is tens of thousands of years, and the Observer does not know whether that gap is real or an accident of what survives. The reading would weaken if a non-circumstantial dating method were ever found.
--- question 1
Did having the capacity for language mean a population spoke habitually, or could capacity precede use by tens of thousands of years?
--- question 2
Did Neanderthals have language, or only a simpler form of it?

=== event/origin-of-life
title: Origin of Life
type: event
date: 3.8 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c3902711786b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Life on Earth arose at least 3.5 billion years ago and possibly earlier. How it began is not yet known.
--- fact 1
Stromatolites (layered structures made by microbes) in the Dresser Formation of Western Australia's Pilbara region are about 3.48 billion years old and are described as the oldest convincing evidence of life.
--- fact 2
Claims for older evidence are debated. In 2016 researchers reported stromatolites about 3.7 billion years old at Isua in Greenland, and a paper in the same journal soon disputed the observations. Later studies disagree on whether the structures are biological.
--- fact 3
A 2024 molecular-clock study estimated that the last universal common ancestor, the organism from which all living cells descend, lived about 4.2 billion years ago. That ancestor was not the first life, and the estimate carries large uncertainty.
--- fact 4
Leading hypotheses for how life began include hydrothermal vents, shallow pools, and an early RNA-based stage; none is settled.
--- context
The origin of life marks the transition from the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] to the biology-dominated [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]]. Its later effects include the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]].
--- observer
The Observer regards this as the least understood event in the record. It is also the one that made every later subject possible, including the Observer's own field.
--- question 1
Did life begin once or several times?
--- question 2
Where did it begin?
--- question 3
How reliable are molecular-clock ages for a common ancestor that predates the fossil record by a large margin?

=== event/origin-of-mammals
title: Origin of Mammals
type: event
date: 225 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 9b30f1564085
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The origin of mammals was the gradual evolution of mammals from mammal-line reptiles called synapsids, with the oldest candidates from the Late Triassic, roughly 225 to 205 million years ago. The date depends on how a mammal is defined.
--- fact 1
Mammals descend from synapsids, which dominated Permian land life, through the therapsids and then the cynodonts of the Triassic.
--- fact 2
The bones that formed the jaw joint of reptile-like ancestors became the middle-ear bones of mammals, a change traced through fossils of Late Triassic and Early Jurassic mammal relatives.
--- fact 3
The oldest fossils that are candidates for mammals or their close relatives, the mammaliaforms, are about 225 to 205 million years old. Which of them are true mammals depends on the definition used.
--- fact 4
Mesozoic mammals were mostly small, though a few, such as Repenomamus from China, about 125 million years old, reached the size of a badger.
--- fact 5
Hair, milk and warm-bloodedness arose along the lineage, but soft tissues are rarely preserved, so the order is uncertain.
--- context
The Origin of Mammals falls in the [[era/triassic-period|Triassic Period]] and the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]], alongside the rise of [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]], and its lineage grew out of the mammal-line reptiles of the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]]. Mammals expanded after the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that hearing, one of the species' most ordinary senses, depends on bones that once formed the jaw joint of a reptile-like ancestor. New structures are built from old parts, and the Observer treats that as a general rule of the record, not a special case. The reading would weaken if the ear bones proved to have arisen without any earlier function in the jaw.
--- question 1
When did the first true mammals appear, given how the definition moves the date?
--- question 2
In what order did hair, milk and warm-bloodedness arise?

=== event/origin-of-multicellularity
title: Origin of Multicellularity
type: event
date: 1.05 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 7c1d3292e12f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Multicellularity, in which cells stay attached, divide labour and reproduce as a unit, arose independently many times. The clearest early fossil evidence is about 1.05 billion years old, and earlier claims are disputed.
--- fact 1
Multicellularity evolved independently in bacteria, in several groups of algae, in fungi, in plants and in animals; estimates for eukaryotes alone run to more than twenty separate origins.
--- fact 2
Older claims include the Francevillian biota of Gabon, about 2.1 billion years old, and the coiled fossil Grypania, about 1.9 billion years old; their interpretation is disputed.
--- fact 3
The red alga Bangiomorpha, dated in different studies to between roughly 1.05 and 1.2 billion years ago, shows differentiated cells and sexual reproduction.
--- fact 4
Experiments have produced simple multicellular clusters from single-celled algae and yeast within weeks to months. The step to complex bodies with many cell types is much rarer.
--- fact 5
Animals evolved multicellularity separately from plants, and large soft-bodied animals appear in the Ediacaran; see [[species/ediacaran-biota|Ediacaran Biota]] and [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]].
--- context
The Origin of Multicellularity followed the [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]] and overlaps the [[event/the-boring-billion|The Boring Billion]] in the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]]. It belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a body is cooperation among cells that share ancestry, held together by mechanisms that suppress cheating. Whether that is a useful comparison for human cooperation is a question the Observer treats as open; the analogy is offered as a way to look, not as an explanation. The reading would weaken if it obscured more than it clarified about either case.
--- question 1
Which of the early fossil claims are truly multicellular?
--- question 2
What conditions favoured the origin of animal multicellularity?

=== event/origin-of-photosynthesis
title: Origin of Photosynthesis
type: event
date: 3.5 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 3c9969dc2faa
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Photosynthesis, the use of light to power the making of organic molecules, arose in bacteria some time in the Archean, possibly as early as 3.5 billion years ago. The date of the first oxygen-producing form is uncertain and disputed.
--- fact 1
The first photosynthesis probably did not release oxygen. Anoxygenic forms in some living bacteria use hydrogen, sulfide or dissolved iron in place of water.
--- fact 2
Oxygen-producing photosynthesis is known from [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]]. Estimates for its origin run from about 3.5 to about 2.4 billion years ago, and the earliest unambiguous cyanobacterial fossils are about 2.15 billion years old.
--- fact 3
Biomarker molecules once cited as evidence of cyanobacteria at 2.7 billion years were later shown to be younger contamination.
--- fact 4
Layered [[species/stromatolites|Stromatolites]] about 3.48 billion years old from the Dresser Formation of Western Australia may record microbial mats, possibly photosynthetic; some researchers dispute that they are biological.
--- fact 5
Early oxygen was consumed by dissolved iron and other chemicals before it built up in the air; see [[event/banded-iron-formations|Banded Iron Formations]] and [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]].
--- context
The Origin of Photosynthesis follows the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]] within the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and sets up the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] of the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a way of getting energy from light, invented by microbes, ended up setting the chemistry that everything larger would run on. What stands out is the very long delay between the invention and its planetary effects, and the date matters less to this reading than that delay. The reading would weaken if oxygen-producing photosynthesis were shown to have begun only shortly before the oxygen rise.
--- question 1
When did oxygen-producing photosynthesis first evolve?
--- question 2
What delayed the accumulation of oxygen after it began?

=== event/out-of-africa-dispersal
title: Out of Africa Dispersal
type: event
date: c. 70 – 50 ka
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: 24b98599a481
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Homo sapiens spread out of Africa and across the world. Genetic studies trace most present-day non-African ancestry to a single major dispersal roughly 70,000 to 50,000 years ago, though earlier and smaller dispersals also left traces and the exact timing is debated.
--- fact 1
Genetic studies published in 2016 concluded that most non-African ancestry comes from one major dispersal about 50,000 to 72,000 years ago. Genetic dating is imperfect, and one climate study favored a window of 80,000 to 100,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
Earlier, smaller dispersals occurred. Homo sapiens fossils at Skhul and Qafzeh in Israel are about 90,000 to 120,000 years old. A fossil upper jaw from Misliya Cave in Israel was dated in 2018 to 177,000 to 194,000 years ago, but a published comment disputes that dating. A separate genetic signal suggests an exodus about 120,000 years ago that left about 2 percent of Papuan genomes.
--- fact 3
Modern humans reached Australia and New Guinea at least about 50,000 years ago, and possibly about 65,000 years ago, a date that is debated; see [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]].
--- fact 4
Present-day non-African populations carry up to about 2 percent Neanderthal ancestry. Oceanian populations carry much more Denisovan ancestry, up to about 5 percent in some.
--- fact 5
Both coastal and inland routes have been proposed.
--- context
The dispersal ends the isolation of human populations; see [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] and [[species/denisovans|Denisovans]]. It sets up the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]].
--- observer
The Observer regards this as the most remarkable feat of the Paleolithic: a single species occupying deserts, tundra, rainforest, and islands, mostly by learning rather than by biological change.
--- question 1
How many dispersals were there, and by which routes? The fossil record shows repeated early departures, while the genetic record points to one main wave, and the two are not yet fully reconciled.

=== event/paleocene-eocene-thermal-maximum
title: Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum
type: event
date: 56 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 9ef23311112a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum was a burst of global warming about 56 million years ago, in which a large release of carbon raised global temperatures by roughly 5 to 6 degrees Celsius for about 200,000 years.
--- fact 1
Deep-sea sediments record a sharp negative excursion in carbon isotopes, showing a large input of carbon to the ocean and atmosphere.
--- fact 2
The event lasted on the order of 200,000 years including recovery, with the onset taking only a few thousand years.
--- fact 3
The ocean acidified and many deep-sea foraminifera species went extinct.
--- fact 4
Proposed carbon sources include volcanic activity in the North Atlantic and the release of methane from seafloor deposits; the mix is debated.
--- fact 5
Several mammal groups, including early primates, horses, and artiodactyls, appear or spread across the Northern Hemisphere near its start.
--- context
The event is the best-studied ancient analogue for [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]] and for the carbon release of the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]. It occurred about ten million years after the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]. Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum sits in the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Earth has run this experiment before, without observers, and kept a record in mud. The present differs in that a species is both causing the change and measuring it. Studies generally find present emissions to be much faster than those at the onset of the PETM.
--- question 1
How much of the carbon was volcanic and how much came from methane?

=== event/partition-of-india
title: Partition of India
type: event
date: 1947
themes: war, kinship
src: 39eb251e787f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In August 1947, British India was divided into the independent states of India and Pakistan, and the drawing of new borders through Punjab and Bengal caused mass migration and violence.
--- fact 1
Independence and partition took effect on 14 and 15 August 1947.
--- fact 2
The boundary was drawn by a commission led by Cyril Radcliffe in a matter of weeks; he later admitted that he had relied on out-of-date maps and census materials.
--- fact 3
Estimates suggest that 12 to 20 million people were displaced.
--- fact 4
Estimates of deaths vary widely: 1 to 2 million is commonly cited, some sources give as many as 3 million, and about 15 million people were displaced.
--- fact 5
Mahatma Gandhi opposed partition and was assassinated in January 1948.
--- context
Partition was a result of the end of British rule and is a case of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] in which a line on a map divided communities. [[person/mahatma-gandhi|Mahatma Gandhi]] opposed the division. Partition of India sits in the [[era/information-age|Information Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a line drawn on a map in a few weeks required more than ten million people to relocate. This is a stark case of a claim-line generating movement, violence, and memory long after the ink dried.
--- question 1
How many people died?

=== event/peace-of-westphalia
title: Peace of Westphalia
type: event
date: 1648
themes: war, religion
src: 09e985e9806a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 consisted of the treaties of Osnabruck and Munster, which ended the Thirty Years' War and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic.
--- fact 1
The peace was negotiated from 1644 in the Westphalian cities of Osnabruck and Munster; the Spanish-Dutch treaty was signed on 30 January 1648, and the treaty involving the Holy Roman emperor, the German princes, France, and Sweden on 24 October 1648.
--- fact 2
Spain recognized the independence of the Dutch Republic.
--- fact 3
The settlement extended the Peace of Augsburg's principle, that rulers determine the religion of their territory, to include Calvinists.
--- fact 4
It is often cited as the origin of the modern system of sovereign states, though many historians regard this as a later simplification.
--- fact 5
It reduced the authority of the Holy Roman Emperor over the German princes.
--- context
The settlement ended the [[event/thirty-years-war|Thirty Years' War]] and the religious conflicts of the [[event/protestant-reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. Its later reputation as the birth of state sovereignty is discussed in [[observer-note/the-fiction-of-claim-lines|The Fiction of Claim-Lines]]. Peace of Westphalia sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a treaty that mainly solved a set of local problems later became a founding myth of the international order. Stories about founding moments tend to be assembled afterward.
--- question 1
Did Westphalia actually create the sovereign state system?

=== event/peopling-of-europe-by-homo-sapiens
title: Peopling of Europe by Homo sapiens
type: event
date: c. 45 ka
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: ffd0ae06f91d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Homo sapiens reached Europe by about 45,000 years ago, and possibly earlier, entering a continent already occupied by Neanderthals. The earliest securely dated remains, from Bacho Kiro Cave in Bulgaria and Ranis in Germany, are about 45,000 years old.
--- fact 1
At Bacho Kiro Cave, Bulgaria, Homo sapiens fossils dated to about 46,000 to 44,000 years ago were found with Initial Upper Paleolithic stone tools.
--- fact 2
At Ranis, Germany, bone fragments directly dated in 2024 to about 45,000 years ago came with leaf-shaped stone points also known from Britain, which shows an early reach into northern latitudes.
--- fact 3
At Grotte Mandrin, France, a child's tooth and distinctive stone points dated to about 54,000 years ago were attributed to Homo sapiens in 2022. The occupation appears to have been brief, and some researchers find the attribution unconvincing.
--- fact 4
Neanderthals and Homo sapiens overlapped in Europe for several thousand years, and some early Europeans had Neanderthal ancestors only a few generations back; see [[event/neanderthal-extinction|Neanderthal Extinction]] and [[event/interbreeding-of-homo-sapiens-with-neanderthals-and-denisovans|Interbreeding of Homo sapiens with Neanderthals and Denisovans]].
--- fact 5
Proposed routes include the Danube corridor and the Mediterranean coast; the evidence does not settle which was used.
--- context
The peopling of Europe follows the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]] and belongs to the [[era/upper-paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]], with new stone-tool industries such as the Aurignacian and the [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]] seen at [[place/chauvet-cave|Chauvet Cave]]. It overlaps the last [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] and precedes the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' arrival in Europe looks less like one march than like several small, sometimes brief settlements in land already held by another human species. The Observer takes the Mandrin tooth and the Bacho Kiro jaws as reminders that "first arrival" describes the evidence found so far, not the past itself. The reading would weaken if the earliest sites proved to belong to one continuous, growing population.
--- question 1
Did the 54,000-year-old occupation at Mandrin belong to Homo sapiens?
--- question 2
How much ancestry from the earliest Europeans survives in later Europeans?

=== event/peopling-of-sahul
title: Peopling of Sahul
type: event
date: c. 50 ka
themes: technology, earth-systems, kinship
src: d001359cafc8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Modern humans reached Sahul, the Pleistocene landmass joining Australia and New Guinea, at least 50,000 years ago and possibly about 65,000 years ago.
--- fact 1
Reaching Sahul required crossing open sea in the Wallacea island chain, making it among the earliest known deliberate sea crossings.
--- fact 2
Rock-shelter finds at Madjedbebe in Arnhem Land have been dated to about 65,000 years ago, a date that is debated; about 50,000 years ago is widely accepted.
--- fact 3
Human remains at Lake Mungo are about 40,000 years old.
--- fact 4
Many giant marsupials disappeared within several thousand years of human arrival; the relative roles of people and climate are debated.
--- context
The settlement followed the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]] and founded the ancestry of the [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]]. Compare the later [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species crossed open water tens of thousands of years before it built anything that could be called a city. Boat-building and planning are far older than farming.
--- question 1
How early did the first boats cross Wallacea?

=== event/peopling-of-southeast-asia-and-wallacea
title: Peopling of Southeast Asia and Wallacea
type: event
date: c. 86 – 42 ka
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: 459d58538fbe
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Homo sapiens reached mainland Southeast Asia by about 86,000 to 68,000 years ago and the Wallacean islands beyond it, such as Sulawesi and Timor, by about 51,000 to 42,000 years ago. This dispersal supplied the route and rough timing for the further crossing into Sahul.
--- fact 1
Fossils from Tam Pà Ling cave, Laos, dated in 2023, extend Homo sapiens presence in mainland Southeast Asia to roughly 86,000 to 68,000 years ago; earlier work at the site had suggested a younger range.
--- fact 2
Human teeth from Lida Ajer, Sumatra, are dated to about 73,000 to 63,000 years ago, but only indirectly, from the surrounding sediment and animal teeth rather than the human teeth themselves.
--- fact 3
The Niah Cave "Deep Skull," Borneo, once disputed as possibly disturbed, is now dated to about 45,000 to 39,000 years ago by direct dating of the bone.
--- fact 4
Within Wallacea, the earliest well-dated evidence is rock art on Sulawesi, at least 51,200 years old, and occupation at Laili Cave and Jerimalai Cave, Timor, about 44,000 to 42,000 years ago.
--- fact 5
These Wallacean dates sit oddly late against the debated claim that Sahul was reached by about 65,000 years ago, which would require an earlier, undiscovered Wallacean crossing; a floor of about 50,000 years ago is more secure.
--- fact 6
A separate, non-sapiens hominin, Homo luzonensis, lived on Luzon, Philippines, outside Wallacea proper, and should not be confused with this dispersal.
--- context
This dispersal follows the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]] and feeds into the [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]]. Early modern humans in the region also survived the [[event/toba-eruption|Toba Eruption]]. [[species/homo-floresiensis|Homo floresiensis]], on Flores, is a separate, older hominin lineage. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that crossing into Wallacea meant repeated open-water voyages made without metal, writing, or domesticated animals, thousands of years before farming anywhere. The dates keep moving as methods improve, which the Observer treats as ordinary science rather than a settled record. The reading would weaken if a securely dated site put Homo sapiens in Wallacea well before 51,000 years ago.
--- question 1
Was Sahul reached by about 65,000 years ago, and if so, where is the matching early Wallacean evidence?
--- question 2
Did the Tam Pà Ling population leave descendants among later Southeast Asian peoples?

=== event/peopling-of-the-americas
title: Peopling of the Americas
type: event
date: c. 23 – 13 ka
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: f0538815e7c3
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Humans reached the Americas from northeastern Asia by at least 15,000 years ago, and possibly much earlier. The earliest date is contested.
--- fact 1
Footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, have been dated to about 21,000 to 23,000 years ago. The first dates used seeds of an aquatic plant, which critics said could read too old. A 2023 study radiocarbon-dated terrestrial pollen from the same layers and applied luminescence dating to the sediments, and reported that the original chronology is robust. Some researchers still question the dating.
--- fact 2
The old 'Clovis-first' model, which put the first arrival about 13,000 years ago, is no longer accepted. Sites such as Cooper's Ferry in Idaho, about 16,000 years old, predate Clovis.
--- fact 3
Two routes are proposed. The inland ice-free corridor is now thought to have opened only about 13,800 years ago, later than the earliest sites, which makes a Pacific coastal route the more likely path for the first arrivals.
--- fact 4
Genetic studies show that Indigenous Americans descend from a mixture of ancestry related to ancient North Eurasians in Siberia and to East Asians. Scholars now generally favor several small migrations over a single event.
--- context
The migration follows the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]], occurred around the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]], and was the last major expansion into unoccupied continents before the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that within a few thousand years humans spread from Alaska to Tierra del Fuego. The pace suggests a species that carries its environment, in the form of knowledge, with it.
--- question 1
When did the first migrants arrive, and by what route? Some proposed sites, such as Chiquihuite Cave in Mexico (about 26,000 years), are disputed because it is not settled that their stones are human-made.

=== event/quaternary-megafauna-extinctions

type: event

themes: earth-systems
src: 8248ce1b4a49
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between roughly 50,000 and 10,000 years ago, a large share of the world's biggest land animals went extinct, with the timing differing by continent and often following human arrival.
--- fact 1
Animals lost include the woolly mammoth, woolly rhinoceros, giant ground sloths, glyptodonts, the giant marsupial Diprotodon, and the giant bird Genyornis.
--- fact 2
Losses were proportionally severest in Australia, the Americas, and northern Eurasia.

--- fact 4
The overkill hypothesis attributes the extinctions mainly to human hunting; the climate hypothesis attributes them to environmental change.
--- fact 5
Recent global analyses link the declines more closely to the expansion of Homo sapiens than to climate change, though regional debate continues.
--- context
The extinctions accompany human expansion into [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]] and the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]], and the retreat of the [[species/woolly-mammoth|Woolly Mammoth]] during the shifts around the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. --- observer
The Observer notes that the same period saw the species arrive on new continents and the largest animals on those continents disappear. Whether the species was the primary cause is contested, but it is the first time its impact on other life was large enough to be a candidate cause.
--- question 1
How much of each regional extinction was due to humans and how much to climate?

=== event/radiation-of-placental-mammals
title: Radiation of Placental Mammals
type: event
date: 66 – 50 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c93d745d2b2d
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The radiation of placental mammals was the rapid diversification of the placental group of mammals after the end-Cretaceous extinction, from about 66 to 50 million years ago. Most living mammals belong to it.
--- fact 1
Placental mammals bear well-developed young nourished by a placenta. Most living mammal species, including rodents, bats, whales, hoofed animals and primates, are placentals.
--- fact 2
In the fossil record, large herbivores and predators appear within a few million years after the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], and many modern orders are recognizable by about 50 million years ago.
--- fact 3
Molecular-clock studies place the origin of the placental orders in the Cretaceous, while the oldest fossils assigned to most modern orders are Paleogene. Explosive, short-fuse and long-fuse models of the timing are all argued.
--- fact 4
The [[event/paleocene-eocene-thermal-maximum|Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum]], about 56 million years ago, coincides with the first appearance in the Northern Hemisphere of primates, horses and artiodactyls.
--- fact 5
Whales evolved from land-dwelling hoofed mammals over about ten million years, from about 50 to 40 million years ago.
--- context
The Radiation of Placental Mammals follows the [[event/origin-of-mammals|Origin of Mammals]] and the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]] and fills much of the [[era/paleogene-period|Paleogene Period]] of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the rise of the mammals followed the loss of the dominant land animals, and that the same pattern of vacated space followed other extinctions. It reads the radiation as a consequence of opening, not of superiority. The reading would weaken if the fossil record showed large placental mammals already common before the extinction.
--- question 1
Did the modern orders arise in the Cretaceous or only after the extinction?
--- question 2
What role did warming episodes such as the thermal maximum play in the radiation?
