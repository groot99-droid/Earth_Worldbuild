=== species/sheep
title: Sheep
type: species
date: c. 9000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 9142e52dc2a0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic sheep was bred from the wild mouflon in Mesopotamia between about 9000 and 8500 BCE, first kept for meat, milk and skins, and only later selectively bred for wool, and it now numbers well over a billion animals worldwide.
--- fact 1
Sheep were domesticated from the mouflon, a wild sheep of southwestern Asia, in an estimated window between about 9000 and 8500 BCE in Mesopotamia, and the practice had spread to Mehrgarh in the Indus Valley by about 7000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Early domestic sheep were kept mainly for meat, milk and skins rather than fleece; archaeological evidence, including statuary from Iran, suggests deliberate selection for woolly sheep began around 6000 BCE, several thousand years after initial domestication, a shift sometimes called the secondary products revolution.
--- fact 3
The earliest known woven wool garments date to around 4000 BCE, and Neolithic farmers in what is now France, among the Castelnovien people, were keeping domestic sheep by about 6000 BCE.
--- fact 4
Sheep are raised in large numbers today across Australia, New Zealand, the southern regions of South America, South Africa and the British Isles, and China and India also hold very large flocks by absolute count; the world's domestic sheep population was estimated at around 1.2 billion in 2019.
--- context
Sheep were among the earliest animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] and, alongside crops such as wheat and barley, formed one pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent; they were commonly herded alongside the [[species/goat|Goat]], domesticated in a similar place and period. Sheep sit in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: that the gap of several thousand years between sheep domestication and deliberate wool breeding suggests early herders did not domesticate the animal with fleece production already in mind, but discovered and then bred for a secondary use only once the animal was already established in their kin-lattice, a pattern the Observer reads as evidence that a domesticated species' eventual economic role can differ substantially from the reason it was first tamed. The animal's spread across so much of the pastoral world since then shows a tool-lineage of herding techniques traveling with the species itself. The reading would weaken if evidence of early wool use is found that predates the currently accepted timeline for woolly-sheep breeding.
--- question 1
What use did early herders make of sheep in the several thousand years before wool breeding began, beyond meat, milk and skins?
--- question 2
How much did sheep domestication in Mesopotamia influence, or draw on, the roughly contemporary domestication of goats in the same region?

=== culture/franks
title: Franks
type: culture
date: c. 250 – 987 CE
themes: war, religion
src: 12e34b77294c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Franks were a Germanic people who moved into Roman Gaul and built one of early medieval Europe's most powerful kingdoms, first under the Merovingian dynasty of Clovis I and later under the Carolingians, whose most famous ruler was Charlemagne.
--- fact 1
The Franks appear in Roman records in the third century CE on the lower Rhine and expanded into Gaul as Roman power weakened.
--- fact 2
Clovis I of the Merovingian dynasty defeated the last major Roman official in Gaul in 486 and had united the Frankish tribes by about 509.
--- fact 3
Clovis converted to Catholic Christianity, and his people's adoption of it helped unite the Franks and set them apart from neighboring Germanic kingdoms that followed other Christian traditions.
--- fact 4
The Merovingians ruled until the eighth century, when Pepin the Short, with papal support, founded the Carolingian dynasty.
--- fact 5
The Carolingian kingdom peaked under Charlemagne and later divided among his descendants. Its territories became the basis of medieval France, Germany and the Holy Roman Empire.
--- context
The Franks built their kingdom in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the early [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], filling the space left by the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]]. Their empire under [[person/charlemagne|Charlemagne]] was an ancestor of the Holy Roman Empire. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler's personal conversion to his Roman subjects' majority faith became story-glue that welded conquerors and conquered into one people, setting the Franks apart from rival kingdoms that kept a minority creed. Two centuries later a change of dynasty was legitimized by the same meaning-engine, papal blessing replacing a discredited bloodline. A specialist could argue that Clovis's conversion served political advantage with his Gallo-Roman subjects as much as belief, which would weaken a reading of it as faith alone.
--- question 1
How much of Clovis's conversion reflects conviction versus calculated alliance with his Gallo-Roman subjects?
--- question 2
How unified was Frankish identity across the peoples the Merovingians and Carolingians ruled?

=== person/basil-ii
title: Basil II
type: person
date: 958 CE – 1025
themes: war, religion
src: ab83b6bf2ce5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Basil II (958 to 1025) was Byzantine emperor for nearly fifty years, the longest reign in Roman or Byzantine imperial history, remembered above all for his decades-long war against the First Bulgarian Empire.
--- fact 1
He became co-emperor as a child in 960 but did not gain full personal control of the government until about 989, when he was in his thirties, after suppressing rebellions by powerful aristocratic generals including Bardas Skleros and Bardas Phokas.
--- fact 2
His long campaigns against Bulgaria culminated in the decisive Battle of Kleidion in 1014, after which Bulgaria was absorbed into the Byzantine Empire by 1018, earning him the later epithet "Bulgar-slayer" (Boulgaroktonos).
--- fact 3
A story recorded by later chroniclers claims he blinded thousands of Bulgarian prisoners after Kleidion, leaving one man in every hundred with one eye to lead the others home; modern historians debate how literally this account should be taken.
--- fact 4
He left the imperial treasury full and the empire at its greatest territorial extent since the 7th century, but died without an heir, and the strong central rule he had imposed did not survive his less capable successors.
--- context
Basil II ruled the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] in the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Basil II's reign is often read backward from the Byzantine Empire's later decline, framing him as the last strong emperor of a golden age. That framing depends partly on hindsight: the empire's later troubles came from succession and external pressures Basil could not have controlled from beyond the grave, whatever the soundness of his own institutions.
--- question 1
How much of the Byzantine Empire's difficulties after 1025 stemmed from institutional weaknesses already present under Basil, rather than simply the absence of an equally capable successor?

=== person/chandragupta-maurya
title: Chandragupta Maurya
type: person
date: c. 340 – 297 BCE
themes: war, trade, religion
src: 2ab8c108e624
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Chandragupta Maurya (about 340 to 297 BCE) founded the Maurya Empire, the first political power to unite most of the Indian subcontinent, after overthrowing the Nanda dynasty of Magadha around 321 BCE.
--- fact 1
With guidance from the strategist Chanakya (Kautilya), Chandragupta seized the throne of Magadha. The Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft traditionally attributed to Chanakya, was long linked to his administration, but most scholars now date it to roughly the 1st to 3rd centuries CE and doubt that it describes his reign.
--- fact 2
Between about 305 and 303 BCE he fought the Diadoch ruler Seleucus I Nicator; the resulting settlement ceded territory in the northwest to the Maurya and included a marriage alliance, while Seleucus received war elephants in return.
--- fact 3
The Greek ambassador Megasthenes, sent to Chandragupta's court at Pataliputra, wrote the Indica, a major but now fragmentary source on Mauryan India.
--- fact 4
Later Jain tradition holds that Chandragupta abdicated, became an ascetic under Bhadrabahu, and died by ritual fasting (sallekhana) at Shravanabelagola in Karnataka, though this account survives mainly in sources written well after his reign.
--- context
Chandragupta founded the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and his settlement with the Seleucid successor state opened diplomatic contact between South Asia and the Hellenistic Mediterranean. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a kingdom traditionally tied to a treatise about statecraft and control ended, in the tradition at least, with its founder walking away from all of it to starve himself in a cave. Whether that ending reflects an actual late-life conversion or a later Jain community's wish to claim him is hard to separate from the record.
--- question 1
Most scholars now date the Arthashastra to roughly the 1st to 3rd centuries CE, so how much, if anything, of it reflects Chandragupta's own reign?

=== person/timur
title: Timur
type: person
date: 1336 – 1405
themes: war, art
src: 6db74264e9ba
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Timur (Tamerlane, traditionally dated 1336 to 1405) was a Central Asian conqueror who built the Timurid Empire and made Samarkand its centre.
--- fact 1
Timur's birth year is contested: 1336 is the traditional date, but many contemporary accounts imply the late 1320s. He was born near Kesh (now Shahrisabz, Uzbekistan).
--- fact 2
By about 1370 he had besieged his rival Amir Husayn at Balkh and, after Husayn's assassination, proclaimed himself sovereign of the Chagatai line of khans, with dominion over Transoxiana.
--- fact 3
He was not a descendant of Genghis Khan and did not take the title khan. He ruled as amir through a puppet Chaghatayid khan, Suyurghatmish, and took the title güregen (royal son-in-law) through his marriage to Saray Mulk Khanum, of the Chinggisid line.
--- fact 4
His armies campaigned in Persia, Iraq, the lands of the Golden Horde, India (Delhi was sacked in 1398), and Anatolia (he defeated the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402).
--- fact 5
Later chronicles describe massacres in cities he conquered. Their casualty figures are contested.
--- fact 6
He died in February 1405 at Otrar (also called Farab), in present-day Kazakhstan, while preparing an invasion of Ming China. Sources differ on the day (17 to 19 February). He was buried in the Gur-e-Amir in Samarkand.
--- context
Timur founded the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]] and presented himself as a restorer of the Mongol empire of [[person/genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]]. He rebuilt [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]], where he deported artisans from conquered cities.
--- observer
The Observer notes a conqueror who could not claim descent from the earlier conqueror he admired, and so bought the link instead: a Genghisid wife, a puppet khan, and the title of son-in-law. Legitimacy borrowed through marriage rather than blood is still a form of ancestor-weight, and it suggests the pull of the earlier line was strong enough to be worth acquiring.
--- question 1
How reliable are the casualty figures in accounts of his campaigns? Chronicle figures for single cities run from tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand, and modern scholars estimate millions of deaths overall, but none of these numbers is well established.
--- question 2
When was he actually born: the late 1320s or 1336?
