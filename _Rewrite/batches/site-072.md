=== place/arkaim
title: Arkaim
type: place
date: c. 2000 – 1700 BCE
themes: war, technology
src: 61f6c0a6ce48
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Arkaim is a fortified Bronze Age settlement of the Sintashta culture in Chelyabinsk Oblast, in the southern Urals of Russia, occupied from about 2000 to 1700 BCE by older reckoning, with a current dating of about 2050 to 1900 BCE.
--- fact 1
Archaeologists identified Arkaim in 1987 during surveys ahead of a planned reservoir; the reservoir was cancelled in April 1991 and the site was declared a historical and geographical museum.
--- fact 2
Arkaim had a roughly circular plan, on the order of 160 meters across, with concentric walls of earth, timber and clay around houses set in a ring.
--- fact 3
Arkaim shows evidence of copper and bronze metalworking, and sites of the same culture in the region are associated with graves holding horses and the earliest known spoke-wheeled chariots.
--- fact 4
Arkaim appears to have been abandoned around 1700 BCE, though sources differ on the date; some accounts describe it as burned, and whether by attack, accident or deliberate burning on departure is debated.
--- fact 5
Since the 1990s Arkaim has become a tourist and pilgrimage destination and a focus of popular claims that it was an "Aryan" homeland or proto-city, claims that professional archaeologists do not accept as established.
--- context
Arkaim is one of the fortified sites of the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]], whose chariot-using elites are often linked, though not conclusively, to early Indo-Iranian speakers discussed under [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Arkaim, its bronze workshops and the horse-and-chariot graves of its region come from a people with no known mark-code, so everything about them is inferred from soil, metal and bone. The concentric walls are an early durable claim-line on the steppe, and their pairing with elite chariot burials suggests that sanctioned harm was entangled with status-signals. That the site is now used to support identity stories about ancestry shows how a silent place invites story-glue. The reading would weaken if the settlement proves to have been mainly ceremonial or economic rather than defensive.
--- question 1
Was Arkaim mainly a defensive settlement, a ceremonial center or a metalworking town?
--- question 2
Why was Arkaim abandoned around 1700 BCE, and was it burned?

=== place/chang-an
title: Chang'an
type: place
date: c. 202 BCE – 904 CE
themes: trade, religion, art
src: 026998f8bdc7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chang'an was the capital of the Western Han dynasty from 202 BCE and, in a rebuilt form, of the Sui and Tang dynasties, on the site of present-day Xi'an in Shaanxi, China, until it was dismantled in 904 CE.
--- fact 1
Chang'an was founded as the Han capital after 202 BCE, with the Changle Palace completed in 200 BCE; the census of 2 CE recorded about 250,000 residents.
--- fact 2
The Sui dynasty built a new planned city, Daxing, southeast of the Han site from 582, and the Tang, from 618, kept its plan and the name Chang'an.
--- fact 3
Tang Chang'an was a grid of walled wards, with gates closed at night, enclosing about 78 square kilometers, with a central avenue and two state-supervised markets. Estimates of its population, with suburbs, vary by source, from several hundred thousand up to about one million.
--- fact 4
Chang'an received envoys and merchants from Central Asia, Persia, Korea, Japan and Tibet. The monk Xuanzang returned there in 645 with Buddhist texts from India, and the Nestorian Stele, recording a Christian community, was erected there in 781.
--- fact 5
Chang'an fell to rebels in 756 during the An Lushan Rebellion and was briefly held by Tibetan forces in 763. In 904 the warlord Zhu Wen forced the court to move to Luoyang and had the city's palaces and houses pulled down.
--- fact 6
The plan of Chang'an influenced the layout of the Japanese capitals Nara and Kyoto.
--- context
Chang'an was founded just after the fall of the dynasty created by the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]], and it served as the capital of the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]]. It was the eastern end of the routes described in [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], where [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] were prominent traders, and its plan was echoed in [[place/nara|Nara]] and [[place/kyoto|Kyoto]]. Chang'an sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], with its peak in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], in the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Tang Chang'an turned social order into geometry: walled wards, gates closed at night, two supervised markets, and one avenue leading to the palace. A plan with claim-lines this exact was later copied at Nara and Kyoto, which suggests that a floor plan can carry story-glue about what a capital should look like. Its end in 904 shows the reverse, that a capital's meaning could be moved by moving the court and taking down the buildings. This reading would weaken if the Japanese copying turns out to have been driven mainly by practical drainage and defense rather than a shared idea of order.
--- question 1
How densely built was Tang Chang'an inside its walls, especially in the southern wards?
--- question 2
How faithfully were the plans of Nara and Kyoto copied from Chang'an, and by what route did the design travel?

=== place/karakorum
title: Karakorum
type: place
date: 1235 – 1388
themes: war, trade, religion
src: ddb3219e12c3
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Karakorum was the capital of the Mongol Empire in the Orkhon Valley of central Mongolia, built from 1235 under Ögedei Khan, and it was damaged and razed by Ming forces in the 1370s and 1380s.
--- fact 1
Karakorum stood in the Orkhon Valley; Chinggis Khan is reported to have set up a camp there around 1220, and his son Ögedei ordered a walled city built from 1235.
--- fact 2
Craftsmen and captives from conquered lands, including China, Central Asia and Europe, were settled at Karakorum to work as artisans.
--- fact 3
William of Rubruck, a Flemish Franciscan sent by Louis IX of France, visited Karakorum in 1254 and described temples of several traditions, mosques, a Christian church, and a silver tree that dispensed drinks, made by the French goldsmith Guillaume Boucher.
--- fact 4
After Kublai Khan took power in 1260 and shifted the center of the empire to northern China, Karakorum declined.
--- fact 5
Ming forces damaged the city in 1372 and razed it in 1380, and one passage dates the destruction of the permanent palace to 1388; the Buddhist monastery of Erdene Zuu was built nearby from 1585, partly with stone from the ruins.
--- fact 6
The ruins of Karakorum were identified in the 19th century and excavated by a Soviet-Mongolian expedition in 1948 and 1949; UNESCO inscribed the Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape, which includes the site, in 2004.
--- context
Karakorum was the capital of the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] during the period of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], in a valley that also holds inscriptions of the earlier [[culture/gokturk-khaganate|Göktürk Khaganate]], and it was destroyed by forces of the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Karakorum was a fixed point built by a people whose power rested on mobility, a place to gather tribute, envoys and skilled captives, where the silver tree served as a status-signal aimed at foreign delegations. It was the center of the empire for only a few decades before power moved toward the taxable cities of northern China, which suggests that the capital followed the revenue and not the herds. The forced resettlement of artisans is a reminder that such a capital was built partly through sanctioned harm. The sources, mostly written by outsiders, do not settle how much the city mattered to the Mongol elite compared with the camps they moved between.
--- question 1
How large was Karakorum, and how many people lived there year-round?
--- question 2
How much does Rubruck's account, written by a visiting envoy, reflect the city as its rulers saw it?

=== place/kyoto
title: Kyoto
type: place
date: 794 CE – 1868
themes: art, religion, war
src: ff48f6374f46
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kyoto is a city on the Japanese island of Honshu, founded in 794 CE as the imperial capital Heian-kyō and the seat of the emperor until 1868 to 1869.
--- fact 1
Kyoto was founded in 794 by Emperor Kanmu as Heian-kyō, on a grid plan modeled on Chinese capitals such as Chang'an.
--- fact 2
Under the Heian court (794 to 1185) Kyoto was the center of court culture, including the novel The Tale of Genji, written in the early 11th century by Murasaki Shikibu.
--- fact 3
The Ashikaga shogunate ruled from Kyoto from 1336 to 1573, and the Onin War (1467 to 1477) destroyed much of the city.
--- fact 4
Under the Tokugawa shogunate (1603 to 1868) political power lay in Edo, while Kyoto remained the imperial seat and a center of crafts and Buddhist institutions.
--- fact 5
The emperor moved to Tokyo (formerly Edo) in 1868 to 1869 after the Meiji Restoration, ending Kyoto's role as capital.
--- fact 6
In 1945 Kyoto was removed from the list of possible atomic-bomb targets at the urging of US Secretary of War Henry Stimson, who is traditionally said to have cited its cultural significance.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed 17 "Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto" in 1994.
--- context
Kyoto's plan followed [[place/chang-an|Chang'an]], and the city succeeded [[place/nara|Nara]] as the imperial capital. It remained the imperial seat under the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] and ceased to be the capital with the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kyoto held an imperial house for more than a thousand years while, for much of that time, armies and administrations answered to shoguns elsewhere, so the story-glue of legitimacy and the practical power to govern sat in different places. A city laid out from a foreign template was maintained through fires and wars as a store of court culture, and its survival in 1945 shows that a city can be spared because of what its buildings hold, in a war that destroyed others. This reading would weaken if the decision not to bomb Kyoto rested mainly on operational reasons and not on cultural ones.
--- question 1
How much of Kyoto's original Heian layout survives in the modern street plan?
--- question 2
How far did cultural reasons, compared with military ones, decide Kyoto's removal from the 1945 target list?

=== place/petra
title: Petra
type: place
date: c. 312 BCE
themes: trade, art
src: 70a3ac73b990
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Petra is a rock-cut city in the mountains of southern Jordan that served as the capital of the Nabataean kingdom from about the 2nd century BCE until Rome annexed the kingdom in 106 CE.
--- fact 1
Petra lies in a basin among sandstone mountains, entered through a gorge about 1.2 kilometers long known as the Siq.
--- fact 2
Greek writers describe an expedition against the Nabataeans in 312 BCE and their rock refuge, which is sometimes identified with Petra, though the identification is doubtful.
--- fact 3
Petra's carved facades, including al-Khazneh ("the Treasury"), date mostly from about the 1st century BCE to the 1st century CE. Most facades are thought to be tombs, though the use of some, including the Treasury, is debated.
--- fact 4
Petra was supplied by dams, channels, ceramic pipes and cisterns in a desert climate, and its wealth is generally attributed to the caravan trade in incense and other goods. Estimates of its peak population are uncertain, with about 20,000 commonly cited and some figures as high as 30,000.
--- fact 5
After Roman annexation in 106 CE, Petra became part of the province of Arabia. An earthquake in 363 CE destroyed many buildings and the city declined afterward; the Swiss traveler Johann Ludwig Burckhardt brought it to European attention in 1812.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed Petra in 1985.
--- context
Petra was the capital of the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]] and a node in the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]] before its annexation by the [[culture/romans|Romans]]; its sister site is [[place/hegra|Hegra]] in Saudi Arabia. The site lies in Jordan, at the northern edge of the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]]. Petra sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Petra's most visible investment, the carved facades, is largely funerary, so a trading people's wealth became permanent chiefly as grief-rites and status-signals for the dead, while the caravans left little trace. A city that lived on stored water and control of a passage, not on farmland, shows how control of a route can stand in for control of land. The reading depends on the tomb attribution, which is well supported for many facades but debated for others such as the Treasury.
--- question 1
What were the carved buildings of Petra used for, and how many were tombs, banquet halls or temples?
--- question 2
What did Petra depend on after the caravan routes shifted?

=== place/santo-domingo
title: Santo Domingo
type: place
date: 1496
themes: trade, war, religion
src: cc6152b27af7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Santo Domingo is the capital of the Dominican Republic, on the south coast of Hispaniola, founded by Spain in 1496 and commonly described as the oldest continuously inhabited European-founded city in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Santo Domingo was founded in 1496 by Bartholomew Columbus on the east bank of the Ozama River, and governor Nicolás de Ovando moved it to the west bank in 1502 after storm damage.
--- fact 2
Santo Domingo became the seat of Spanish administration in the Caribbean, with the first Real Audiencia in the Americas (created by royal decree in 1511, with its start of operation dated differently by sources), a cathedral begun in the 1510s and completed in 1540, and a university founded in 1538.
--- fact 3
Expeditions to Puerto Rico, Cuba and the mainland were organized from Hispaniola under this colonial administration.
--- fact 4
The Taíno population of Hispaniola fell sharply under forced labor, violence and disease, including a smallpox epidemic beginning in 1518. Estimates of the pre-contact population range from tens of thousands to over a million, and a Spanish count in 1514 recorded about 26,000 Taíno on the island.
--- fact 5
Enslaved Africans were brought to Hispaniola from the early 1500s; in December 1521 enslaved Africans rebelled on a sugar estate near the city, an uprising commonly described as the first of its kind in the Americas.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Colonial City of Santo Domingo in 1990.
--- context
Santo Domingo was the base for the first phase of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] in the Caribbean, which brought devastation to the [[culture/taino|Taino]] and drew the island into the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]]. Its administrators and settlers later took part in the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]] and other mainland ventures. The city stands on [[place/hispaniola|Hispaniola]], in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that within about four decades Spain had placed a high court, a cathedral and a university on a shore where none had stood, extending a claim-line through stone institutions while the people already on the island were drawn into a forced-labor system. The city marks a point where two exchange-webs, one from Europe and one from the Americas, began moving people, crops and pathogens in both directions. The rebellion of 1521 shows that those held in the system resisted it from early on. Because the population figures rest on Spanish colonial counts, the size of the Taíno loss is uncertain even though its direction is not.
--- question 1
How large was the pre-contact population of Hispaniola, and how much of its decline came from disease compared with forced labor and violence?
--- question 2
How did Santo Domingo's role change as Spain's attention shifted to the mainland?

=== place/yellow-river
title: Yellow River
type: place
date: c. 6000 BCE
themes: earth-systems, war, technology
src: ab5f414c2767
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Yellow River (Huang He) is a river of northern China, about 5,460 kilometers long, whose loess-laden floodplain was a center of early farming and of the Shang and Zhou states, and whose floods and course changes have shaped Chinese history.
--- fact 1
The Yellow River rises in the Bayan Har Mountains of Qinghai and flows through northern China to the Bohai Sea.
--- fact 2
Farming communities growing millet are documented in the Yellow River basin from about the 7th to 6th millennia BCE, and the Shang and Zhou states arose in its middle and lower reaches.
--- fact 3
The Yellow River carries a heavy load of loess sediment from the Loess Plateau, which has raised its bed above the surrounding plain in the lower reaches and made dikes necessary; records list many breaches and several major changes of course.
--- fact 4
From 1128 (some sources give 1194) until 1855 the river mostly ran south of the Shandong Peninsula into the Yellow Sea, until a breach around 1851 to 1855 returned it to a northern course.
--- fact 5
A flood in 1887 is estimated to have killed between about 900,000 and 2 million people.
--- fact 6
In 1938, during the Second Sino-Japanese War, Nationalist forces breached dikes at Huayuankou to slow the Japanese advance; several hundred thousand people died, with published estimates up to about 900,000, and millions were displaced.
--- context
The Yellow River is linked to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in northern China, to the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]] and the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] as states that organized flood control, and to the [[event/second-sino-japanese-war|Second Sino-Japanese War]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a river that floods without warning helped tie authority to water control, and the traditional story of Yu the Great, who is said to have tamed the floods and founded the first dynasty, is a story-glue joining rule to engineering. The 1938 breach shows the same river used as sanctioned harm by a state against an invading army, with civilians as its main victims. The image of the Yellow River as the sole cradle of Chinese civilization is itself a story, since sites on the Yangtze and elsewhere also show early farming and state formation. The hypothesis that flood control drove state formation is contested, and specialists could weaken this reading by showing that other factors mattered more.
--- question 1
How large was the death toll of the 1938 flood, and how many people were displaced?
--- question 2
How much did flood control contribute to early state formation compared with warfare and trade?

=== culture/anglo-saxons
title: Anglo-Saxons
type: culture
date: c. 450 CE – 1066
themes: kinship, religion, war, language
src: 2e452127e539
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Anglo-Saxons were Germanic-speaking settlers and their descendants who founded the English kingdoms of early medieval Britain, conventionally from the fifth century until the Norman Conquest of 1066. Their language became Old English.
--- fact 1
Settlers from North Sea coasts, whom Bede called Angles, Saxons and Jutes, reached eastern and southern Britain from the fifth century (perhaps earlier), as Roman rule ended.
--- fact 2
Most Anglo-Saxons farmed in timber-built villages; from the seventh century, trading towns such as Ipswich and Hamwic appeared, and slavery was common.
--- fact 3
Small kingdoms, later labelled the seven-kingdom Heptarchy, competed for overlordship; Kent's law code, probably of about 600, set compensation by rank.
--- fact 4
Augustine's mission reached Kent in 597; the Sutton Hoo ship burial, early seventh century, held Byzantine silver, and furnished burial largely ended by about 680.
--- fact 5
Vikings raided Lindisfarne in 793; Alfred of Wessex beat Guthrum in 878; Cnut conquered England in 1016; Harold Godwinson died at Hastings in 1066.
--- context
The Anglo-Saxons settled Britain, in [[region/europe|Europe]], during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], as the [[culture/romans|Romans]] left and the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] unfolded. They shared the island with Celtic-speaking Britons (see [[culture/celts|Celts]]), converted to [[culture/christianity|Christianity]], and fought the [[culture/norse|Norse]] before Norman conquest. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Anglo-Saxons held strangers together through kin-lattice duties priced in wergild, then through a Christian meaning-engine that kings adopted. Their grief-rite moved from goods-filled graves to plain ones, and what survives includes buried gold, monastic manuscripts, and Offa's Dyke, perhaps a claim-line. A specialist could argue that "Anglo-Saxon" was a modern scholars' label more than a felt shared identity, which would weaken a reading of one people held together by shared obligation.
--- question 1
How large was the Anglo-Saxon migration, and how did settlers and Britons interact?
--- question 2
Should "Anglo-Saxon" remain the scholarly label, given debate over its meaning and modern political use?

=== culture/etruscans
title: Etruscans
type: culture
date: c. 800 – 264 BCE
themes: trade, religion, art
src: 961eb44409d7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Etruscans were a people of Etruria in central Italy, roughly modern Tuscany and northern Lazio, whose city-states flourished from about the eighth to the third century BCE. Rome gradually conquered and absorbed them.
--- fact 1
Etruscan city-states included Tarquinia, Cerveteri, Vulci and Veii. Livy describes a league of twelve peoples meeting at Voltumna's shrine.
--- fact 2
Their wealth came from farming, metals and sea trade. Populonia smelted Elba iron ore, and ports such as Pyrgi linked Etruria with Greeks and Phoenicians.
--- fact 3
They wrote a non-Indo-European language in a Greek-derived alphabet. Gold tablets from Pyrgi, with Etruscan and Phoenician texts, date to about 500 BCE.
--- fact 4
Etruscans and Carthaginians fought Phocaean Greeks at Alalia about 540 BCE, and Greek fleets defeated the Etruscans at Cumae in 474 BCE.
--- fact 5
Rome traditionally took Veii in 396 BCE and sacked Volsinii in 264 BCE. Etruscans gained Roman citizenship around 90 BCE, and their language survived into the first century CE.
--- context
The Etruscans flourished in [[region/europe|Europe]] from the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]], trading with the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]], [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]] and [[place/carthage|Carthage]] before absorption by the [[culture/romans|Romans]]. Etruscan writing was a probable route for the [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]] into Latin. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Etruscan cities shared a meaning-engine and sound-code rather than one state, and Roman writers describe ritual books that treated a claim-line as a sacred act. At Cerveteri, their grief-rite laid out a cemetery as streets of houses for the dead, and with Etruscan books largely lost, tombs and short inscriptions remain. A specialist could argue that the league's unity was partly a Greek and Roman simplification, which would weaken reading the Etruscans as one community.
--- question 1
Did the Etruscans see themselves as one people, or as citizens of separate cities?
--- question 2
How far did Etruscan rulers shape early Rome, given that stories of Etruscan kings are partly legendary?

=== culture/funan
title: Funan
type: culture
date: c. 100 – 550 CE
themes: trade, religion, technology
src: 368a707219f6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Funan is the Chinese name for a polity, perhaps a league of towns, in the Mekong delta and Cambodia from about the 1st to the 6th century CE. It is known from Chinese records and Oc Eo excavations; no known local text uses the name.
--- fact 1
A canal of 70-80 km linked the port of Oc Eo to Angkor Borei, likely for transport and water management.
--- fact 2
Oc Eo yielded Roman gold medallions of Antoninus Pius, and possibly Marcus Aurelius, plus Indian and Persian objects.
--- fact 3
Chinese sources credit the early 3rd-century king Fan Shiman with a fleet and attacks on more than ten kingdoms. Later histories quote envoys Kang Tai and Zhu Ying on walled towns and archives.
--- fact 4
Funan practiced Hinduism and Buddhism, with Sanskrit inscriptions.
--- fact 5
Chinese records list envoys from Funan's last known king, Rudravarman, until 539. Chenla is said to have annexed Funan between the 6th century and about 627; scholars dispute how.
--- context
Funan lay in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], beside [[culture/champa|Champa]] and on [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] sea routes that brought [[culture/hinduism|Hinduism]] and [[culture/buddhism|Buddhism]]. Its canals in the [[place/mekong-river|Mekong River]] delta relate to other [[technology/irrigation-canals|Irrigation Canals]], and the later [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] arose nearby. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes Funan drew Roman, Indian and Persian goods into one exchange-web, while its rulers' Indian religion and script may have worked as a status-signal binding strangers. Canals and statues remain, but Funan's name and claim-lines come mostly from outsiders' texts. A specialist could argue that Funan was a loose set of allied ports, not one state, which would weaken reading it as one realm with one story-glue.
--- question 1
Was Funan one kingdom, or several centers Chinese writers treated as one?
--- question 2
Did Chenla conquer Funan, or did power shift among Cambodian centers?
--- question 3
What did Funan's people call themselves, and what language did they speak?

=== culture/garifuna
title: Garifuna
type: culture
date: c. 1635
themes: war, kinship, language, art
src: 5ec1c9d35399
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Garifuna are an Afro-Indigenous people who formed on St Vincent from African and Kalinago ancestry, were deported by Britain to Central America in 1797, and now live along the Caribbean coast from Belize to Nicaragua.
--- fact 1
Tradition traces Garifuna origins to Africans from Spanish slave ships wrecked near St Vincent (1635 or about 1675); no eyewitness account is known, and many historians instead favor runaways from nearby islands.
--- fact 2
Garifuna women grew cassava and made ereba bread while men fished; remittances now supplement many rural incomes.
--- fact 3
Chatoyer, a Black Carib leader, resisted British expansion in the First Carib War (1769-1773), and was killed on 14 March 1795 in the Second Carib War.
--- fact 4
Britain deported over 4,000 Garifuna to Baliceaux from 1796, where about half died; about 2,000 landed on Roatan on 12 April 1797, and most then moved to Trujillo.
--- fact 5
UNESCO proclaimed Garifuna language, dance and music in 2001; in 2015 the Inter-American Court found Honduras violated two Garifuna communities' collective property rights.
--- context
The Garifuna took shape in the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], through the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and alongside the [[culture/kalinago|Kalinago]]. Their 1790s war and deportation fell within the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Garifuna joined African and Kalinago ancestry into a kin-lattice, sustained by a sound-code and the dugu ancestor rite through an exile that killed thousands. The 1773 treaty's claim-line did not hold, and Honduran titling later failed some Garifuna communities, the Inter-American Court found. A specialist could argue that the shipwreck story is a later founding narrative, which would weaken any reading dating the kin-lattice to one seventeenth-century event.
--- question 1
How much of the shipwreck story rests on documented events rather than later tradition?
--- question 2
How did Arawakan grammar and Carib-derived vocabulary come to combine in Garifuna speech?

=== culture/hawaiian-kingdom
title: Hawaiian Kingdom
type: culture
date: c. 1795 – 1893
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 47fddf5f5060
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Hawaiian Kingdom was a Native Hawaiian monarchy united by Kamehameha I between about 1795 and 1810 and overthrown in 1893. The United States annexed the islands in 1898; the annexation's legality remains disputed.
--- fact 1
Kamehameha I conquered Oʻahu in 1795 and gained Kauaʻi and Niʻihau by negotiation in 1810; sources differ on the founding year.
--- fact 2
The Hawaiian Kingdom's farmers worked taro terraces and fishponds in mountain-to-sea ahupuaʻa; exports shifted from sandalwood to sugar, boosted by an 1875 United States treaty.
--- fact 3
The kapu system of taboos ended in 1819, a written constitution followed in 1840, and Britain and France recognized independence in 1843.
--- fact 4
Introduced diseases cut the Native Hawaiian population to about 71,000 by 1853. After the 1848 Great Māhele, commoners' successful claims under the 1850 Kuleana Act covered under one percent of the land.
--- fact 5
An armed militia forced the 1887 Bayonet Constitution on Kalākaua. In 1893 a Committee of Safety, with United States Marines ashore, deposed Liliuokalani. About 21,000 petitioned against annexation in 1897; Congress annexed the islands in 1898.
--- context
The Hawaiian Kingdom, from [[person/kamehameha-i|Kamehameha I]] to [[person/liliuokalani|Liliuokalani]], ruled descendants of the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]] ([[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]) and, later, many immigrants in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Hawaiian Kingdom merged rival chiefdoms into one kin-lattice, then entered treaties and constitutions in its recognizers' mark-code. The same tools, including the Māhele's tradable claim-lines, let American- and European-descended businessmen gather power against the monarchy. A specialist could argue that coercion in 1887 and 1893, not the tools, caused the loss, which would weaken a reading that finds the loss built into them.
--- question 1
How large was the Hawaiian population in 1778, given estimates from 110,000 to over 800,000?
--- question 2
Did the Hawaiian Kingdom survive as a state in international law after 1893, as some scholars argue?

=== culture/lakota
title: Lakota
type: culture
date: c. 1760 – 1890
themes: war, kinship, trade
src: 45515ef9b57c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Lakota, or Teton Sioux, are the western division of the Oceti Sakowin, the Seven Council Fires. From the 1700s they built a mounted bison-hunting society on the northern Great Plains, broken by United States force by 1890.
--- fact 1
The Lakota form seven divisions, among them the Oglala and Hunkpapa.
--- fact 2
Most historians say the Lakota moved west from Minnesota, reaching the Black Hills between the 1770s and 1820s; Lakota tradition places their origin there.
--- fact 3
The Lakota obtained horses from the Cheyenne between about 1700 and 1730, becoming mounted bison hunters who gathered each summer.
--- fact 4
Lakota bands were extended families (tiospaye); akicita societies policed camps and hunts.
--- fact 5
The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty set the Black Hills aside within the Great Sioux Reservation. Lakota and allies destroyed Custer's command in 1876, and the United States seized the Hills in 1877; in 1980 the Supreme Court held this taking required compensation.
--- fact 6
With bison nearly gone by the 1880s, soldiers killed an estimated 250 to 300 Lakota at Wounded Knee on 29 December 1890.
--- context
The Lakota flourished in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], on horses from the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; [[person/sitting-bull|Sitting Bull]] was a Hunkpapa leader. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Lakota linked scattered bands through a kin-lattice and a shared summer gathering, and made the imported horse a tool-lineage. Their claim-line rested on use and treaty, and the Black Hills still carry a meaning-engine's weight, shown by the Lakota refusal of the 1980 award. A specialist could argue that the bands often acted apart and that Lakota power was regional and brief, which would weaken a lasting kin-lattice reading.
--- question 1
When did the Lakota cross the Missouri, and how do winter counts and emergence accounts fit the record?
--- question 2
How far did smallpox among Missouri villages, not Lakota strength alone, drive Lakota expansion?

=== culture/mississippian-culture
title: Mississippian Culture
type: culture
date: c. 1000 – 1450
themes: religion, trade, kinship, art
src: 61c2198b7cea
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Mississippian culture was a family of Native American societies that farmed maize and built platform mounds across the Midwest and Southeast, from about 800 or 1000 to 1600 CE, with Cahokia as its largest town.
--- fact 1
Mississippian communities farmed river floodplains, growing maize with beans, squash, and older native crops.
--- fact 2
Hereditary chiefs ruled from platform mounds carrying their houses or temples.
--- fact 3
Cahokia grew rapidly from about 1050 to perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 people. Its Mound 72 held about 270 burials, including mass graves of mostly young women, interpreted as sacrifices.
--- fact 4
Shell gorgets and copper plates showing bird-warrior imagery, called the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, circulated among centers from roughly 1100 or 1200.
--- fact 5
Cahokia declined from about 1200 and was largely abandoned by roughly 1350 to 1450, amid drought, flooding, and social strain. After Hernando de Soto's 1539 expedition, disease, warfare, and slave raiding helped end the remaining chiefdoms by about 1730.
--- context
The Mississippian culture flourished in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], farming maize domesticated in Mesoamerica ([[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]]), with its largest town at [[place/cahokia|Cahokia]]. Mississippian towns overlapped in time with the [[culture/ancestral-puebloans|Ancestral Puebloans]] and later faced the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
Mississippian towns were bound less by one state than by a shared meaning-engine, its imagery carried on shell and copper status-signals through an exchange-web. Chiefly families marked claim-lines with mounds, and Mound 72's mass graves, if read as sacrifice, show sanctioned harm within a grief-rite; earthworks and living Native nations survive. A specialist could argue that the shared imagery reflects parallel local choices more than one connected tradition, which would weaken the reading of a single meaning-engine.
--- question 1
Did the Mississippian culture spread by migration from Cahokia or by neighbors adopting its practices?
--- question 2
How much did drought, warfare, and European disease each contribute to Mississippian decline?

=== culture/yamnaya-culture
title: Yamnaya Culture
type: culture
date: c. 3300 – 2600 BCE
themes: language, technology, kinship
src: 63277401bbc5
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Yamnaya culture is an archaeological grouping of mobile pastoralists on the Pontic-Caspian steppe, in present Ukraine and southern Russia, usually dated about 3300 to 2600 BCE. Yamnaya-related ancestry spread across Eurasia, and the culture is central to debates over Indo-European origins.
--- fact 1
Yamnaya herders lived between the Dniester and Ural rivers, keeping cattle, sheep, goats and horses; few permanent settlements are known.
--- fact 2
The name derives from Russian *yama*, "pit": the dead lay in pit graves under earthen mounds (kurgans), covered in red ochre.
--- fact 3
Some graves held wagons or wagon parts, showing wheeled transport and suggesting modest social ranking, not cities or states.
--- fact 4
From about 3100 BCE, Yamnaya-related groups moved west along the Danube into Hungary; Afanasievo burials in the Altai match Yamnaya ancestry closely.
--- fact 5
Corded Ware groups, appearing in central Europe by about 2900 BCE, carried up to about 75 percent Yamnaya-like ancestry, though not all from known Yamnaya groups.
--- fact 6
The Catacomb culture in the west and Poltavka on the middle Volga followed and overlapped Yamnaya.
--- context
Yamnaya belongs to the early [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] of eastern [[region/europe|Europe]] and preceded steppe societies such as the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]]. Yamnaya wagons connect to [[technology/the-wheel|The Wheel]], Yamnaya ancestry to the [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Yamnaya herders raised kurgans across their seasonal grazing grounds, so the mounds may have served as claim-lines and grief-rites at once. Wagons in some graves read as status-signal built on a tool-lineage, and with no mark-code, mostly mounds and bones remain. A specialist could argue that the mounds' territorial role is inferred from placement rather than shown, which would weaken the claim-line reading.
--- question 1
Did Yamnaya herders ride horses regularly, or mainly drive cattle-drawn wagons?
--- question 2
Did Yamnaya-related ancestry spread by migration, conquest, disease or gradual mixing, and did Yamnaya herders speak the ancestral Indo-European language?
