=== species/sugarcane
title: Sugarcane
type: species
date: c. 4000 BCE
themes: trade, war
src: b0195c3df66c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea and nearby parts of Southeast Asia around 6,000 years ago, spread through Asia and the Mediterranean world over the following millennia, and after 1492 became the crop most closely tied to slave labor in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Sugarcane was domesticated from the wild grass Saccharum robustum in New Guinea around 6,000 years ago; a related domestication of Saccharum sinense by Austronesian peoples in southern China and Taiwan occurred by at least 5,500 years ago.
--- fact 2
Austronesian traders carried sugarcane to China and India by about 3,000 years ago, and Persian and Greek writers encountered it in the 6th to 4th centuries BCE; by the 8th century CE sugar was traded into the Mediterranean world as an expensive luxury spice from India.
--- fact 3
Christopher Columbus brought sugarcane to Hispaniola on his second voyage, and the first sugar harvest in the Americas took place there in 1501; Portuguese colonizers introduced the crop to Brazil, where by 1540 the island of Santa Catarina alone held about 800 sugar mills.
--- fact 4
The Atlantic sugar trade formed one side of a triangular trade linking European manufactured goods, enslaved Africans, and New World sugar and other raw materials, and sugar plantations across the Caribbean and Brazil were worked overwhelmingly by enslaved labor; in Louisiana, enslaved workers helped make the state responsible for nearly a quarter of the world's exportable sugar by 1853.
--- fact 5
Global sugarcane production reached about 1.92 billion tonnes in 2022, with Brazil producing about 38 percent and India about 23 percent.
--- context
Sugarcane's post-1492 spread across the Atlantic world is described in more detail under the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and is inseparable from the history of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]; its introduction to Hispaniola is one of the earliest transfers of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that sugarcane's long, slow spread across Asia over thousands of years contrasts sharply with the speed and scale of its expansion in the Americas after 1492, where within a few decades an exchange-web moved the plant, an enslaved workforce and a market for the finished product across an ocean at once, a compression the Observer treats as evidence of how quickly extractive systems can scale once the pieces are in place. The suffering of enslaved workers on sugar plantations is recorded here as central to the crop's modern history, not incidental to it. The reading would weaken if the Louisiana sugar economy's growth is shown to have depended as much on land and capital as on enslaved labor specifically.
--- question 1
How much of sugar's price as a "luxury spice" in medieval Europe reflected genuine scarcity versus deliberately controlled supply along the trade route from India?
--- question 2
How did the scale of the Atlantic sugar plantation system compare with earlier sugar production in the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean worlds?

=== species/tea-plant
title: Tea Plant
type: species
date: c. 200 BCE
themes: trade, war
src: 53d7f8be2c7b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The tea plant is native to the borderlands of southwestern China, northeastern India and northern Myanmar, drunk in China by at least the 2nd century BCE, and its later global trade helped provoke both British cultivation in colonial India and the Opium Wars with China.
--- fact 1
Chinese tradition credits the legendary ruler Shennong with discovering tea in 2737 BCE, but the earliest physical evidence, tea leaves found in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han near Xi'an, dates to the 2nd century BCE, and the earliest written reference to boiling tea appears in a text from 59 BCE.
--- fact 2
Tea drinking and its associated ceremonial culture spread from China to Korea, Japan and Vietnam during the Tang dynasty, taking root in Japan as the practice later known as chado.
--- fact 3
Portuguese priests and merchants brought tea to Europe in the 16th century, and the Dutch East India Company shipped tea from Macao to Java in 1607, the first recorded tea shipment by a European trading power; tea became fashionable in Britain after King Charles II married the tea-drinking Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza in 1662.
--- fact 4
Britain's growing demand for Chinese tea created a large trade imbalance paid for in silver, and British merchants turned to selling opium into China to offset the cost, a trade dispute that escalated into the Opium Wars.
--- fact 5
The British introduced Chinese tea plants to India in 1836, and the East India Company sent Robert Fortune to China in 1848 to obtain more; planters then found a distinct variety growing wild in Assam and hybridized it with the Chinese plant, the Alubari garden in Darjeeling opened in 1856, and tea reached Ceylon in 1867.
--- fact 6
Global tea production reached about 29.76 million tonnes in 2022, with China supplying about 49 percent and India about 20 percent; China, India, Kenya and Sri Lanka together account for roughly 81 percent of world output.
--- context
Tea's ceremonial culture was carried across East Asia during the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], following earlier consumption recorded under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], and the trade imbalance it created between Britain and China was a direct cause of the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]]; British cultivation of tea in India became a pillar of the economy of the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a plant first recorded as an elite and ceremonial drink later became the trigger for an armed conflict over a trade imbalance, showing how a taste adopted by one society can force hard choices on a distant government. Britain's substitution of opium for silver, and its later transplanting of tea cultivation to India, both read as attempts to escape dependence on a single exchange-web by force or by relocation. The reading would weaken if the Opium Wars are shown to have had causes more central than the tea trade imbalance.
--- question 1
How distinct were the Assam and Chinese tea varieties before their hybridization, and how much did the Assam discovery owe to prior local cultivation?
--- question 2
How much of the decision to trade opium for tea reflects a considered strategy versus an improvised response to a worsening balance of payments?

=== species/tobacco
title: Tobacco
type: species
date: c. 1400 BCE
themes: trade, religion
src: 37380d8bca42
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Tobacco is a plant native to the Americas, cultivated there since at least the 2nd millennium BCE and used ceremonially by many Indigenous peoples, that Spanish colonizers carried to Europe in the 16th century and that became a cash crop underwriting Virginia's colonial economy.
--- fact 1
Cultivation sites in Mexico show tobacco use dating back to between about 1400 and 1000 BCE, and it was used across the Americas both socially and ceremonially, including to seal peace treaties and trade agreements; in many Indigenous traditions tobacco is regarded as a gift used to carry prayers, and it remains part of ceremonial practice today.
--- fact 2
The Spanish chronicler Francisco Hernández de Toledo brought tobacco seeds to Spain in 1559 on the order of King Philip II, an early step in the plant's introduction to the Old World, and it spread rapidly across Europe, Africa and Asia in the following decades.
--- fact 3
Tobacco became central to the economy of the English colony of Jamestown, used at times as a form of currency and exported as a cash crop that is often credited with saving the struggling colony; its cultivation across the Caribbean and the American South became deeply tied to the history of enslaved labor.
--- fact 4
The World Health Organization has identified tobacco use as the world's single greatest preventable cause of death.
--- fact 5
China is the largest producer of tobacco today, growing about 36 percent of the world total in 2020, followed by India and Brazil.
--- context
Tobacco's cultivation underwrote the early economy of [[place/jamestown|Jamestown]] and, more broadly, its spread to the Old World is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; the labor that grew it across the Americas was increasingly supplied through the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that tobacco carried two very different meanings across the ocean it crossed: a substance woven into ceremony and treaty-making among many Indigenous peoples of the Americas, and a commercial export crop in the colonies that displaced those same peoples, grown increasingly by enslaved labor for a European market that treated it mainly as a source of profit and pleasure. That a plant used to seal peace agreements became bound up with a system built on the ownership of human beings is, in the Observer's reading, a stark case of a substance's meaning-engine changing entirely as it moved between very different societies. The reading would weaken if ceremonial tobacco use is shown to have been far less widespread among Indigenous peoples than the general accounts suggest.
--- question 1
How did the meaning and use of tobacco change among Indigenous communities themselves as European demand for the crop grew?
--- question 2
How much of Virginia's economic survival is fairly credited to tobacco alone, as opposed to other factors in the colony's early development?

=== species/wheat
title: Wheat
type: species
date: c. 9600 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 952114c4932f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Wheat is a cereal grass domesticated from wild einkorn and emmer in the Fertile Crescent from about 9600 BCE, and it is now the crop grown on more land than any other food plant on Earth.
--- fact 1
Wild emmer wheat was first cultivated in the southern Levant as early as 9600 BCE, and wild einkorn was gathered in southeastern Anatolia from about 10,700 BCE, with clearly domestic einkorn appearing after about 8800 BCE; sites such as Tell Abu Hureyra and Çayönü record this transition.
--- fact 2
Bread wheat arose later when domesticated emmer hybridized with a wild goatgrass, producing a form with three genome sets (hexaploid) that is believed to have first appeared in farmers' fields rather than in the wild.
--- fact 3
Wheat cultivation reached Cyprus and Greece by the 7th millennium BCE, Egypt shortly after 6000 BCE, and the Yellow River valley in China by about 2600 BCE.
--- fact 4
Domestication changed the plant's ear so that it no longer shatters and scatters its own seed without pressure, which made harvesting far more efficient but made the plant dependent on humans to reproduce at scale.
--- fact 5
In the 20th century, breeders including Norman Borlaug developed short-stemmed, high-yield varieties as part of the Green Revolution, following earlier Japanese dwarf-wheat breeding; global wheat production reached about 799 million tonnes in 2024, more than any other food crop by land area.
--- fact 6
Pests and diseases, including rust fungi, are estimated to consume roughly a fifth of the world's wheat crop each year.
--- context
Wheat's domestication is one of the founding crops of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent, alongside the parallel [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] of sheep and goats, and its farming shaped early settlements such as [[place/catalhoyuk|Çatalhöyük]]. Its 20th-century yield increases are central to the [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]]. Wheat sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: that in some tool-lineage of cultivation, wheat can be said to have domesticated humans as much as humans domesticated wheat, since a shattering-resistant grain that cannot reseed itself requires people to sow, harvest and store it every year, binding a community to a fixed place and a yearly cycle. The plant's spread across three continents within a few thousand years shows an early exchange-web moving alongside human migration and trade. The reading would weaken if wheat's spread proves to have tracked climate suitability more than deliberate human choice.
--- question 1
How many separate hybridization events produced the hexaploid bread wheats grown today, and where did they occur?
--- question 2
How much of wheat's early spread followed trade and migration routes as opposed to independent local adoption?

=== place/amazon-river
title: Amazon River
type: place
date: c. 1000 BCE
themes: trade, earth-systems
src: 5cadc6b34f3c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Amazon River flows about 6,575 kilometers through Peru, Colombia and Brazil and carries by far the largest volume of any river on Earth, draining a rainforest basin long settled by farming and fishing societies before European contact.
--- fact 1
The Amazon's length, about 6,575 kilometers, and whether it or the Nile is the world's longest river are disputed depending on which headwater and mouth channels are counted; its average discharge at the delta, about 224,000 cubic meters per second, is unmatched by any other river.
--- fact 2
Communities along the river and its floodplains built raised fields, mounds and patches of enriched dark earth from at least the 1st millennium BCE, evidence used to argue for larger and more settled pre-Columbian populations than once assumed.
--- fact 3
The Spanish expedition of Francisco de Orellana made the first recorded European descent of the river in 1541 to 1542, reaching its mouth on 24 August 1542; Orellana's account of fighting women warriors is the traditional source of the river's name.
--- fact 4
Rubber tapped from Amazonian trees fed a boom from 1879 to 1912 that enriched cities such as Manaus and Iquitos and brought forced labor and violence against Indigenous peoples, with estimates of about 100,000 deaths across the region in that period; the boom collapsed after rubber plantations in Southeast Asia entered the market in 1912.
--- fact 5
Deforestation for agriculture, logging and mining has reduced parts of the rainforest, and large dams such as Belo Monte on a major tributary have displaced communities and altered river flow; the extent of long-term ecological damage is debated.
--- context
The Amazon's floodplain was home to Indigenous societies including the [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]] near its mouth, and the dark-earth soils tied to the [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]] event and to societies such as the [[culture/casarabe-culture|Casarabe Culture]] farther upriver. European arrival on the river opened it to the wider [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], and its forests are now central to debates over [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] by the date_start used here and the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Amazon's forest was long read by outsiders as nearly empty of large societies, and that raised fields, mounds and dark earth are evidence used to challenge that reading, an example of how a landscape's story-glue can change as new evidence is found. The rubber boom shows an exchange-web reaching into the basin and extracting wealth through sanctioned harm against the people living there, which the Observer records as suffering. The reading would weaken if pre-Columbian populations along the river prove to have been smaller and less settled than the dark-earth evidence suggests.
--- question 1
How large were pre-Columbian populations along the Amazon, and how much of the "pristine forest" idea reflects population loss after 1492 rather than an original absence of large societies?
--- question 2
How much of the river's basin can absorb further deforestation and damming before its water cycle changes significantly?

=== place/antarctica
title: Antarctica
type: place
date: 1820
themes: science, earth-systems
src: 040d45eab0c7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Antarctica is Earth's southernmost continent, with no indigenous population, first sighted by ships in 1820, reached at its geographic pole by Roald Amundsen in 1911, and governed since 1961 under a treaty that reserves it for peaceful, scientific use.
--- fact 1
Antarctica covers about 14,200,000 square kilometers under an ice sheet averaging about 1.9 kilometers thick, holding roughly 70 percent of the world's fresh water.
--- fact 2
The continent's coast was probably first sighted in 1820 by a Russian expedition under Fabian von Bellingshausen and Mikhail Lazarev, among rival claims from British and American sealers in the same years; the first confirmed landing came in 1895 by a Norwegian party.
--- fact 3
Roald Amundsen's Norwegian team reached the geographic South Pole on 14 December 1911, weeks ahead of a British team led by Robert Falcon Scott, whose party died on the return journey.
--- fact 4
The Antarctic Treaty, signed in 1959 and in force from 1961, bars military activity, mining, and nuclear testing or waste disposal, reserves the continent for peaceful and scientific use, and sets aside territorial claims among its parties.
--- fact 5
About 5,000 people work at research stations across Antarctica in the southern summer, falling to about 1,000 in winter.
--- fact 6
Warming has been recorded across the continent, with the Antarctic Peninsula's temperature rising by about 3 degrees Celsius since the mid-20th century and the West Antarctic ice sheet losing roughly 100 billion metric tons of ice a year since 2002, contributing to projected sea-level rise.
--- context
Antarctic research became a proving ground for international science during the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]], alongside the [[event/space-age|Space Age]], and its ice sheet is now a central subject of [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]]. It sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] by the date of first sighting and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Antarctica is the one large landmass claimed by no kin-lattice of its own, so the claim-lines drawn across it are entirely the product of outside states, and the 1959 treaty is an unusual case of several such states agreeing to set territorial claims aside in favor of shared, peaceful use. The race to the pole in 1911 was a status-signal fought over an empty landscape, and one side's deaths on the return march are recorded here as suffering in the pursuit of that signal. The reading would weaken if the treaty's restraint proves to depend mainly on the continent's low resource value rather than genuine cooperation, a test now approaching as mineral and fishing interest grows.
--- question 1
How long will the Antarctic Treaty's ban on resource extraction hold as demand for minerals and fisheries grows?
--- question 2
How much of the continent's ice loss is now irreversible on a human timescale?

=== place/atlantic-ocean
title: Atlantic Ocean
type: place
date: c. 1000
themes: trade, technology
src: dba31f6cf141
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Atlantic Ocean, the second largest of Earth's oceans at about 85 million square kilometers, became from around 1000 CE a corridor for transoceanic voyages that reshaped the connections among Europe, Africa and the Americas.
--- fact 1
The Atlantic covers about 85,133,000 square kilometers, has an average depth of about 3,646 meters, and reaches a maximum depth of 8,376 meters in the Puerto Rico Trench.
--- fact 2
Norse voyagers were the first people known to have crossed the North Atlantic, reaching Greenland and, around 1000 CE, North America.
--- fact 3
Christopher Columbus's 1492 voyage opened sustained, repeated crossings between Europe and the Americas, beginning an age of European exploration and colonization that the vault treats through the Columbian Exchange.
--- fact 4
From the 16th to the 19th centuries the ocean carried the Atlantic slave trade; estimates for the number of enslaved Africans embarked run to about 12.5 million, of whom roughly 10.7 million are estimated to have survived the crossing.
--- fact 5
The first transatlantic telegraph cable operated briefly from August to October 1858 before failing; a successful and lasting cable was laid by the ship Great Eastern in 1866, linking Ireland and Newfoundland by telegraph.
--- fact 6
The Atlantic remains central to global shipping and fishing, and its western basin is the source of Atlantic hurricanes that form there in summer and autumn.
--- context
Norse crossings of the Atlantic preceded the far larger movement of people, goods, animals and diseases in the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], which the ocean also carried the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] across. The 1866 cable was an early feat of the [[technology/telegraph-and-submarine-cables|Telegraph and Submarine Cables]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] by its earliest crossing and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Atlantic went from a barrier crossed rarely and by a few to the busiest exchange-web on the planet within about five centuries, and that the same routes that carried crops, animals and settlers also carried millions of enslaved people, a use of the ocean that the Observer records as sustained, organized harm rather than as a footnote to trade. The 1866 telegraph cable shows the ocean being crossed a third way, by signal rather than by ship, compressing communication from weeks to minutes. The reading would weaken if the Norse voyages prove to have had more lasting exchange with North America than the archaeological record currently shows.
--- question 1
How much contact, if any, did Norse voyagers have with Indigenous peoples of northeastern North America beyond the brief presence recorded at sites like L'Anse aux Meadows?
--- question 2
How did the 1866 telegraph cable change the pace of transatlantic trade and politics in the decades that followed?

=== place/botany-bay
title: Botany Bay
type: place
date: 1770
themes: war, trade
src: 3e07180e9f27
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Botany Bay is an inlet on the coast of Sydney, Australia, where the Gweagal and Kamaygal peoples lived for thousands of years and where James Cook landed in 1770, though the British colony of 1788 was founded nearby at Port Jackson instead.
--- fact 1
Botany Bay lies about 13 kilometers south of central Sydney; the Gweagal lived on its south shore and the Kamaygal on its north, part of a wider set of at least 28 clans in the region, who call the bay Kamay in the Dharawal language, with archaeological evidence of occupation going back some 5,000 years.
--- fact 2
James Cook landed at Kurnell on 29 April 1770; he first called the site Stingrays Harbour, then renamed it Botany Bay for the many plants collected there by the naturalists Joseph Banks and Daniel Solander.
--- fact 3
Cook's landing party had a brief and wary encounter with local Aboriginal people; when the First Fleet arrived in January 1788, Aboriginal observers were reported as curious but suspicious of the newcomers.
--- fact 4
Governor Arthur Phillip found Botany Bay unsuitable for the planned colony because of poor freshwater, shallow anchorage, weak soil and exposed, swampy ground, and on 26 January 1788 he moved the fleet to found the settlement at Sydney Cove on Port Jackson instead.
--- fact 5
Sydney Airport now occupies the northwestern shore of the bay, with runways extending into it, and Port Botany is a major container port.
--- context
Botany Bay was long the country of the [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]] before Cook's 1770 landing, and the colony that followed at Port Jackson began the [[event/first-fleet-and-the-colonization-of-australia|First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]]. Like [[place/uluru|Uluru]], it is a place where a European-given name sits over a much older Aboriginal name and presence. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Botany Bay is remembered in outside accounts mainly for a brief 1770 landing and for being passed over in 1788, which risks erasing the much longer claim-line of the Gweagal and Kamaygal who lived there for thousands of years before either visit. The colony's move to Port Jackson was a practical decision about water and anchorage, not a decision about whose country it was, and the disruption that followed for Aboriginal people around Sydney is recorded here as sustained harm, whichever bay the ships used. The reading would weaken if evidence shows more direct conflict at Cook's 1770 landing than the brief, wary encounter usually described.
--- question 1
How did Gweagal and Kamaygal communities understand and respond to Cook's 1770 visit, in their own terms rather than the ship's log?
--- question 2
How did the colony's move to Port Jackson change the impact of British settlement on the peoples of the Botany Bay area compared with what a Botany Bay colony would have done?

=== place/lake-titicaca
title: Lake Titicaca
type: place
date: c. 1500 BCE
themes: religion, earth-systems
src: 9b03230da316
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Lake Titicaca is a large high-altitude lake on the border of Peru and Bolivia, sacred to the Inca as the birthplace of their first rulers and long settled by farming and fishing communities of the Andean highlands.
--- fact 1
The lake lies at about 3,812 meters elevation, with a surface area of about 8,372 square kilometers, and is often called the world's highest navigable lake for commercial craft.
--- fact 2
Communities around the lake farmed and fished from at least the mid-2nd millennium BCE; the date_start of -1500 marks the period conventionally associated with early settled use of the basin, and older human presence in the Andes belongs to a different brief.
--- fact 3
The Tiwanaku civilization, centered south of the lake, built a temple on the Island of the Moon by about 650 to 1000 CE and left pottery and stonework recovered from the islands.
--- fact 4
Inca tradition holds that the first Inca ruler, Manco Cápac, and his sister-wife Mama Ocllo, emerged from the lake or its Island of the Sun by order of the sun god Inti; the Sacred Rock on the Island of the Sun is linked in tradition to Manco Cápac's remains.
--- fact 5
The Uros people build and live on floating islands made from layers of totora reed; about 1,200 Uros lived on some 60 such islands as of 2011.
--- fact 6
A 2011 United Nations report found high concentrations of cadmium, arsenic and lead in parts of the lake, and its water level fell 81 centimeters between April and November 2009, attributed to shorter rainy seasons and glacier melt in its tributaries.
--- context
Lake Titicaca is the traditional birthplace of the ruling line of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]], whose later capital was [[place/cusco|Cusco]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] by the date_start used here and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a body of water this large and this high became a meaning-engine's point of origin: the Inca placed their ruling ancestors' arrival at the lake's islands, tying a kin-lattice claim to a landscape rather than to a single city. The Uros islands are themselves a technology, floating ground built and rebuilt from reed, which the Observer reads as a claim-line drawn on water rather than land. The reading would weaken if the Manco Cápac tradition is shown to be a later invention with little connection to how the earliest lake communities saw their own origins.
--- question 1
How far back does continuous human use of the Titicaca basin go, and what shaped its earliest settlement?
--- question 2
How much of the lake's current pollution and water-level decline is reversible?

=== place/marajo-island
title: Marajó Island
type: place
date: c. 400 BCE – 1600
themes: kinship, art
src: 00316d5b6bc0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Marajó Island, at the mouth of the Amazon River in Pará, Brazil, was home to the Marajoara culture, which built earthen mounds and made elaborate pottery from about 400 BCE until its decline after European contact in the 16th century.
--- fact 1
Marajó, with a land area of about 40,100 square kilometers, is the second-largest island in South America, comparable in size to Switzerland.
--- fact 2
The Marajoara culture existed from about 400 BCE to around 1600 CE; people lived in houses with tamped-earth floors, organized in matrilineal clans, and made large ceremonial pottery, including burial urns.
--- fact 3
Archaeologist Betty Meggers argued the culture was founded by migrants from the Andes and declined from about 1400 due to poor soil; Anna Roosevelt's later research argued instead that Marajoara society developed independently in the Amazon, with intensive farming and large-scale earthworks, and estimated a peak population of more than 100,000.
--- fact 4
European contact in the 16th century brought epidemic disease, and an estimated 90 percent of the island's Indigenous population died from introduced illnesses to which they had no immunity.
--- fact 5
The island's economy today rests partly on introduced water buffalo, whose herds, numbering about 450,000, outnumber the roughly 400,000 human residents; lumber and açaí palm are also produced there.
--- context
Marajó Island's builders are the [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]], part of the wider evidence gathered under [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]] for complex pre-Columbian societies in the [[place/amazon-river|Amazon River]] basin; its population collapse followed the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that two readings of Marajó, one crediting outside migrants and environmental limits, the other crediting an independent and well-organized society, disagreed less about the pottery and mounds themselves than about how much complexity to expect from a society outside the usual centers of early states, an assumption the Observer treats as itself worth questioning. The population's near-collapse after contact is recorded here as suffering on a scale that removed most of the people who could have explained their own society firsthand, which is part of why the debate persisted so long. The reading would weaken if further excavation shows population and organization closer to Meggers's lower estimates.
--- question 1
How large and how socially organized was Marajoara society at its peak, and how much did later scholars' assumptions about the Amazon shape earlier estimates?
--- question 2
How much of the culture's own memory of itself was lost in the population collapse after contact?

=== place/nan-madol
title: Nan Madol
type: place
date: c. 900 CE – 1628
themes: kinship, technology
src: 3e2e18d60e0d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nan Madol is a ruined city of artificial stone islets built in a lagoon off Pohnpei, in the Federated States of Micronesia, the ceremonial and political center of the Saudeleur dynasty from about the 12th century until around 1628.
--- fact 1
Nan Madol consists of about 92 artificial islets built from stacked basalt columns and coral fill, linked by tidal canals, over a core area of about 1.5 by 0.5 kilometers.
--- fact 2
Human activity at the site dates from as early as the 1st or 2nd century CE, islet construction began in the 8th or 9th century, and the megalithic stonework that gives the site its present form was built from about 1180 to 1200.
--- fact 3
Tradition holds that the Saudeleur dynasty ruled Pohnpei from Nan Madol, uniting an estimated population of about 25,000 on the island, though the number of people living at Nan Madol itself was probably under 1,000.
--- fact 4
Around 1628 a warrior named Isokelekel is said in tradition to have invaded and ended Saudeleur rule; some scholars also point to population decline as a contributing cause of the site's abandonment.
--- fact 5
UNESCO inscribed Nan Madol as a World Heritage Site in 2016.
--- context
Nan Madol's builders were part of the wider settlement of the Pacific carried by the [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] and the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]], and Micronesian voyaging traditions share roots with cultures such as the [[culture/lapita-culture|Lapita Culture]] farther south and east. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Nan Madol turned a lagoon into a claim-line: a ruling lineage built its seat on stone islets separated from the rest of the island by water, a status-signal that set the center of power physically apart from those it ruled. The tradition of Isokelekel's conquest, like many founding and ending stories, is ancestor-weight carried forward by the community rather than a document, and the Observer treats it as a claim to be tested rather than a settled fact. The reading would weaken if archaeology shows the site's decline came mainly from environmental or demographic causes rather than conquest.
--- question 1
How much of the Isokelekel tradition reflects an actual conquest, and how much is later legend explaining Saudeleur rule's end?
--- question 2
How was the labor to move and stack the basalt columns organized, given Pohnpei's small population?

=== place/nazca-lines
title: Nazca Lines
type: place
date: c. 400 BCE – 500 CE
themes: religion, science
src: 00bc2eab0a35
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Nazca Lines are a group of geoglyphs in the desert of southern Peru, made between about 400 BCE and 500 CE by the Paracas and Nazca cultures, showing animal, plant and geometric shapes and hundreds of straight lines visible mainly from the air.
--- fact 1
The lines lie in the Nazca Desert between the towns of Nazca and Palpa, about 400 kilometers south of Lima, and cover about 50 square kilometers.
--- fact 2
They were made by removing a top layer of reddish, iron-oxide-coated pebbles to expose lighter ground beneath; the lines and figures cut roughly 10 to 15 centimeters deep.
--- fact 3
The combined lines run more than 1,300 kilometers, and the largest figures, such as a 134-meter condor and a 93-meter hummingbird, reach lengths of tens to a few hundred meters.
--- fact 4
Peruvian archaeologist Toribio Mejía Xesspe noted the lines on foot in 1927; American historian Paul Kosok observed them from the air in 1940 to 1941 and proposed astronomical alignments, a theory developed further by Maria Reiche; researchers Gerald Hawkins and Anthony Aveni concluded in 1990 that the evidence did not support a systematic astronomical purpose.
--- fact 5
Other proposed purposes include ritual paths connected to water and fertility, walked in ceremony, and markers tied to underground water sources; no single explanation is agreed on.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Lines and Geoglyphs of Nazca and Pampas de Jumana in 1994.
--- fact 7
The site has been damaged by squatters, a limestone quarry, a 2014 Greenpeace protest that left marks near one geoglyph, and a 2018 truck crossing, and erosion and off-road vehicles remain ongoing threats.
--- context
The Nazca Lines were made well before the rise of [[place/cusco|Cusco]] and the Inca state farther south in the Andes. They sit in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a mark made to be read from the ground and a mark made to be read from the air are different kinds of pattern-taming, and the debate over the Lines is partly a debate over which kind their makers intended. Reiche's proposal that they served as an astronomical instrument was a claim about precision that later analysis did not confirm, which the Observer treats as an example of how a compelling reading can outrun its evidence. Because no written record from the makers survives, every purpose offered here is inference from the marks themselves. The reading would weaken if excavation along the lines turns up consistent ritual deposits that point to one purpose over the others.
--- question 1
Were the lines walked as ritual paths, viewed as an observatory, or used for another purpose entirely?
--- question 2
How did the people who made the Lines organize the labor needed to plan and clear such long, straight paths?

=== place/uluru
title: Uluru
type: place
date: c. 8000 BCE
themes: religion, earth-systems
src: 4d00ac9a1d61
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Uluru is a large sandstone inselberg in central Australia, sacred to the Anangu people, who have been associated with the site for more than 10,000 years and hold traditional custodianship of it today.
--- fact 1
Uluru rises 348 meters above the surrounding plain (863 meters above sea level) and lies about 335 kilometers southwest of Alice Springs; it is composed of arkose sandstone whose lack of internal jointing has left it largely free of scree slopes.
--- fact 2
Archaeological evidence shows human presence in the area for more than 10,000 years; the date_start of -8000 is a round figure within that range, and older evidence for the peopling of the surrounding region belongs to a different brief.
--- fact 3
The Anangu, whose Pitjantjatjara and Yankunytjatjara traditions call the rock's stories Tjukurpa, hold that ancestral beings shaped the landscape, and some tracks and sites at Uluru carry restrictions tied to gender and ceremony.
--- fact 4
Surveyor William Gosse named the formation Ayers Rock on 19 July 1873; the government returned ownership to the local Pitjantjatjara people on 26 October 1985, under a 99-year lease-back and joint management arrangement, and dual naming (later reordered to Uluru / Ayers Rock in 2002) followed in 1993.
--- fact 5
The Uluru-Kata Tjuta National Park board voted in 2017 to end recreational climbing, which had long concerned the Anangu as crossing a sacred track and as a safety risk; the ban took effect on 26 October 2019.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed Uluru-Kata Tjuta National Park in 1987.
--- context
Uluru has been significant to the [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]] since long after the [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]] first brought people to the continent, and its handback in 1985 and the 2019 climbing ban came long after the disruption of the [[event/first-fleet-and-the-colonization-of-australia|First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]]. It sits in the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Uluru carried two names and two systems of meaning at once for over a century, a European name attached by a surveyor and a body of Tjukurpa tradition held by the Anangu, and that the 1985 handback and the 2019 climbing ban both moved authority over the site back toward the tradition that predates the European name. Treating Tjukurpa as tradition rather than as folklore to be judged is a deliberate choice made here, following the same standard applied to other traditions in this record. The reading would weaken if the closure of the climb is shown to have been driven mainly by liability concerns rather than respect for Anangu wishes.
--- question 1
How has joint management under the 1985 agreement changed decisions about the site since then?
--- question 2
How many of the deaths and injuries associated with the former climbing route shaped the 2017 decision, alongside the Anangu's own long-standing requests?

=== culture/ancestral-puebloans
title: Ancestral Puebloans
type: culture
date: c. 700 CE – 1300
themes: earth-systems, art
src: f6ae5cf1848f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Ancestral Puebloans built large stone and cliff settlements across the American Southwest from about 700 to 1300 CE, including the great houses of Chaco Canyon and the cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde, before drought and hardship led them to disperse into communities that became today's Pueblo peoples.
--- fact 1
Their architecture developed from simple pit houses into multi-room stone great houses, most visible today at Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde.
--- fact 2
Mesa Verde was occupied from about 450 to 1300 CE, and most of its famous cliff dwellings were built between 1190 and 1300.
--- fact 3
Both Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde were abandoned by about 1300 CE.
--- fact 4
A prolonged drought from 1276 to 1299, alongside food shortages, is the leading explanation, as communities moved south into what is now Arizona and New Mexico.
--- fact 5
Their descendants live today as the Hopi, Zuni and other Pueblo peoples of the Rio Grande region.
--- context
The Ancestral Puebloans built [[place/chaco-canyon|Chaco Canyon]] and [[place/mesa-verde|Mesa Verde]] in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], then dispersed under climatic pressure into the communities that became the Pueblo peoples. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a long drought, rather than conquest or disease, ended two of the era's largest settlement complexes in North America, and that the people who left did not vanish but continued as named communities in the same region. The word "ancestral" acknowledges a continuity that popular labels like "vanished" once denied. A specialist could argue that drought alone cannot explain the timing and manner of abandonment and that social or political strain within the settlements compounded it, which would weaken a purely climatic reading.
--- question 1
How much did social or political conflict contribute to abandonment, alongside drought?
--- question 2
How direct is the line between specific Ancestral Puebloan communities and specific modern Pueblo nations?
