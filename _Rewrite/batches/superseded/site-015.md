=== event/peopling-of-europe-by-homo-sapiens
title: Peopling of Europe by Homo sapiens
type: event
date: c. 45 ka
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: ffd0ae06f91d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Homo sapiens reached Europe by about 45,000 years ago, and possibly earlier, entering a continent already occupied by Neanderthals. The earliest securely dated remains, from Bacho Kiro Cave in Bulgaria and Ranis in Germany, are about 45,000 years old.
--- fact 1
At Bacho Kiro Cave, Bulgaria, Homo sapiens fossils dated to about 46,000 to 44,000 years ago were found with Initial Upper Paleolithic stone tools.
--- fact 2
At Ranis, Germany, bone fragments directly dated in 2024 to about 45,000 years ago came with leaf-shaped stone points also known from Britain, which shows an early reach into northern latitudes.
--- fact 3
At Grotte Mandrin, France, a child's tooth and distinctive stone points dated to about 54,000 years ago were attributed to Homo sapiens in 2022. The occupation appears to have been brief, and some researchers find the attribution unconvincing.
--- fact 4
Neanderthals and Homo sapiens overlapped in Europe for several thousand years, and some early Europeans had Neanderthal ancestors only a few generations back; see [[event/neanderthal-extinction|Neanderthal Extinction]] and [[event/interbreeding-of-homo-sapiens-with-neanderthals-and-denisovans|Interbreeding of Homo sapiens with Neanderthals and Denisovans]].
--- fact 5
Proposed routes include the Danube corridor and the Mediterranean coast; the evidence does not settle which was used.
--- context
The peopling of Europe follows the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]] and belongs to the [[era/upper-paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]], with new stone-tool industries such as the Aurignacian and the [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]] seen at [[place/chauvet-cave|Chauvet Cave]]. It overlaps the last [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] and precedes the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' arrival in Europe looks less like one march than like several small, sometimes brief settlements in land already held by another human species. The Observer takes the Mandrin tooth and the Bacho Kiro jaws as reminders that "first arrival" describes the evidence found so far, not the past itself. The reading would weaken if the earliest sites proved to belong to one continuous, growing population.
--- question 1
Did the 54,000-year-old occupation at Mandrin belong to Homo sapiens?
--- question 2
How much ancestry from the earliest Europeans survives in later Europeans?

=== event/peopling-of-sahul
title: Peopling of Sahul
type: event
date: c. 50 ka
themes: technology, earth-systems, kinship
src: d001359cafc8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Modern humans reached Sahul, the Pleistocene landmass joining Australia and New Guinea, at least 50,000 years ago and possibly about 65,000 years ago.
--- fact 1
Reaching Sahul required crossing open sea in the Wallacea island chain, making it among the earliest known deliberate sea crossings.
--- fact 2
Rock-shelter finds at Madjedbebe in Arnhem Land have been dated to about 65,000 years ago, a date that is debated; about 50,000 years ago is widely accepted.
--- fact 3
Human remains at Lake Mungo are about 40,000 years old.
--- fact 4
Many giant marsupials disappeared within several thousand years of human arrival; the relative roles of people and climate are debated.
--- context
The settlement followed the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]] and founded the ancestry of the [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]]. Compare the later [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species crossed open water tens of thousands of years before it built anything that could be called a city. Boat-building and planning are far older than farming.
--- question 1
How early did the first boats cross Wallacea?

=== event/peopling-of-the-americas
title: Peopling of the Americas
type: event
date: c. 23 – 13 ka
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: f0538815e7c3
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Humans reached the Americas from northeastern Asia by at least 15,000 years ago, and possibly much earlier. The earliest date is contested.
--- fact 1
Footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, have been dated to about 21,000 to 23,000 years ago. The first dates used seeds of an aquatic plant, which critics said could read too old. A 2023 study radiocarbon-dated terrestrial pollen from the same layers and applied luminescence dating to the sediments, and reported that the original chronology is robust. Some researchers still question the dating.
--- fact 2
The old 'Clovis-first' model, which put the first arrival about 13,000 years ago, is no longer accepted. Sites such as Cooper's Ferry in Idaho, about 16,000 years old, predate Clovis.
--- fact 3
Two routes are proposed. The inland ice-free corridor is now thought to have opened only about 13,800 years ago, later than the earliest sites, which makes a Pacific coastal route the more likely path for the first arrivals.
--- fact 4
Genetic studies show that Indigenous Americans descend from a mixture of ancestry related to ancient North Eurasians in Siberia and to East Asians. Scholars now generally favor several small migrations over a single event.
--- context
The migration follows the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]], occurred around the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]], and was the last major expansion into unoccupied continents before the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that within a few thousand years humans spread from Alaska to Tierra del Fuego. The pace suggests a species that carries its environment, in the form of knowledge, with it.
--- question 1
When did the first migrants arrive, and by what route? Some proposed sites, such as Chiquihuite Cave in Mexico (about 26,000 years), are disputed because it is not settled that their stones are human-made.

=== event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific
title: Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific
type: event
date: c. 1000 – 1300
themes: technology, kinship, science
src: 662883401715
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Polynesian navigators settled the last remote islands of the central and eastern Pacific, culminating with Aotearoa New Zealand around 1250 to 1300 CE.
--- fact 1
Ancestors of Polynesians, associated with the Lapita culture, reached Samoa and Tonga about 3,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
A pause of roughly a thousand years preceded the final expansion to Hawai'i, Rapa Nui, and Aotearoa.
--- fact 3
Voyagers used star paths, swells, and bird flight rather than instruments.
--- fact 4
Ancient DNA and language link the settlers to Austronesian speakers from Island Southeast Asia.
--- context
The settlement was the last great human dispersal into unoccupied lands, after the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]]. See [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]].
--- observer
The Observer regards this as one of the species' most extraordinary achievements in knowledge: crossing thousands of kilometers of open ocean without instruments, using memorized navigation.
--- question 1
Why was there a pause before the final wave?

src: 551d08998cef
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Vasco da Gama's fleet reached Calicut in India on 20 May 1498 after sailing around Africa, opening a direct sea route from Europe to Asia.
--- fact 1
Da Gama left Lisbon in July 1497 and rounded the Cape of Good Hope in November.
--- fact 2
He reached Calicut on the Malabar Coast in May 1498.
--- fact 3
The Portuguese armed ships with cannon and later seized ports, including Goa in 1510.
--- fact 4
Their entry gave Europe direct access to Asian spices without Middle Eastern intermediaries.
--- fact 5
The Indian Ocean had earlier been crossed by large Chinese fleets under Zheng He, whose voyages ended in 1433.
--- context
The voyage created a new connection in the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] and, with the voyages behind the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], began direct European trade across the oceans. The Chinese voyages of [[person/zheng-he|Zheng He]] had reached the same waters decades earlier. Portuguese Sea Route to India sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the newcomers entered a busy ocean with no dominant navy and made it dangerous with cannon-armed ships. What changed was less the route than the willingness to fight for control of it.

How much did the new route change the spice trade?

=== event/protestant-reformation
title: Protestant Reformation
type: event
date: 1517 – 1648
themes: religion, language
src: fc4f813ccf7b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Protestant Reformation began in 1517 when Martin Luther publicized his Ninety-five Theses against church practices, and it split Western Christianity into Catholic and Protestant churches.
--- fact 1
Luther sent his Ninety-five Theses to Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz in October 1517; the tradition that he nailed them to a church door is uncertain.
--- fact 2
He was summoned to the Diet of Worms in 1521 and refused to recant.
--- fact 3
His German New Testament appeared in 1522, and the whole Bible in 1534.
--- fact 4
Other reformers included Huldrych Zwingli and John Calvin, and Henry VIII established the Church of England.
--- fact 5
The religious wars that followed continued into the Thirty Years' War, ending in 1648.
--- context
The Reformation used the [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]] invented by [[person/johannes-gutenberg|Johannes Gutenberg]] to spread pamphlets and translated Bibles. Its conflicts ended in the [[event/thirty-years-war|Thirty Years' War]] and the [[event/peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]]. Protestant Reformation sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a new technology for copying text allowed a dispute among clerics to become a mass movement within a few years. Once meaning-engines can be reproduced cheaply, rival versions multiply.
--- question 1
How central was printing to the Reformation's success?

=== event/quaternary-megafauna-extinctions
title: Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions
type: event
date: c. 50 ka – 10000 BCE
themes: earth-systems
src: 8248ce1b4a49
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between roughly 50,000 and 10,000 years ago, a large share of the world's biggest land animals went extinct, with the timing differing by continent and often following human arrival.
--- fact 1
Animals lost include the woolly mammoth, woolly rhinoceros, giant ground sloths, glyptodonts, the giant marsupial Diprotodon, and the giant bird Genyornis.
--- fact 2
Losses were proportionally severest in Australia, the Americas, and northern Eurasia.
--- fact 3
Africa lost proportionally fewer large species, which is often explained by its large animals having evolved alongside hominins.
--- fact 4
The overkill hypothesis attributes the extinctions mainly to human hunting; the climate hypothesis attributes them to environmental change.
--- fact 5
Recent global analyses link the declines more closely to the expansion of Homo sapiens than to climate change, though regional debate continues.
--- context
The extinctions accompany human expansion into [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]] and the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]], and the retreat of the [[species/woolly-mammoth|Woolly Mammoth]] during the shifts around the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions sits in the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the same period saw the species arrive on new continents and the largest animals on those continents disappear. Whether the species was the primary cause is contested, but it is the first time its impact on other life was large enough to be a candidate cause.
--- question 1
How much of each regional extinction was due to humans and how much to climate?

=== event/radiation-of-placental-mammals
title: Radiation of Placental Mammals
type: event
date: 66 – 50 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c93d745d2b2d
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The radiation of placental mammals was the rapid diversification of the placental group of mammals after the end-Cretaceous extinction, from about 66 to 50 million years ago. Most living mammals belong to it.
--- fact 1
Placental mammals bear well-developed young nourished by a placenta. Most living mammal species, including rodents, bats, whales, hoofed animals and primates, are placentals.
--- fact 2
In the fossil record, large herbivores and predators appear within a few million years after the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], and many modern orders are recognizable by about 50 million years ago.
--- fact 3
Molecular-clock studies place the origin of the placental orders in the Cretaceous, while the oldest fossils assigned to most modern orders are Paleogene. Explosive, short-fuse and long-fuse models of the timing are all argued.
--- fact 4
The [[event/paleocene-eocene-thermal-maximum|Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum]], about 56 million years ago, coincides with the first appearance in the Northern Hemisphere of primates, horses and artiodactyls.
--- fact 5
Whales evolved from land-dwelling hoofed mammals over about ten million years, from about 50 to 40 million years ago.
--- context
The Radiation of Placental Mammals follows the [[event/origin-of-mammals|Origin of Mammals]] and the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]] and fills much of the [[era/paleogene-period|Paleogene Period]] of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the rise of the mammals followed the loss of the dominant land animals, and that the same pattern of vacated space followed other extinctions. It reads the radiation as a consequence of opening, not of superiority. The reading would weaken if the fossil record showed large placental mammals already common before the extinction.
--- question 1
Did the modern orders arise in the Cretaceous or only after the extinction?
--- question 2
What role did warming episodes such as the thermal maximum play in the radiation?

=== event/renaissance
title: Renaissance
type: event
date: c. 1400 – 1600
themes: art, science
src: b486eb13e13a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Renaissance was a movement of art, learning, and political thought, beginning in Italian city-states such as Florence in the 14th and 15th centuries and spreading across Europe.
--- fact 1
Italian humanists such as Petrarch recovered and studied classical Latin and Greek texts.
--- fact 2
Filippo Brunelleschi developed linear perspective in Florence in experiments of about 1410 to 1415; Leon Battista Alberti codified it in 1435.
--- fact 3
Leonardo da Vinci (1452 to 1519), Michelangelo (1475 to 1564), and Raphael (1483 to 1520) worked under the patronage of wealthy families and the Church.
--- fact 4
The Medici family of Florence financed artists and scholars through banking.
--- fact 5
The idea of a distinct age of rebirth was popularized in the 19th century, notably by Jacob Burckhardt in 1860; historians debate how sharply it broke from the medieval period.
--- context
The Renaissance revived the heritage of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]] and was amplified by the [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]]. It overlapped the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]]. Renaissance sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a period defined itself as a rebirth of an earlier age, and that later historians adopted the label. A culture may draw its identity from a chosen ancestor that it partly invents.
--- question 1
Was the Renaissance a break with the Middle Ages or a continuation?

=== event/russian-expansion-into-siberia
title: Russian Expansion into Siberia
type: event
date: 1582 – 1639
themes: war, trade, earth-systems
src: e49e304fba59
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between 1582 and 1639, Russian Cossacks and traders advanced across Siberia to the Pacific, driven by the fur trade and by tribute (yasak) taken from Indigenous peoples.
--- fact 1
In 1582 the Cossack leader Yermak, with about 540 Cossacks and 300 Livonian war prisoners, seized the capital of the Khanate of Sibir.
--- fact 2
Russians reached the Pacific at Okhotsk by 1639.
--- fact 3
The advance was driven by European demand for luxury furs.
--- fact 4
Yasak, a fur tribute, was demanded of men aged eighteen to fifty; Indigenous populations suffered from disease, violence, and forced levies.
--- context
The advance followed the collapse of steppe khanates after the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] and is a parallel to the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. See [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]] and [[culture/yakut-sakha|Yakut (Sakha)]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a frontier that moved across thousands of kilometers in about six decades, powered by small bands living off the fur trade. Commodity demand can move borders faster than armies.
--- question 1
How large was the Indigenous population loss in the first century of Russian rule?

The revolution grew out of the strains of [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and created the Soviet state, one of the two poles of the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. The Scientific Revolution was the shift, roughly 1543 to 1687, toward systematic experiment, mathematics, and observation as the basis for knowledge of nature, centered in Europe.

Marker works include Copernicus's heliocentric model and Vesalius's anatomy (both 1543).

It drew on earlier work in the Islamic world, India, and China; see [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]].
--- fact 4
Historians debate whether it was a single 'revolution'.

The revolution sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and was enabled by the [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]]. See [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer calls this pattern-taming and notes a distinctive feature: the institutionalization of doubt. Claims are supposed to be tested and are open to revision by strangers.

=== event/scramble-for-africa
title: Scramble for Africa
type: event
date: c. 1881 – 1914
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: d9ad05168519
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Scramble for Africa was the rapid partition of the African continent by European powers between about 1881 and 1914, in which the share of Africa under European control rose from roughly 10 percent to about 90 percent.
--- fact 1
The Berlin Conference of November 1884 to February 1885 set rules for claiming territory but did not itself divide the continent.
--- fact 2
By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent of European rule.
--- fact 3
African societies resisted in many wars, including the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]] and the Anglo-Zulu War; see [[culture/zulu-kingdom|Zulu Kingdom]].
--- fact 4
Colonial borders were drawn with little regard for existing societies; many remain today.
--- context
See [[event/congo-free-state|Congo Free State]] and [[culture/sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]]. The partition is the background to [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a process of claim-lines drawn on maps, in a distant capital, over the homes of millions who were not consulted. The lines outlasted the empires that drew them. See [[observer-note/the-fiction-of-claim-lines|The Fiction of Claim-Lines]].
--- question 1
How did colonial borders shape later state formation?

=== event/second-italo-ethiopian-war
title: Second Italo-Ethiopian War
type: event
date: 1935 – 1936
themes: war, technology
src: d0f0e15fe0ab
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Second Italo-Ethiopian War (October 1935 to May 1936) was Italy's colonial invasion of Ethiopia, in which Italian forces used mustard gas, took the capital, and drove out Emperor Haile Selassie, while the League of Nations failed to stop them. Ethiopia was liberated in 1941.
--- fact 1
The war began on 3 October 1935, when General Emilio De Bono crossed the Mareb River into Ethiopia without a formal declaration of war. Emperor Haile Selassie I led the defence, and Benito Mussolini claimed victory in 1936.
--- fact 2
Italian forces took the capital, Addis Ababa, on 5 May 1936. Haile Selassie went into exile, first to Palestine and then to England.
--- fact 3
Italy used mustard gas in the war, in violation of the international agreements it had signed.
--- fact 4
Both Italy and Ethiopia were members of the League of Nations. The League failed to stop Italy or protect Ethiopia, and the episode is remembered as exposing its weakness.
--- fact 5
Italian rule was ended in 1941 by Ethiopian resistance fighters together with Allied forces.
--- context
The war reversed the outcome of the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]] of 1896, in which Ethiopia under [[person/menelik-ii|Menelik II]] had defeated an Italian army, and it stood apart from the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]] as a late colonial conquest. It is often seen as a prelude to [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]].
--- observer
The Observer records the use of chemical weapons against a state that had appealed to a body of nations for protection, and the failure of that body to act. A story-glue of collective security proved to be only as strong as the willingness of its members to bear a cost.
--- question 1
What was the number of Ethiopian civilians and soldiers killed by gas and by other means?
--- question 2
How much did Ethiopian resistance during the occupation contribute to the liberation of 1941, compared with Allied forces?

=== event/second-sino-japanese-war
title: Second Sino-Japanese War
type: event
date: 1937 – 1945
themes: war
src: fa08ae0d7e1f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Second Sino-Japanese War (1937 to 1945) was the full-scale war between Japan and China that began at the Marco Polo Bridge near Beijing and later merged into World War II. It included mass killings of civilians, among them at Nanjing, whose death toll is disputed.
--- fact 1
Full-scale war began on 7 July 1937 after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident near Beijing. It was an undeclared war that quickly grew into large-scale fighting in northern China.
--- fact 2
The incident hastened the formation of the Second United Front between the Kuomintang and the Chinese Communist Party.
--- fact 3
Japanese forces took Nanjing on 13 December 1937 and carried out mass killings there beginning that day. Estimates of the number of Chinese killed range from about 100,000 to more than 300,000. One scholarly assessment gives about 50,000 killed within the city walls in the first days and more than 100,000 but fewer than 200,000 in the city and surrounding counties by March 1938.
--- fact 4
The figures are contested. Some Japanese revisionists give much lower numbers, while some Chinese official figures are higher than the scholarly range.
--- context
The war followed Japan's industrialization and expansion after the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]] and merged into [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]] after 1941. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer records the mass killings at Nanjing as an atrocity, whatever the argument over the count. That the number itself has become a claim-line between governments and historians shows how a single figure can carry the weight of a whole nation's memory.
--- question 1
How should the Nanjing death toll be established, given records that are incomplete and sources that differ by nationality?
--- question 2
How many Chinese died in total in the war, including famine and disease?

=== event/seven-years-war
title: Seven Years' War
type: event
date: 1756 – 1763
themes: war, trade
src: 17ce8da21a66
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Seven Years' War (1756 to 1763) was fought between Britain and France, and their allies, on several continents, and left Britain dominant in North America and India.
--- fact 1
The war was fought in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and the Philippines.
--- fact 2
In North America it is known as the French and Indian War.
--- fact 3
The Treaty of Paris in 1763 gave Britain Canada and French territory east of the Mississippi.
--- fact 4
British victory at Plassey in 1757 began the East India Company's rule over Bengal.
--- fact 5
Britain's war debts led to new taxes on the American colonies.
--- context
The war is sometimes called the first global war. Its consequences fed the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]], and it reshaped control of trade in India and the Atlantic, including the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. === event/siberian-traps-eruptions
title: Siberian Traps Eruptions
type: event
date: 252 – 251 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 2f6dde36e8aa
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Siberian Traps eruptions were one of the largest volcanic outpourings of the last 500 million years, in what is now northern Siberia, at about 252 to 251 million years ago. They coincide with the end-Permian mass extinction.
--- fact 1
The eruptions produced lava flows and intrusions over a huge area of northern Siberia, with estimates of the volume running from about one to several million cubic kilometres.
--- fact 2
Precise radiometric dating places the main phase within a window of about a million years or less that brackets the extinction, near 251.9 million years ago.
--- fact 3
Proposed killing mechanisms include large releases of carbon dioxide and methane that warmed the planet, ocean acidification and oxygen loss, and toxic gases from sulfur and halogens. Magma intruding coal, carbonate and salt-bearing rocks may have released large gas volumes.
--- fact 4
Marine and land records show a rapid warming of the tropics and oceans at the extinction; how much of it the eruptions caused is debated.
--- fact 5
The extinction is described in [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]].
--- context
The Siberian Traps Eruptions close the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]] and are the leading proposed cause of the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]], after which the [[era/triassic-period|Triassic Period]] opens the Mesozoic. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a volcanic episode lasting about a million years was enough to remove most of the life then living, and that comparisons with any modern process hold or fail on rates, not on totals. The Observer treats the timing question as central for that reason. The reading would weaken if the eruptions were shown to have started well before the extinction with little effect.
--- question 1
Which of the eruption's effects, from warming to toxic gases, did the most damage?
--- question 2
How closely does the eruption's timing match the extinction pulses?

=== event/spread-of-grasslands
title: Spread of Grasslands
type: event
date: 25 – 5 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 1bc890fce7dd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The spread of grasslands was the expansion of open grass-dominated vegetation across continents, from about 25 to 5 million years ago, in the Miocene. It changed animal life and set up the plant family that now supplies most of the world's staple crops.
--- fact 1
Grass pollen and microscopic plant silica are known from the latest Cretaceous or early Paleogene, but grasses were a minor part of vegetation for tens of millions of years.
--- fact 2
Open grasslands expanded from the early to middle Miocene, about 20 to 15 million years ago. Grasses using the C4 pathway, which suits hot, dry conditions and low carbon dioxide, spread widely about 8 to 6 million years ago, as shown by carbon isotopes in fossil soils and teeth.
--- fact 3
Proposed drivers include cooling and drying, seasonal rainfall, falling carbon dioxide and fire. Their relative roles are debated.
--- fact 4
Grazing mammals evolved high-crowned teeth and long limbs for running; horses shifted from browsing to grazing, and grazing antelope, cattle and their relatives diversified.
--- fact 5
The cereals, including wheat, rice and maize, are grasses; see [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]].
--- context
The Spread of Grasslands belongs mostly to the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and is a background to the story of the Miocene apes and early hominins. Its crop lineage is the source of [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]] and [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the staple foods of nearly every large human society come from a plant family that became widespread only in the last few tens of millions of years. Open landscapes are also the setting of a long-running hypothesis about human origins, which the Observer treats as a hypothesis, not a finding. It would weaken if early hominins proved to have lived mainly in closed woodland.
--- question 1
What mix of climate, carbon dioxide and fire drove the spread of C4 grasses?
--- question 2
How much did open grassland shape hominin evolution?

--- summary
The Tanzimat (Reorganization) was a series of reforms in the Ottoman Empire between 1839 and 1876 that tried to modernize the state, promise equality before the law to all subjects, and move it toward constitutional government.
--- fact 1
The era ran from 1839 to 1876 under the sultans Abdülmecid I and Abdülaziz.
--- fact 2
The Edict of Gülhane, proclaimed on 3 November 1839, promised security of life, property, and honour for all subjects, fair taxation and conscription, public trials, and an end to tax farming.

--- fact 4

The Tanzimat was a response to European pressure and to earlier reformers in the empire. Compare [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]] in Japan and the reforms of [[person/muhammad-ali-of-egypt|Muhammad Ali of Egypt]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a state trying to change its own story-glue while the empire was still being defended, extending equality to subjects of every faith in the hope of holding a plural population together. Reforms promised on paper met the resistance of those with something to lose, and the constitution lasted only two years in practice.

=== event/the-boring-billion
title: The Boring Billion
type: event
date: 1.8 Ga – 800 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 0956f91b83eb
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Boring Billion is an informal name for the interval from about 1.8 to 0.8 billion years ago, a span with no well-established ice ages and little apparent change in the fossil record. Whether it was really uneventful is debated.
--- fact 1
The interval covers the [[era/mesoproterozoic-era|Mesoproterozoic Era]] and the edges of the eras on either side of it, and it lies between the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] and the ice ages of [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]].
--- fact 2
Chemical records suggest that atmospheric oxygen stayed well below today's level and that some waters at middle depth were rich in sulfide. Shortages of nutrients such as nitrogen and trace metals have been proposed as limits on life.
--- fact 3
Eukaryotes were present and diversifying slowly. The red alga Bangiomorpha, dated in different studies to between roughly 1.05 and 1.2 billion years ago, belongs to this interval.
--- fact 4
The supercontinent Nuna broke apart and Rodinia began to assemble.
--- fact 5
Some researchers argue that the name misleads, because records show fluctuations in oxygen and ecological change within the interval.
--- context
The Boring Billion lies within the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] and follows the [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]]. It ends with the changes of the Neoproterozoic, including the break-up of Rodinia and the glaciations of [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that "boring" is a verdict on the surviving record, not on the planet, and that a billion years is long enough for slow processes to matter. The reading would weaken if better records showed genuine stasis in oxygen, climate and eukaryote diversity across the whole interval.
--- question 1
Was the interval truly stable, or does a sparse record hide change?
--- question 2
What kept oxygen and complex life at low levels for so long?

=== event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon
title: Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon
type: event
date: 4.51 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: c4ede0b43e4d
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
According to the giant-impact hypothesis, a Mars-sized body called Theia struck the young Earth about 4.5 billion years ago, and the debris formed the Moon.
--- fact 1
The hypothesis explains the Moon's relatively small iron core and its similarity to Earth's mantle in isotopes.
--- fact 2
Lunar rocks returned by the Apollo missions helped test the idea.
--- fact 3
The exact timing is uncertain, with estimates ranging from about 4.52 to 4.35 billion years ago: lunar zircons require the Moon to have formed within the first 60 million years of the Solar System, while the youngest estimates fall about 150 to 200 million years after it began.
--- fact 4
The Moon stabilizes Earth's axial tilt and produces most of Earth's tides.
--- context
The impact is an early event of the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]]. The Moon's stabilizing effect on climate is one reason Earth's long-term conditions stayed steady enough for complex life.
--- observer
The Observer sees a single accident with very long consequences. Human calendars, tides, and myths about the Moon all trace to a collision billions of years before any observer existed.
--- question 1
Was Theia a single impactor or were there several?
