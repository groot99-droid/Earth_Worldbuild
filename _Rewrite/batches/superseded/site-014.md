=== event/mfecane

--- summary
The Mfecane ('the crushing') is the name given to a period of upheaval, warfare, and migration across southern Africa in the early 19th century, associated with the rise of the Zulu Kingdom.
--- fact 1
Several new states emerged, including the Ndebele under Mzilikazi and the Swazi under Sobhuza.
--- fact 2
Many adopted Zulu-style military organization.
--- fact 3
Population movements reshaped the region and left large areas depopulated in some accounts.
--- fact 4
Historians debate the scale, the causes, and the role of Shaka, and some argue that European slave raiding and colonial pressure were also important.
--- context
See [[culture/zulu-kingdom|Zulu Kingdom]] and [[person/shaka|Shaka]]. The Mfecane came before the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]].

The Observer notes that the same event is named and interpreted differently by historians. A single dramatic story about one leader can hide a complex regional change.

=== event/middle-pleistocene-transition
title: Middle Pleistocene Transition
type: event
date: c. 1.25 Ma – 700 ka
themes: earth-systems
src: 57e9f1001ec2
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Middle Pleistocene Transition was a change in the rhythm of the ice ages between about 1.25 million and 0.7 million years ago. Glacial cycles lengthened from about 41,000 to about 100,000 years, with larger ice sheets and colder glacial stages.
--- fact 1
Marine oxygen-isotope records show glacial cycles paced by the 41,000-year tilt cycle until about 1.25 million years ago and dominated by cycles of about 100,000 years by about 0.7 million years ago. Estimates of the start and end vary.
--- fact 2
The 100,000-year cycles carry more ice and have a sawtooth shape: slow growth, then rapid melting.
--- fact 3
The shift came without a matching change in orbital forcing, so its cause lies inside the climate system. Proposed causes include declining atmospheric carbon dioxide, removal of loose regolith beneath northern ice sheets so that ice grew thicker and more stable, and changes in deep-ocean circulation.
--- fact 4
Some reconstructions place a slowdown of deep-ocean circulation near 0.9 million years ago; others find only modest change. Whether the shift was gradual or abrupt is debated.
--- context
The transition falls within the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and [[era/quaternary-period|Quaternary Period]]. It changed the rhythm that began with the [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]] under the pacing described in [[event/milankovitch-cycles-and-the-ice-ages|Milankovitch Cycles and the Ice Ages]], and it set up the later cycles, including the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. It overlaps the range of [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]]; no causal link to hominin evolution is established.
--- observer
The Observer files the transition under preconditions: the ice-age world every later people lived in changed its tempo partway through the Quaternary, from a 41,000-year beat to a 100,000-year one. Longer glacial stages mean longer stretches of lower sea level and shrunken habitable ranges, though the Observer does not claim to know how hominins responded. The reading would weaken if the change proved to be an artifact of how sediment records are dated and aligned.
--- question 1
What triggered the change when orbital forcing did not change?
--- question 2
Did longer, harsher glacial stages alter hominin populations in Africa and Eurasia?

=== event/milankovitch-cycles-and-the-ice-ages
title: Milankovitch Cycles and the Ice Ages
type: event
date: c. 2.58 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c9c7a9d0de50
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Milankovitch cycles are slow, regular changes in the shape of Earth's orbit, the tilt of its axis, and the wobble of that axis. They shift how sunlight is spread across latitudes and seasons, and since about 2.6 million years ago they have paced the growth and melting of the Quaternary ice sheets.
--- fact 1
The three cycles are eccentricity (orbit shape; about 100,000 and 400,000 years), obliquity (axial tilt, between about 22.1 and 24.5 degrees; 41,000 years), and precession (repeating about every 19,000 to 23,000 years).
--- fact 2
Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković calculated between the 1920s and 1940s how these changes alter sunlight by latitude. In 1976 Hays, Imbrie, and Shackleton found the same three periods in oxygen-isotope records from deep-sea sediment cores, which track global ice volume.
--- fact 3
The orbital changes alter the distribution of sunlight far more than its yearly total, so ice sheets, ocean circulation, and atmospheric carbon dioxide amplify the effect.
--- fact 4
Glacial cycles followed the 41,000-year tilt cycle until about 1.25 million years ago, then lengthened to about 100,000 years; see [[event/middle-pleistocene-transition|Middle Pleistocene Transition]]. Why the 100,000-year cycle dominates is unresolved.
--- fact 5
The cycles are far older than the ice ages; what changed about 2.7 million years ago was the climate's response (see [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]]).
--- context
Milankovitch cycles pace the glacial stages of the [[era/quaternary-period|Quaternary Period]] and [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]], including the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]], and the current interglacial, the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]]. The [[event/younger-dryas|Younger Dryas]] interrupted the last warming. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' whole written record sits inside one warm phase of a cycle set by the geometry of Earth's orbit, a cycle uncovered by pattern-taming from Milanković's sums to the 1976 sediment cores. The Observer treats it as a precondition, not a cause of later history. The reading would weaken if glacial terminations proved to depend more on internal ice-sheet behavior than on orbital pacing.
--- question 1
Why did the 100,000-year cycle come to dominate?
--- question 2
How much do ice sheets, oceans, and carbon dioxide each amplify the response?

=== event/miocene-great-ape-radiation
title: Miocene Great Ape Radiation
type: event
date: 23 – 7 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: cdfd4f7be228
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Miocene great ape radiation was the diversification of apes in Africa, Europe and Asia between about 23 and 7 million years ago. Many more ape forms lived then than survive today.
--- fact 1
Early Miocene apes of Africa include Proconsul from Kenya, about 20 million years old. Apes then spread into Europe and Asia, about 17 to 16 million years ago, when a land connection opened.
--- fact 2
Later forms include Dryopithecus and Danuvius from Europe, Ouranopithecus from Greece, and Sivapithecus from South Asia, which is related to orangutans. Only about twenty ape species live now.
--- fact 3
Molecular studies place the split of the gibbon lineage roughly 17 to 20 million years ago and the orangutan lineage roughly 13 to 16 million years ago, and that of the gorilla lineage later still.
--- fact 4
Apes declined in Europe and Asia as the climate cooled and dried, while the ancestors of the African apes and of the hominins survived in Africa.
--- fact 5
Whether the last common ancestor of the African apes and humans lived in Africa or in Europe is debated.
--- context
The Miocene Great Ape Radiation belongs to the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] and leads to the [[event/divergence-of-the-hominin-lineage|Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]], with early candidates such as [[species/sahelanthropus-tchadensis|Sahelanthropus tchadensis]]. It runs alongside the [[event/spread-of-grasslands|Spread of Grasslands]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species belongs to a group once far more diverse than it is now, and that its living relatives are a small remnant. Reading the history of apes from the survivors alone would give a false picture. The reading would weaken if Miocene apes turned out to be mostly close variants of a few forms.
--- question 1
Which Miocene ape, if any, lies closest to the ancestor of the African apes and humans?
--- question 2
Did that ancestor live in Africa or in Europe?

date: c. 1850

src: ecf1d74da1da
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Modern climate change is the warming of Earth caused mainly by human emissions of greenhouse gases, mostly from burning fossil fuels.
--- fact 1
Global surface temperature was about 1.1 degrees Celsius above 1850 to 1900 levels in 2011 to 2020 (IPCC), and has risen further since.
--- fact 2
Atmospheric carbon dioxide has risen from about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution to a global average of about 423 in 2024 (422.8 in NOAA's record).
--- fact 3
The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change states that human influence has unequivocally warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land.
--- fact 4
The Paris Agreement, reached on 12 December 2015, set goals of holding warming well below 2 degrees Celsius and pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5 degrees.
--- context
Climate change links the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] with the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]. See [[observer-note/the-species-as-a-geological-force|The Species as a Geological Force]] and compare [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a species has become a driver of planetary climate without any intention of doing so. The remaining question is whether it can coordinate at planetary scale to change course.
--- question 1
How much warming will occur, and how will societies adapt?

=== event/neanderthal-extinction
title: Neanderthal Extinction
type: event
date: c. 41 – 39 ka
themes: kinship, earth-systems
src: 8f9cebe1a055
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Neanderthals disappeared from the archaeological record of Europe about 41,000 to 39,000 years ago, according to radiocarbon dating of 40 sites. They vanished after several thousand years of overlap with Homo sapiens, and no single cause is agreed.
--- fact 1
A 2014 study using improved radiocarbon dating at 40 sites from Spain to Russia found that the Mousterian, the Neanderthal stone-tool industry, ended by about 41,030 to 39,260 calibrated years before present. It estimated an overlap with Homo sapiens of about 2,600 to 5,400 years, a mosaic and not a sudden replacement.
--- fact 2
Claims of later survival, such as Gorham's Cave in Gibraltar at about 28,000 years ago, rest on indirect dating and are widely regarded as unproven.
--- fact 3
Proposed factors include unstable climate, with a cold, dry spell called Heinrich Stadial 4 around 40,000 years ago; competition with Homo sapiens; disease; and small, inbred populations.
--- fact 4
Neanderthal DNA survives in present-day people outside Africa at about 1 to 2 percent, so some Neanderthal ancestry was absorbed; see [[event/interbreeding-of-homo-sapiens-with-neanderthals-and-denisovans|Interbreeding of Homo sapiens with Neanderthals and Denisovans]].
--- fact 5
A very large eruption in Italy about 40,000 years ago, the Campanian Ignimbrite Eruption, has been suggested as a factor. Its effect on Neanderthals is disputed.
--- context
The extinction ends the [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] lineage near the boundary between the [[era/middle-paleolithic|Middle Paleolithic]] and the [[era/upper-paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]]. It overlaps the [[event/peopling-of-europe-by-homo-sapiens|Peopling of Europe by Homo sapiens]]. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer treats this as a case in which the species has a stake in the answer, and is wary of the tidy version in which one group beat another. The evidence supports thousands of years of contact, some interbreeding, small Neanderthal populations, and hard climate. The reading would weaken if a well-dated site showed direct conflict or a sudden collapse.
--- question 1
Did any Neanderthal populations survive after about 39,000 years ago?
--- question 2
How much did climate, competition, disease, and small population size each contribute?

=== event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation
title: Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation
type: event
date: c. 2.7 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 511d0be9d6af
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The onset of Northern Hemisphere glaciation was the growth of large ice sheets across North America and Eurasia about 2.7 million years ago. It began the repeating glacial and interglacial cycles of the Quaternary Period.
--- fact 1
Ice-rafted debris in North Atlantic seafloor sediments becomes widespread about 2.7 million years ago, and loess deposits begin to build up on the Chinese Loess Plateau about 2.6 million years ago.
--- fact 2
Smaller ice sheets existed earlier in the Northern Hemisphere; the change was a large expansion, not a first appearance of ice.
--- fact 3
Proposed causes include a slow decline in atmospheric carbon dioxide through the Pliocene, changes in ocean circulation, and mountain uplift that altered atmospheric flow. The closure of the Isthmus of Panama is a candidate cause, but its timing is debated; see [[event/closure-of-the-isthmus-of-panama|Closure of the Isthmus of Panama]].
--- fact 4
The first cycles followed variations in the tilt of Earth's axis, with a period of about 41,000 years.
--- context
The Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation comes near the end of the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] and leads into the [[era/quaternary-period|Quaternary Period]], and it builds on the earlier cooling of the [[event/eocene-oligocene-transition|Eocene-Oligocene Transition]]. The event belongs to the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the world every human society has known is one of repeating ice ages, which began only about 2.7 million years ago after a slow decline in carbon dioxide and perhaps a rearrangement of ocean gateways. The Observer takes this as context, not cause: what it did to the making of humans is a separate and unsettled question. The reading would weaken if the carbon dioxide decline proved too small to explain the change.
--- question 1
What combination of carbon dioxide decline and ocean change tipped the climate into glacial cycles?
--- question 2
How did the new climate rhythm affect early hominins?

=== event/onset-of-plate-tectonics
title: Onset of Plate Tectonics
type: event
date: 3 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: d628aa12ae10
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The onset of plate tectonics is the start of the motion of rigid plates of Earth's outer shell, with ocean floor recycled into the mantle at subduction zones. Proposed dates range from before 4 billion to about 0.8 billion years ago, and the question remains one of the most disputed in Earth science.
--- fact 1
Plate tectonics moves about a dozen large plates and recycles ocean floor at subduction zones. Earth is the only planet known to have it.
--- fact 2
Proposed onsets include before 4.0 billion years ago, about 3.2 billion years ago, about 2.5 billion years ago and about 0.8 billion years ago. Some researchers argue for a gradual transition from a rigid outer lid, or for episodic subduction long before modern-style plate motion.
--- fact 3
Blueschists, which form in cold subduction zones, and ultra-high-pressure metamorphic rocks appear in the record only after about 0.8 billion years ago, which some geologists read as a marker of modern-style subduction.
--- fact 4
Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912, and plate tectonics gained wide acceptance in the 1960s.
--- fact 5
Plate tectonics builds continents and recycles carbon; whether it was needed for a long-lived biosphere is debated.
--- context
The Onset of Plate Tectonics is tied to the growth of continents in the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and to the [[event/earliest-continental-crust|Earliest Continental Crust]]. Its later effects include the [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]] and the [[event/collision-of-india-and-asia|Collision of India and Asia]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Earth's surface has recycled its own earliest evidence, so when the process began may be settled only indirectly. Some geologists treat plate tectonics as a precondition for a long-lived biosphere, and the Observer keeps that as an open hypothesis. It reads the current disagreement as information about the limits of the record, and it would weaken if a reliable early rock record were found.
--- question 1
When did plate tectonics begin, and was the change gradual or sudden?
--- question 2
Was plate tectonics required for a long-lived biosphere?

=== event/origin-of-bipedalism
title: Origin of Bipedalism
type: event
date: 7 – 3.7 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c7657be34286
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Habitual upright walking arose in the human lineage in Africa between roughly 7 and 4 million years ago. It is the earliest hallmark of the hominins, millions of years older than large brains or stone tools, but its starting point and cause are disputed.
--- fact 1
Sahelanthropus tchadensis, from Chad and about 7 million years old, has a skull base that suggests upright posture. A 2022 study of a femur argued for habitual bipedalism, but others dispute this, and its status as a hominin is debated.
--- fact 2
Orrorin tugenensis, from Kenya and about 6 million years old, is known from thigh bones read as evidence of bipedality; the reading is controversial.
--- fact 3
Ardipithecus ramidus, from Ethiopia and about 4.4 million years old, had a pelvis combining climbing and walking features and a grasping big toe. How well it walked is debated, and its excavators read its site as woodland, not open savanna.
--- fact 4
Footprints at Laetoli, Tanzania, about 3.66 million years old and attributed to [[species/australopithecus-afarensis|Australopithecus afarensis]], are the earliest direct record of upright walking.
--- fact 5
Proposed reasons include energy efficiency, carrying food or infants, heat regulation, and feeding from thin branches. None is agreed.
--- context
The origin of bipedalism follows the [[event/divergence-of-the-hominin-lineage|Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]] and falls within the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]]. It precedes the [[event/emergence-of-the-genus-homo|Emergence of the Genus Homo]] and the [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]] by millions of years. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' ancestors walked upright for millions of years before they had large brains or made recognizable tools, so the posture was not an adaptation for those things, and nothing in the record shows it heading toward them. The Observer reads it as a precondition: hands freed from walking could later carry and make. The reading would weaken if the earliest claimed bipeds proved to be apes with independently evolved postures.
--- question 1
Was Sahelanthropus a habitual biped, and was it a hominin?
--- question 2
Did upright walking begin on the ground or in the trees?

=== event/origin-of-birds
title: Origin of Birds
type: event
date: 160 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 2c5b97910207
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Birds evolved from small feathered theropod dinosaurs in the Jurassic, with the earliest bird-like fossils about 160 to 150 million years old. Birds are the only dinosaur lineage that survived to the present.
--- fact 1
Birds share many features with theropod dinosaurs, including hollow bones, a wishbone and a three-fingered hand. Feathered non-avian dinosaurs found in China from the 1990s onward strengthened the link.
--- fact 2
Feathers arose before flight, probably for insulation or display. The theropod Anchiornis, about 160 million years old, and [[species/archaeopteryx|Archaeopteryx]], about 150 million years old, both had feathered wings.
--- fact 3
Early Cretaceous fossils from China, about 125 million years old, show many forms of early birds, including toothless-beaked Confuciusornis.
--- fact 4
How flight began, from the ground up or from the trees down, is debated, and some small feathered dinosaurs had four wings.
--- fact 5
Only the lineage of modern birds survived the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], and living birds diversified after it.
--- context
The Origin of Birds lies within the [[era/jurassic-period|Jurassic Period]] and the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]] and belongs to the story of the [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a category as familiar as bird sits inside a category as remote as dinosaur, a reminder that names for kinds of animals are cuts across a continuous line of descent. The reading concerns naming only, and it would not be affected by the debate over where flight began.
--- question 1

=== event/origin-of-eukaryotes
title: Origin of Eukaryotes
type: event
date: 2 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: dfb56e57b875
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Eukaryotes, the cells with nuclei and internal organelles from which all animals, plants, and fungi are built, arose roughly 2 billion years ago, with very wide uncertainty about the date.
--- fact 1
Mitochondria descend from bacteria taken into a host cell, a process called endosymbiosis, which is widely accepted.
--- fact 2
Chloroplasts likewise descend from cyanobacteria that became resident in some eukaryotic cells.
--- fact 3
Genomic studies place the host cell within the Archaea, with the Asgard archaea often named as the closest relatives.
--- fact 4
The oldest unambiguous eukaryote fossils, large organic-walled microfossils, are more than 1.65 billion years old; older candidates such as Grypania (about 1.87 billion years) are less convincing than younger, larger forms.
--- fact 5
Molecular-clock estimates for the last common ancestor of living eukaryotes vary widely.
--- context
The origin of eukaryotes followed the oxygen rise of the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] and made large multicellular bodies possible, leading eventually to the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]]. Origin of Eukaryotes sits in the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that complex life arose through a merger rather than a single lineage inventing new parts alone. Cooperation between unlike partners sits at the base of every body, including the human one. A different origin would be indicated if strong evidence showed the organelles were built internally without symbiosis.
--- question 1
Did the host cell acquire the mitochondrion before or after gaining complexity?
--- question 2
How old is the last eukaryotic common ancestor?

title: Origin of Homo sapiens
type: event
date: c. 300 ka
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 584204583308
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anatomically modern humans, Homo sapiens, emerged in Africa by about 300,000 years ago through a gradual, continent-wide process.
--- fact 1
The Jebel Irhoud fossils in Morocco are about 315,000 years old (315 plus or minus 34 thousand years) and show a modern face and teeth with a more archaic braincase.
--- fact 2
Fossils from Omo Kibish (Ethiopia) are at least 233,000 years old, dated against a volcanic ash layer.

Some scholars describe the process as 'African multiregionalism', with several connected populations contributing.

--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' origin is not a single place or moment. It is a widespread, interbreeding population that became recognizably modern over tens of thousands of years.
--- question 1

=== event/origin-of-life
title: Origin of Life
type: event
date: 3.8 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c3902711786b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Life on Earth arose at least 3.5 billion years ago and possibly earlier. How it began is not yet known.
--- fact 1
Stromatolites (layered structures made by microbes) in the Dresser Formation of Western Australia's Pilbara region are about 3.48 billion years old and are described as the oldest convincing evidence of life.
--- fact 2
Claims for older evidence are debated. In 2016 researchers reported stromatolites about 3.7 billion years old at Isua in Greenland, and a paper in the same journal soon disputed the observations. Later studies disagree on whether the structures are biological.
--- fact 3
A 2024 molecular-clock study estimated that the last universal common ancestor, the organism from which all living cells descend, lived about 4.2 billion years ago. That ancestor was not the first life, and the estimate carries large uncertainty.
--- fact 4
Leading hypotheses for how life began include hydrothermal vents, shallow pools, and an early RNA-based stage; none is settled.
--- context
The origin of life marks the transition from the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] to the biology-dominated [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]]. Its later effects include the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]].
--- observer
The Observer regards this as the least understood event in the record. It is also the one that made every later subject possible, including the Observer's own field.
--- question 1
Did life begin once or several times?
--- question 2
Where did it begin?
--- question 3
How reliable are molecular-clock ages for a common ancestor that predates the fossil record by a large margin?

=== event/origin-of-mammals
title: Origin of Mammals
type: event
date: 225 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 9b30f1564085
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The origin of mammals was the gradual evolution of mammals from mammal-line reptiles called synapsids, with the oldest candidates from the Late Triassic, roughly 225 to 205 million years ago. The date depends on how a mammal is defined.
--- fact 1
Mammals descend from synapsids, which dominated Permian land life, through the therapsids and then the cynodonts of the Triassic.
--- fact 2
The bones that formed the jaw joint of reptile-like ancestors became the middle-ear bones of mammals, a change traced through fossils of Late Triassic and Early Jurassic mammal relatives.
--- fact 3
The oldest fossils that are candidates for mammals or their close relatives, the mammaliaforms, are about 225 to 205 million years old. Which of them are true mammals depends on the definition used.
--- fact 4
Mesozoic mammals were mostly small, though a few, such as Repenomamus from China, about 125 million years old, reached the size of a badger.
--- fact 5
Hair, milk and warm-bloodedness arose along the lineage, but soft tissues are rarely preserved, so the order is uncertain.
--- context
The Origin of Mammals falls in the [[era/triassic-period|Triassic Period]] and the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]], alongside the rise of [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]], and its lineage grew out of the mammal-line reptiles of the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]]. Mammals expanded after the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that hearing, one of the species' most ordinary senses, depends on bones that once formed the jaw joint of a reptile-like ancestor. New structures are built from old parts, and the Observer treats that as a general rule of the record, not a special case. The reading would weaken if the ear bones proved to have arisen without any earlier function in the jaw.
--- question 1
When did the first true mammals appear, given how the definition moves the date?
--- question 2
In what order did hair, milk and warm-bloodedness arise?

=== event/origin-of-multicellularity
title: Origin of Multicellularity
type: event
date: 1.05 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 7c1d3292e12f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Multicellularity, in which cells stay attached, divide labour and reproduce as a unit, arose independently many times. The clearest early fossil evidence is about 1.05 billion years old, and earlier claims are disputed.
--- fact 1
Multicellularity evolved independently in bacteria, in several groups of algae, in fungi, in plants and in animals; estimates for eukaryotes alone run to more than twenty separate origins.
--- fact 2
Older claims include the Francevillian biota of Gabon, about 2.1 billion years old, and the coiled fossil Grypania, about 1.9 billion years old; their interpretation is disputed.
--- fact 3
The red alga Bangiomorpha, dated in different studies to between roughly 1.05 and 1.2 billion years ago, shows differentiated cells and sexual reproduction.
--- fact 4
Experiments have produced simple multicellular clusters from single-celled algae and yeast within weeks to months. The step to complex bodies with many cell types is much rarer.
--- fact 5
Animals evolved multicellularity separately from plants, and large soft-bodied animals appear in the Ediacaran; see [[species/ediacaran-biota|Ediacaran Biota]] and [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]].
--- context
The Origin of Multicellularity followed the [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]] and overlaps the [[event/the-boring-billion|The Boring Billion]] in the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]]. It belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a body is cooperation among cells that share ancestry, held together by mechanisms that suppress cheating. Whether that is a useful comparison for human cooperation is a question the Observer treats as open; the analogy is offered as a way to look, not as an explanation. The reading would weaken if it obscured more than it clarified about either case.
--- question 1
Which of the early fossil claims are truly multicellular?
--- question 2
What conditions favoured the origin of animal multicellularity?

=== event/origin-of-photosynthesis
title: Origin of Photosynthesis
type: event
date: 3.5 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 3c9969dc2faa
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Photosynthesis, the use of light to power the making of organic molecules, arose in bacteria some time in the Archean, possibly as early as 3.5 billion years ago. The date of the first oxygen-producing form is uncertain and disputed.
--- fact 1
The first photosynthesis probably did not release oxygen. Anoxygenic forms in some living bacteria use hydrogen, sulfide or dissolved iron in place of water.
--- fact 2
Oxygen-producing photosynthesis is known from [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]]. Estimates for its origin run from about 3.5 to about 2.4 billion years ago, and the earliest unambiguous cyanobacterial fossils are about 2.15 billion years old.
--- fact 3
Biomarker molecules once cited as evidence of cyanobacteria at 2.7 billion years were later shown to be younger contamination.
--- fact 4
Layered [[species/stromatolites|Stromatolites]] about 3.48 billion years old from the Dresser Formation of Western Australia may record microbial mats, possibly photosynthetic; some researchers dispute that they are biological.
--- fact 5
Early oxygen was consumed by dissolved iron and other chemicals before it built up in the air; see [[event/banded-iron-formations|Banded Iron Formations]] and [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]].
--- context
The Origin of Photosynthesis follows the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]] within the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and sets up the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] of the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a way of getting energy from light, invented by microbes, ended up setting the chemistry that everything larger would run on. What stands out is the very long delay between the invention and its planetary effects, and the date matters less to this reading than that delay. The reading would weaken if oxygen-producing photosynthesis were shown to have begun only shortly before the oxygen rise.
--- question 1
When did oxygen-producing photosynthesis first evolve?
--- question 2
What delayed the accumulation of oxygen after it began?

=== event/out-of-africa-dispersal
title: Out of Africa Dispersal
type: event
date: c. 70 – 50 ka
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: 24b98599a481
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Homo sapiens spread out of Africa and across the world. Genetic studies trace most present-day non-African ancestry to a single major dispersal roughly 70,000 to 50,000 years ago, though earlier and smaller dispersals also left traces and the exact timing is debated.
--- fact 1
Genetic studies published in 2016 concluded that most non-African ancestry comes from one major dispersal about 50,000 to 72,000 years ago. Genetic dating is imperfect, and one climate study favored a window of 80,000 to 100,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
Earlier, smaller dispersals occurred. Homo sapiens fossils at Skhul and Qafzeh in Israel are about 90,000 to 120,000 years old. A fossil upper jaw from Misliya Cave in Israel was dated in 2018 to 177,000 to 194,000 years ago, but a published comment disputes that dating. A separate genetic signal suggests an exodus about 120,000 years ago that left about 2 percent of Papuan genomes.
--- fact 3
Modern humans reached Australia and New Guinea at least about 50,000 years ago, and possibly about 65,000 years ago, a date that is debated; see [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]].
--- fact 4
Present-day non-African populations carry up to about 2 percent Neanderthal ancestry. Oceanian populations carry much more Denisovan ancestry, up to about 5 percent in some.
--- fact 5
Both coastal and inland routes have been proposed.
--- context
The dispersal ends the isolation of human populations; see [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] and [[species/denisovans|Denisovans]]. It sets up the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]].
--- observer
The Observer regards this as the most remarkable feat of the Paleolithic: a single species occupying deserts, tundra, rainforest, and islands, mostly by learning rather than by biological change.
--- question 1
How many dispersals were there, and by which routes? The fossil record shows repeated early departures, while the genetic record points to one main wave, and the two are not yet fully reconciled.

=== event/paleocene-eocene-thermal-maximum
title: Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum
type: event
date: 56 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 9ef23311112a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum was a burst of global warming about 56 million years ago, in which a large release of carbon raised global temperatures by roughly 5 to 6 degrees Celsius for about 200,000 years.
--- fact 1
Deep-sea sediments record a sharp negative excursion in carbon isotopes, showing a large input of carbon to the ocean and atmosphere.
--- fact 2
The event lasted on the order of 200,000 years including recovery, with the onset taking only a few thousand years.
--- fact 3
The ocean acidified and many deep-sea foraminifera species went extinct.
--- fact 4
Proposed carbon sources include volcanic activity in the North Atlantic and the release of methane from seafloor deposits; the mix is debated.
--- fact 5
Several mammal groups, including early primates, horses, and artiodactyls, appear or spread across the Northern Hemisphere near its start.
--- context
The event is the best-studied ancient analogue for [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]] and for the carbon release of the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]. It occurred about ten million years after the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]. Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum sits in the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Earth has run this experiment before, without observers, and kept a record in mud. The present differs in that a species is both causing the change and measuring it. Studies generally find present emissions to be much faster than those at the onset of the PETM.
--- question 1
How much of the carbon was volcanic and how much came from methane?

--- summary
The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 consisted of the treaties of Osnabruck and Munster, which ended the Thirty Years' War and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic.
--- fact 1
The peace was negotiated from 1644 in the Westphalian cities of Osnabruck and Munster; the Spanish-Dutch treaty was signed on 30 January 1648, and the treaty involving the Holy Roman emperor, the German princes, France, and Sweden on 24 October 1648.
--- fact 2
Spain recognized the independence of the Dutch Republic.
--- fact 3
The settlement extended the Peace of Augsburg's principle, that rulers determine the religion of their territory, to include Calvinists.
--- fact 4
It is often cited as the origin of the modern system of sovereign states, though many historians regard this as a later simplification.
--- fact 5
It reduced the authority of the Holy Roman Emperor over the German princes.
--- context
The settlement ended the [[event/thirty-years-war|Thirty Years' War]] and the religious conflicts of the [[event/protestant-reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. --- observer
The Observer notes that a treaty that mainly solved a set of local problems later became a founding myth of the international order. Stories about founding moments tend to be assembled afterward.
