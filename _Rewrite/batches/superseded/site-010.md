=== culture/kingdom-of-kongo
title: Kingdom of Kongo
type: culture
date: 1483 – 1665
themes: religion, trade, war
src: 2209ed006779
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Kingdom of Kongo was a centralized state in west-central Africa that adopted Catholic Christianity, dealt with Portugal as a diplomatic partner, and was drawn into the Atlantic slave trade. This note covers the documented period from Portuguese contact in 1483 to the defeat at Mbwila in 1665.
--- fact 1
Portuguese explorers reached the mouth of the Congo River in 1483. King Nzinga Nkuwu adopted Roman Catholicism, taking the name João I, and the capital was renamed São Salvador do Congo (Mbanza Kongo).
--- fact 2
Afonso I (Nzinga Mbemba) ruled from 1506 to 1543. He won a brief civil war against his brother, centralized power at Mbanza Kongo, curbed the power of the nobility, and expanded the kingdom's borders through war.
--- fact 3
Afonso I was raised a Christian, sent his son and others to Europe to be ordained as priests, and worked to convert his subjects.
--- fact 4
From 1526 Afonso wrote a series of twenty-four letters to King João III of Portugal, appealing for an end to the slave trade.
--- fact 5
At the Battle of Mbwila on 29 October 1665, Portuguese forces defeated the Kongo army and killed King António I. Kongo continued to exist, but from then on it no longer functioned as a unified kingdom.
--- context
Kongo was among the first African states to enter sustained diplomatic contact with Portugal, and its history is bound up with the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. It lay in a region long settled by Bantu-speaking peoples; see [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a king who took up his visitors' meaning-engine and mark-code and used them to argue against the trade in his own people, while the exchange-web that consumed his subjects kept running. Adopting a partner's story-glue did not give the adopter control over the partner's demand.
--- question 1
How far did Kongo's own rulers and elites take part in the slave trade, and how far did they resist it?
--- question 2
How much of Kongo's Christianity was imported, and how much was reshaped by Kongo's own traditions?

observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Kingdom of Kush was a Nubian state on the Nile south of Egypt whose rulers conquered Egypt and governed it as the 25th Dynasty (about 744 to 656 BCE).
--- fact 1
Kushite kings such as Piye and Taharqa ruled Egypt and Nubia together.
--- fact 2
Later the capital moved to Meroe, a center of iron working.
--- fact 3
Nubia has more pyramids than Egypt, though they are smaller.
--- fact 4
The Meroitic script has been read phonetically but the language is not well understood.
--- context
Kush interacted with the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] for over a millennium in the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]]. See [[technology/iron-smelting|Iron Smelting]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the well-known Egyptian story is one of two Nile states in long contact. Kush at times ruled Egypt and adopted its symbols while keeping its own.

=== culture/kingdom-of-mapungubwe
title: Kingdom of Mapungubwe
type: culture
date: c. 1050 – 1270
themes: trade, art
src: 0944cfe08ed3
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mapungubwe was an Iron Age trading state at the meeting of the Limpopo and Shashe rivers in southern Africa, active from about the 11th to the 13th century, whose elite graves held gold and imported glass beads.
--- fact 1
The site lies at the confluence of the Limpopo and Shashe rivers. People settled at K2, near Bambandyanalo Hill, in the 11th century and had expanded to nearby Mapungubwe Hill by about 1220. From about 1220 to 1300 it was an advanced trading centre.
--- fact 2
The Limpopo connected the region to the Indian Ocean ports, including Kilwa. Its people traded with Arabia, China, and India through East African harbours.
--- fact 3
Glass beads, almost certainly from India, and fragments of Chinese celadon vessels show trade with states on the coast.
--- fact 4
Some graves held bodies in the upright seated position associated with royalty, together with gold and copper objects and exotic glass beads.
--- fact 5
The site was discovered in 1932 but was not widely publicized until much later. UNESCO declared it a World Heritage site in July 2003.
--- context
Mapungubwe preceded [[place/great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]] as a large regional centre and was linked into the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] through the [[culture/swahili-coast-city-states|Swahili Coast City-States]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a hill in the interior of a continent, far from any coast, held beads made in India and dishes made in China. Status-signal travelled a long way to arrive on a chief's grave, and the trade that carried it left a clearer record than the state that received it.
--- question 1
Why did Mapungubwe decline about the end of the 13th century, and did climate change play a part?
--- question 2
How did Mapungubwe's rulers relate to the later centres at Great Zimbabwe?

=== culture/kingdom-of-saba

type: culture
date: c. 1000 BCE – 275 CE
themes: trade, language
src: 23210eae39b6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Saba (Sheba) was an ancient South Arabian kingdom in what is now Yemen, centered on the city of Marib, and prosperous from the incense trade.
--- fact 1
Its earliest inscriptions date to about the 8th century BCE; some scholars propose an earlier origin, around 1000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Marib was known for a great dam that irrigated the surrounding land, repaired several times before its final failure around the 6th century CE.
--- fact 3
Sabaeans wrote in the South Arabian script.
--- fact 4
The Queen of Sheba appears in the Hebrew Bible and the Quran; her historicity is not established.
--- fact 5
The neighboring kingdom of Himyar absorbed Saba around 275 CE.
--- context
Saba's wealth came from the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]], which connected southern Arabia with the Mediterranean. It sits in the [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]] alongside the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]] of the north. Kingdom of Saba sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the best-known image of Saba, a queen visiting a king, comes from religious texts written elsewhere, while its own inscriptions record dams, temples, and commerce. The outside story shapes memory more than the inside record.
--- question 1
When did the kingdom form, and how far did it extend?

It grew from the Yuezhi confederation and was consolidated by Kujula Kadphises around 30 CE.

--- fact 5
The empire connected Rome, Parthia, India, and Han China commercially.
--- context
The Kushans occupied the crossroads of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]] and carried the traditions of [[person/siddhartha-gautama|Siddhartha Gautama]] eastward. They ruled where the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and Indian states met. Kushan Empire sits in the [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].

=== culture/lapita-culture
title: Lapita Culture
type: culture
date: c. 1600 – 500 BCE
themes: trade, art
src: b30e77a00bfd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Lapita culture was a seafaring society that appeared in the Bismarck Archipelago around 1600 BCE and spread rapidly east through the western Pacific, and its people were ancestors of the Polynesians.
--- fact 1
Lapita is identified by pottery decorated with fine stamped geometric patterns.
--- fact 2
Sites spread from the Bismarck Archipelago to Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa by roughly 1000 to 800 BCE.
--- fact 3
Lapita people moved obsidian and other goods over long distances.
--- fact 4
Ancient DNA from the Teouma cemetery in Vanuatu shows the first settlers there had ancestry related to East Asians; later, Papuan ancestry became dominant in Vanuatu.
--- fact 5
Lapita descendants settled Polynesia in later centuries.
--- context
The Lapita were the Pacific arm of the [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] and the direct ancestors of the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]]. Their voyages set the stage for the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. Lapita Culture sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the culture is defined almost entirely by pots, yet the pots record boats: a sailing tradition that crossed hundreds of kilometers of open ocean. The material that survives tells the story of what did not.
--- question 1
What drove the rapid expansion into previously uninhabited islands?

=== culture/mal-ta-and-the-ancient-north-eurasians

type: culture
date: c. 22 ka
themes: kinship, earth-systems
src: e757577e01fe
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Mal'ta boy, a child who lived near Lake Baikal about 24,000 years ago, has a genome that revealed a previously unknown population of 'Ancient North Eurasians' who contributed to the ancestry of both Europeans and Native Americans.
--- fact 1
The remains were excavated at Mal'ta in Siberia in the 1920s and their genome was sequenced in 2013.
--- fact 2
It was then the oldest modern human genome published.
--- fact 3
Between about 14 and 38 percent of the boy's genome is shared with present-day Native Americans, in different estimates.
--- fact 4
The result showed that Native American ancestry has two roots: East Asian and Ancient North Eurasian.
--- context
The finding reshaped the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]], which occurred around the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. See [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a single child's bones, buried with ornaments, spoke across 24,000 years to a question about the far side of the world. The past holds its answers in unexpected places.
--- question 1
How did Ancient North Eurasian ancestry spread across continents?

=== culture/malacca-sultanate
title: Malacca Sultanate
type: culture
date: 1400 – 1511
themes: trade, religion
src: de2c00526293
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Malacca (Melaka) Sultanate was a Malay port state on the Strait of Malacca, founded about 1400, that became the leading trading port of Southeast Asia in the 15th century and helped spread Islam through the archipelago before the Portuguese took it in 1511.
--- fact 1
Parameswara, a half-Javanese nobleman from Palembang in Sumatra, established the kingdom of Melaka about 1400 after trying to establish himself at Temasik (Singapore).
--- fact 2
According to tradition, Parameswara married the daughter of a Muslim ruler and converted to Islam, taking the name Iskandar and the title of sultan. Melaka became instrumental in the spread of Islam through the Malay world.
--- fact 3
It grew into the most important port in Southeast Asia in the 15th and early 16th century, an entrepôt through which flowed silks, spices, and porcelain, as well as local tin.
--- fact 4
The Portuguese, unable to negotiate a friendly trading arrangement, invaded the city in 1511 and forced the sultan to flee. The fall of Melaka marked the beginning of European colonial power in the region.
--- context
Malacca inherited the role of the older maritime empire of [[culture/srivijaya|Srivijaya]] and competed with the [[culture/majapahit|Majapahit]] state of Java. It sat at the centre of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]], and its capture followed the arrival of the [[event/portuguese-sea-route-to-india|Portuguese Sea Route to India]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a port that was valuable because it sat on a strait, and a ruling house that took on a new meaning-engine in a way that also suited its trade. The Portuguese seizure in 1511 shows an exchange-web being captured at its narrowest point.
--- question 1
How much of the conversion story rests on later Malay tradition, and how much on contemporary records?
--- question 2
How large was Melaka's population, and how did it manage so many merchant communities?

=== culture/minoans
title: Minoans
type: culture
date: c. 2000 – 1450 BCE
themes: trade, art
src: f7a15de42f72
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Minoans were a Bronze Age maritime civilization on the island of Crete, known for palaces such as Knossos and vivid frescoes. Their palatial culture flourished from about 2000 BCE until about 1450 BCE.
--- fact 1
They traded across the eastern Mediterranean, and their cultural influence reached Egypt, Cyprus, Canaan, and Anatolia.
--- fact 2
They used scripts, Linear A and Cretan hieroglyphs, that have not been deciphered. Linear B, adapted from Linear A, records Mycenaean Greek and was deciphered in 1952.
--- fact 3
The eruption of the Thera volcano damaged settlements in the Aegean. Radiocarbon dates put it around 1600 BCE, while links with Egyptian chronology suggest about 1500 BCE. The century-long gap is unresolved.
--- fact 4
Around 1450 BCE, Mycenaean Greeks became dominant on Crete and Linear B was used at Knossos. Scholars debate whether this was a conquest or a gradual absorption. Knossos remained a palace centre after the other Minoan palaces declined, until its own destruction some time later.
--- context
The Minoans are part of the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] trading world that collapsed in the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a culture known mainly through images of bull-leaping and sea life, since its writing cannot yet be read. What survives shapes how a culture is remembered.
--- question 1
Can Linear A be deciphered?
--- question 2
When exactly did Thera erupt, and how much did it contribute to Minoan decline compared with Mycenaean expansion?
