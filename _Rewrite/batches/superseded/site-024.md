=== person/vincent-van-gogh
title: Vincent van Gogh
type: person
date: 1853 – 1890
themes: art
src: 36ee40d4a356
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Vincent van Gogh (30 March 1853 to 29 July 1890) was a Dutch painter who worked in France. He painted for only about ten years, produced around 2,100 works, and sold almost nothing, yet his vivid colour and expressive brushwork made him the most famous artist of modern times.
--- fact 1
Van Gogh was born in Zundert in the southern Netherlands, the son of a Protestant minister. At 16 he joined the art dealers Goupil & Cie and was dismissed in 1876. He then tried teaching and preaching among coal miners in the Borinage. In 1880 he decided to become an artist.
--- fact 2
His younger brother Theo, an art dealer, supported him for the rest of his life. His letters, mainly to Theo, are among the most moving documents of any artist's life.
--- fact 3
In 1886 he moved to Paris, where he met Pissarro, Toulouse-Lautrec, Émile Bernard and Gauguin and discovered Impressionist colour and Japanese prints. He painted copies of two Hiroshige prints.
--- fact 4
In 1888 he moved to Arles. Gauguin joined him in October, and the partnership collapsed in December after Van Gogh cut off part of his own ear. He entered the asylum at Saint-Rémy in May 1889, where he painted The Starry Night.
--- fact 5
In May 1890 he moved to Auvers-sur-Oise and painted about one canvas a day. He shot himself on 27 July 1890 and died two days later, aged 37.
--- fact 6
His work comprises around 2,100 pieces, including some 860 oil paintings. Key works include The Potato Eaters (1885), Sunflowers (1888) and Wheatfield with Crows (1890).
--- fact 7
Theo died six months after Vincent. Theo's widow, Johanna van Gogh-Bonger, promoted the work and published the letters in 1914. The Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam opened in 1973.
--- context
Van Gogh worked in the Netherlands, Belgium and France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and learned from Japanese prints by [[person/utagawa-hiroshige|Utagawa Hiroshige]] and [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]]. He revered [[person/rembrandt-van-rijn|Rembrandt van Rijn]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a person who sold almost nothing in life is now among the most famous of painters, and that the change ran through one channel: his brother's support and, after both men were dead, his sister-in-law's publication of the letters. The letters made the life legible, and the popular myth of the tortured genius, built later by a novel and a film, rests on them. That myth is a story-glue about artists that simplifies a man who planned his compositions carefully and made many drawings and repeated versions.
--- question 1
How far does the popular myth of his life shape how his paintings are read?

=== person/wangari-maathai
title: Wangari Maathai
type: person
date: 1940 – 2011
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: 51cd19543ac4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Wangari Maathai (1940 to 2011) was a Kenyan environmental and political activist who founded the Green Belt Movement, which organized women to plant trees, and in 2004 became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.
--- fact 1
Maathai was born on 1 April 1940 in Ihithe, near Nyeri, Kenya. She studied biology in the United States and at the University of Nairobi, where she received a doctorate in 1971; she is recorded as the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn one.
--- fact 2
In 1977 she founded the Green Belt Movement, which organized women to plant tree seedlings against deforestation, soil loss and fuelwood shortage.- Her campaigns against building projects on public land in Nairobi and against forest clearing brought clashes with the government of President Daniel arap Moi; she was arrested and at times injured.
--- fact 3
She was elected to parliament in 2002 and served as an assistant minister for the environment from 2003.
--- fact 4
She received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004. In the same year a Kenyan newspaper reported that she had said HIV was deliberately created by scientists; she denied saying it and stated that she did not hold that belief.
--- fact 5
She died in Nairobi on 25 September 2011.
--- context
Maathai worked in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] in the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], in the decades after Kenya's independence during [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]]; compare [[person/chico-mendes|Chico Mendes]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Maathai joined a practical task, planting a tree, to an argument about who may decide the fate of land, and that the practice put women's labor at the center of an environmental story. The HIV dispute shows one public figure holding admired and contested positions at once. The reading of her as chiefly a tree planter would weaken if the movement's work on land rights and democracy were given equal weight.
--- question 1
How did the Green Belt Movement's political work shape its environmental results?

=== person/wassily-kandinsky
title: Wassily Kandinsky
type: person
date: 1866 – 1944
themes: art, religion
src: 4b59d2a01e82
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Wassily Kandinsky (16 December 1866 to 13 December 1944) was a Russian painter and theorist, one of the first to create purely abstract works in Europe. He believed colours and forms, freed from depicting objects, could move the viewer as directly as music.
--- fact 1
Kandinsky was born in Moscow and grew up in Odessa. He studied law and economics and began an academic career. In 1896, having seen one of Monet's Haystacks and heard Wagner's Lohengrin, he gave up law and moved to Munich to study painting.
--- fact 2
In 1911 he and Franz Marc founded Der Blaue Reiter (The Blue Rider) and he published On the Spiritual in Art. About then he painted his first fully abstract works.
--- fact 3
The First World War forced him back to Russia, where he took part in Soviet cultural institutions after the 1917 Revolution. He returned to Germany in 1921 and taught at the Bauhaus from 1922 to 1933.
--- fact 4
When the Nazis closed the Bauhaus he moved to Neuilly-sur-Seine near Paris, becoming a French citizen in 1939, and died there.
--- fact 5
He sorted his works into Impressions, Improvisations and Compositions, and made only ten Compositions over 30 years. He probably experienced synaesthesia and assigned musical qualities to colours: yellow like a trumpet, blue like a deep cello.
--- fact 6
Compositions I to III were destroyed in the Second World War. The Guggenheim Museum holds more than 150 of his works.
--- fact 7
His work influenced the Abstract Expressionists. In 2017 Murnau with Church II sold for £33 million.
--- context
Kandinsky lived in Russia, Germany and France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], through [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and the [[event/russian-revolution|Russian Revolution]]. He is usually credited with abstraction alongside [[person/hilma-af-klint|Hilma af Klint]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a lawyer who left his profession after a painting and a piece of music, and who then built a theory that colour and form carry spiritual meaning without pictured objects, a meaning-engine of his own made of pigment. Where af Klint kept comparable work hidden, he published a book, and that difference in visibility helped decide who was later credited with inventing abstraction, a story-glue of art history that is being revised.
--- question 1
Did synaesthesia shape his colour theory, and how much?

=== person/wilfrid-laurier
title: Wilfrid Laurier
type: person
date: 1841 – 1919
themes: kinship, trade
src: 6111d32ed4a5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Wilfrid Laurier (1841 to 1919) was the first French-Canadian Prime Minister of Canada, serving from 1896 to 1911 during a period of rapid western settlement and economic growth.
--- fact 1
A lawyer from Quebec, he became Liberal Party leader in 1887 and prime minister in 1896, holding the office for over fifteen years, then the longest unbroken term in Canadian history.
--- fact 2
His government promoted immigration and settlement of the Canadian prairies, a policy associated with his minister Clifford Sifton, that brought large numbers of European immigrant farmers to Western Canada.
--- fact 3
He worked to balance relations between English- and French-speaking Canada, though his support for Canadian participation in Britain's Boer War strained relations with Quebec nationalists who opposed it.
--- fact 4
He lost the 1911 election over a proposed reciprocity (free trade) agreement with the United States, which opponents attacked as a threat to Canada's ties with Britain.
--- context
Wilfrid Laurier led Canada, in [[region/north-america|North America]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], a period of large-scale immigration and westward settlement. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Laurier's career illustrates a recurring tension in settler societies built from more than one founding population: his efforts to hold English and French Canada together were tested repeatedly by exactly the kind of imperial military and economic questions, war participation and trade policy, that most directly divided them.
--- question 1
How much of the immigration boom under his government reflected deliberate policy versus favorable timing in global migration patterns?

=== person/william-morris
title: William Morris
type: person
date: 1834 – 1896
themes: art
src: 4bc43f9957ad
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
William Morris (1834 to 1896) was an English designer, writer and socialist, the central figure of the Arts and Crafts movement. He rejected cheap mechanised ornament in favour of patterns drawn from nature and made by traditional craft methods.
--- fact 1
Morris was born in Walthamstow in 1834 into a family that had grown wealthy through a Cornish copper mine. At Oxford he met the painter Edward Burne-Jones and, through him, the circle around Dante Gabriel Rossetti.
--- fact 2
He built Red House (1859 to 1860) and, unable to find furnishings he considered well made, founded a decorative arts firm in 1861 with Burne-Jones, Rossetti, Philip Webb and others. It was reorganised in 1875 as Morris & Co.
--- fact 3
He founded the Socialist League in 1884 and wrote extensively on politics and utopian fiction. In 1891 he founded the Kelmscott Press; its illustrated Chaucer was completed months before his death in 1896.
--- fact 4
He designed from real plants, disciplining them into flattened repeating patterns. He revived hand block-printing and indigo-discharge dyeing and letterpress printing from hand-cut type over faster industrial methods.
--- fact 5
His principle was "have nothing in your house that you do not know to be useful or believe to be beautiful". His goods were luxuries that generally only the wealthy could afford.
--- fact 6
Morris & Co. wallpapers and textiles, including Strawberry Thief (1883) and Willow Bough (1887), remain in production. The Kelmscott Chaucer launched the private-press movement.
--- context
Morris worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], in reaction to the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], and his machine-averse approach contrasts with that of [[person/christopher-dresser|Christopher Dresser]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a socialist who made luxury goods that mostly the wealthy could buy, an irony the record itself notes, so that an ethic of honest labour ran through a status-signal. His refusal of the machine was itself a claim about tool-lineage: that the hand-made line was the honest one. The patterns still in production after more than a century show a design outliving the arguments made for it.
--- question 1
How far did the movement he founded shape later industrial design, as opposed to reacting against it?

=== person/yaa-asantewaa
title: Yaa Asantewaa
type: person
date: c. 1840 – 1921
themes: war, kinship, religion
src: 7de16c2f9b09
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Yaa Asantewaa (about 1840 to 1921) was the queen mother of Ejisu in the Asante kingdom, in present-day Ghana, who led an Asante uprising against British rule in 1900. She died in exile in the Seychelles.
--- fact 1
Yaa Asantewaa was born about 1840 and was queen mother (ohemaa) of Ejisu, a title held by a woman who advises and helps choose the ruler.
--- fact 2
The British exiled the Asante king Prempeh I in 1896. In March 1900 the governor, Frederick Hodgson, demanded the Golden Stool, the Asante symbol of national unity, so that he could sit on it.
--- fact 3
According to tradition, she then urged the Asante chiefs at a council to resist and became a leader of the uprising sometimes called the War of the Golden Stool.
--- fact 4
Asante forces besieged the British fort at Kumasi for several months in 1900. British reinforcements relieved it, and the fighting ended that year with a British victory.
--- fact 5
She was captured and exiled to the Seychelles in 1901, and died there on 17 October 1921.
--- fact 6
The British annexed Asante in 1902 but did not obtain the Golden Stool itself.
--- context
Yaa Asantewaa's revolt was part of resistance to colonial conquest during the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]], in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the war centered on a shared object, a stool in which Asante tradition holds the nation's spirit, and that the governor's demand to sit on it was heard as a demand on the story-glue of the whole people. The queen mother's office within the kin-lattice gave her standing to call men to fight. A specialist could argue that later nationalist memory has shaped the accounts of her council speech, which would weaken any reading that leans on the words she is said to have spoken.
--- question 1
How do Asante oral histories and British records differ on her role in the war?

=== person/yermak-timofeyevich
title: Yermak Timofeyevich
type: person
date: c. 1532 – 1585
themes: war, trade
src: 56809db1f35f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Yermak Timofeyevich (about 1532 to 1585) was a Cossack leader whose expedition across the Urals in 1581 and 1582 overthrew the Khanate of Sibir and began the Russian conquest of Siberia. His early life is poorly known.
--- fact 1
Yermak's birth year is uncertain; about 1532 is often given. Most accounts say he was a Volga Cossack, and some say he was wanted for piracy.
--- fact 2
In 1581 he set out from the Ural lands of the Stroganov merchant family with several hundred men armed with firearms. Accounts of the year and the numbers vary.
--- fact 3
In 1582 his force defeated the army of Khan Kuchum of Sibir and took the khanate's capital, Qashliq (Isker), on 26 October.
--- fact 4
He sent word of the conquest to Tsar Ivan IV, who reportedly pardoned him and sent gifts.
--- fact 5
Yermak died in August 1585 (some sources say 1584) in an ambush by Kuchum's forces. Tradition says he drowned in the Irtysh River, weighed down by chain mail sent by the tsar.
--- fact 6
Russian forts followed, including Tyumen in 1586 and Tobolsk in 1587.
--- context
Yermak's campaign opened the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] in [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a few hundred armed men working for a merchant family, not directly for the state, opened a path across a continent, and that the state adopted the result afterward. In Russian tradition he is a national hero, while for the peoples he conquered the campaign began tribute-taking and the loss of independence; both accounts can be accurate. The reading of the venture as private rather than state-directed would weaken if court records show it was planned in Moscow.
--- question 1
How much did Moscow direct the expedition, and how much did it adopt afterward?

=== person/yongle-emperor
title: Yongle Emperor
type: person
date: 1360 – 1424
themes: trade, technology, war
src: 0faeec5d52d2
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Yongle Emperor (Zhu Di; 1360 to 1424) was the third emperor of China's Ming dynasty, whose reign moved the capital to Beijing and sponsored the ocean voyages of Zheng He.
--- fact 1
He seized the throne from his nephew, the Jianwen Emperor, in a civil war from 1399 to 1402, after his father, the dynasty's founder, had passed over him in the succession.
--- fact 2
He moved the imperial capital from Nanjing to Beijing and began construction of the Forbidden City, completed in 1420.
--- fact 3
He commissioned [[person/zheng-he|Zheng He]]'s seven treasure-fleet voyages, the first of which departed in 1405, reaching as far as the coasts of East Africa and Arabia and projecting Ming naval power across the Indian Ocean.
--- fact 4
He sponsored the Yongle Encyclopedia, an enormous compilation of Chinese knowledge finished in 1408 that ran to over 11,000 volumes, most of which was later lost.
--- context
The Yongle Emperor ruled the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and his patronage of Zheng He's fleets connected Ming China directly with trade networks across the Indian Ocean. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the treasure fleets Yongle sponsored were abruptly ended by his successors within decades of his death, their records largely destroyed, while the European voyages that followed half a century later, on a fraction of the scale, are remembered as the start of an age of exploration. The difference lies less in capability than in which government chose to keep looking outward.
--- question 1
Why did the Ming court end the treasure fleet voyages so decisively after Yongle's death, rather than continuing them at reduced scale?

=== person/zenobia
title: Zenobia
type: person
date: c. 240 – 274 CE
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 035bbc4d6b4f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Zenobia (Septimia Zenobia; about 240 to after 272) was queen of the caravan city of Palmyra in present-day Syria. She ruled for her young son and briefly extended Palmyrene control over Roman Egypt, Syria and much of Anatolia before the emperor Aurelian defeated her in 272.
--- fact 1
Zenobia was the wife of Odaenathus, the ruler of Palmyra who had fought the Sasanian Persians for Rome in the 260s. He was killed about 267, and she took power as regent for their son Vaballathus.
--- fact 2
About 270 her forces took Roman Egypt and pushed into Anatolia. By 271 she and her son used the titles Augusta and Augustus.
--- fact 3
Some coins struck in 271 and 272 show Aurelian on one side and Vaballathus on the other, so her claim was for a time framed inside the Roman order.
--- fact 4
Aurelian defeated her armies in 272 at Immae and Emesa and captured her. Palmyra was sacked in 273 after a revolt.
--- fact 5
Her fate is unknown. One tradition says she was shown in Aurelian's triumph in 274; its source, the *Historia Augusta*, is often unreliable.
--- context
Zenobia's revolt against the [[culture/romans|Romans]] took place in [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and Palmyra's wealth rested on caravan trade, part of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Zenobia governed a trading city between two large powers, and that her claim-line, from Egypt to central Anatolia, grew while Rome was weak and shrank within a few years once it recovered. Most personal detail about her comes from Roman writers who defeated her and later writers who embellished her. The reading of her as a rebel would weaken if inscriptions and coins show she sought a place within the Roman order, as some historians argue.
--- question 1
Did she seek independence from Rome or a share of its power?

=== technology/alphabet
title: Alphabet
type: technology
date: c. 1800 BCE
themes: language, technology
src: 7214995af8c1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The alphabet is a writing system in which signs represent individual sounds. The first alphabet, Proto-Sinaitic, appeared around 1800 BCE and gave rise to most alphabets in use today.
--- fact 1
It was adapted by Semitic-speaking workers from Egyptian hieroglyphs.
--- fact 2
The Phoenician alphabet (about 1050 BCE) had about 22 letters, all consonants.
--- fact 3
The Greeks added vowels in the 8th century BCE, and the Latin alphabet descends from a Greek form.
--- fact 4
Hebrew, Arabic, Cyrillic, and many Indian scripts also descend from or are related to early alphabets.
--- context
The alphabet follows the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] and spread through the [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a system of about two dozen signs made literacy learnable in months, not years. Simplicity made writing far more widely available.
--- question 1
Who invented the first alphabet?

=== technology/aqueduct
title: Aqueduct
type: technology
date: c. 690 BCE
themes: technology
src: 3e104ecb395b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
An aqueduct is a channel or bridge that carries water over a distance, often across a valley. One of the earliest known stone examples is the aqueduct at Jerwan in northern Iraq, built for the Assyrian king Sennacherib about 690 BCE to supply Nineveh.
--- fact 1
The Jerwan aqueduct dates to about 703 to 690 BCE and carried a canal across a valley on a stone bridge about 280 meters long, built of more than two million dressed limestone blocks.
--- fact 2
The Oriental Institute of Chicago excavated it in 1933 (Thorkild Jacobsen and Seton Lloyd), and the results appeared in 1935.
--- fact 3
It was one part of Sennacherib's canal system for Nineveh, which his royal inscriptions describe.
--- fact 4
The excavators found no comparable structure in the record before Roman times.
--- fact 5
The Aqua Appia of 312 BCE was the first of the aqueducts that eventually brought water to Rome.
--- context
The aqueduct extended the canal work described in [[technology/irrigation-canals|Irrigation Canals]] over obstacles, and it stands in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] record of [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] and the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]]. Later, the [[culture/romans|Romans]] built the best-known aqueducts. The [[technology/qanat|Qanat]] solved a similar problem underground, and [[technology/sanitation-and-sewers|Sanitation and Sewers]] carried used water away. Thread: [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the aqueduct as a tool-lineage step from ditch to bridge: it needed no new principle, only the will to carry water over an obstacle instead of around it. Such works demanded labor and stone in quantity, so they also served as status-signals of imperial power, and Sennacherib recorded his waterworks in royal inscriptions. The reading would weaken if many similar Assyrian works turned up, which would make Jerwan common rather than singular.
--- question 1
Did the Assyrians build other stone aqueducts that have not survived?
--- question 2
How were the Jerwan blocks quarried, laid, and made watertight?

=== technology/brewing-and-fermentation
title: Brewing and Fermentation
type: technology
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: technology, art
src: c116f1feb9f8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Brewing and fermentation use yeasts to turn sugars in grain, honey, and fruit into alcohol. Residues in pottery from Jiahu in China, dated to about 7000 to 6600 BCE, are the earliest confirmed alcoholic drink.
--- fact 1
Jars from Jiahu in Henan, analyzed by Patrick McGovern's team (2004), held residues of a mixed drink of rice, honey, and fruit (hawthorn or grape), dated to about 7000 to 6600 BCE.
--- fact 2
Jars from Gadachrili Gora and Shulaveris Gora in Georgia carry tartaric acid, the marker of grape wine, and date to about 6000 to 5800 BCE.
--- fact 3
A brewery at Abydos in Egypt, dated to about 3100 to 3000 BCE in the era of King Narmer, had eight units of about 40 basins each and could make an estimated 22,000 liters per batch.
--- fact 4
A hymn to Ninkasi, the Sumerian goddess of brewing, survives in copies from about 1800 BCE and describes how beer was made.
--- fact 5
A report of brewing at Raqefet Cave in Israel about 13,000 years ago is debated.
--- context
Brewing and fermentation depended on [[technology/pottery|Pottery]] for vessels and on the cereals and fruit of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]]. The earliest evidence lies in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]]; later records come from the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] and the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer reads early brewing as a branch of the tool-lineage that needed pottery first, and notes that the Abydos brewery probably supplied royal funerary rites, which ties drink to grief-rite and status-signal. An alternative reading treats fermentation as accidental food storage that later gained social uses, and residues alone cannot say which came first.
--- question 1
Did brewing begin before or after cereals were domesticated?
--- question 2
How should the debated Raqefet evidence be weighed against Jiahu?

=== technology/bronze-metallurgy
title: Bronze Metallurgy
type: technology
date: c. 3300 BCE
themes: technology, trade, war
src: 28df4d8b4443
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Bronze is an alloy of copper and tin (or another metal) that was first widely produced in the Near East around 3300 BCE, giving its name to the Bronze Age.
--- fact 1
Tin was scarce and had to be traded over long distances, from places such as Afghanistan and later Cornwall.
--- fact 2
Bronze was used for tools, weapons, and ritual objects.
--- fact 3
Earlier arsenical-copper alloys came first in some regions.
--- fact 4
The need for tin drove long-distance exchange-webs.
--- context
Bronze metallurgy defines the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and led to [[technology/iron-smelting|Iron Smelting]]. Its trade networks were disrupted in the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a technology needing two rare inputs from distant places forced states to keep trade open, and made them vulnerable when trade failed.

=== technology/calendars
title: Calendars
type: technology
date: c. 2700 BCE
themes: science, religion
src: 658f4455e5c0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Calendars are systems for counting days and grouping them into months and years, usually by following the Sun, the Moon, or both. Egypt's 365-day civil calendar, in use by about 2500 BCE at the latest, is among the earliest.
--- fact 1
The Egyptian civil calendar had twelve 30-day months in three seasons plus five extra days and no leap day, so it drifted against the solar year. Its introduction is dated from about 3000 BCE to the early Old Kingdom, and this note uses about 2700 BCE.
--- fact 2
Mesopotamian calendars were lunisolar, with an added month to keep pace with the seasons.
--- fact 3
The earliest accepted Long Count inscription is Stela 2 at Chiapa de Corzo, Mexico, read as 36 BCE, though part of its date is reconstructed.
--- fact 4
The Julian calendar (45 BCE) added a leap day every fourth year, and the Gregorian reform of 1582 dropped ten days and refined the rule.
--- fact 5
The Islamic Hijri calendar counts lunar years from 622 CE, when [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] moved to Medina.
--- context
Egyptian calendars in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] followed the Nile flood; the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]], [[culture/maya|Maya]], and [[culture/romans|Romans]] built other systems. Threads: [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]]. The [[technology/mechanical-clock|Mechanical Clock]] later divided the day, not the year.
--- observer
The Observer reads calendars as pattern-taming serving story-glue: one shared count of days lets farmers plan the year and lets a priesthood, state, or faith agree when to gather. Every calendar fits an irregular sky to whole days, so each is a compromise that shows who set it. An alternative reading treats calendars as mainly agricultural tools that gained ritual meaning later.
--- question 1
When was the Egyptian civil calendar first used?
--- question 2
Did the Long Count begin with the Maya or with their western neighbors?

=== technology/chariot
title: Chariot
type: technology
date: c. 2000 BCE
themes: war, technology
src: c733b38d5f19
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The chariot is a light two-wheeled vehicle drawn by horses, used in war and racing. The earliest known spoke-wheeled chariots come from Sintashta burials in the southern Urals, about 2000 BCE, though where the design began is debated.
--- fact 1
Sintashta graves contain horses and vehicles with spoked wheels, dated to about 2000 BCE. Radiocarbon dates for the wider Sintashta-Petrovka sites span about 2050 to 1750 BCE.
--- fact 2
Some scholars reject a steppe origin, arguing that the Sintashta vehicles were too unstable for war and favoring the Near East.
--- fact 3
Earlier Mesopotamian vehicles, such as those on the Standard of Ur (about 2600 BCE), were heavy four-wheeled carts, not light spoked chariots.
--- fact 4
By the mid-2nd millennium BCE chariots were used in the Near East, Egypt, and the Aegean. The [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]], around 1274 BCE, was one of the largest chariot battles on record.
--- fact 5
Chariot burials at Anyang from the late [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]], about 1200 BCE, are the earliest archaeological chariots in China.
--- fact 6
By the first millennium BCE, cavalry was replacing chariots in many armies.
--- context
The chariot of the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]] in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] built on [[technology/the-wheel|The Wheel]] and on the horse management seen at the [[culture/botai-culture|Botai Culture]]. It later served the [[culture/hittites|Hittites]] and others. Threads: [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer treats the chariot as a meeting of three older inheritances, the wheel, horse management, and the spoke, so it cannot be credited to one inventor. Chariots persisted as status-signals in ceremony and racing after their military value had faded. The steppe-first account would weaken if a Near Eastern origin is confirmed.
--- question 1
Were the Sintashta vehicles practical war chariots?
--- question 2
By which routes did the chariot reach China?

=== technology/code-of-hammurabi
title: Code of Hammurabi
type: technology
date: c. 1754 BCE
themes: language, kinship
src: cdc4f26cc413
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Code of Hammurabi is a black stone stele inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform with about 282 laws, made for the Babylonian king Hammurabi about 1754 BCE. It was found at Susa in 1901 to 1902 and is in the Louvre.
--- fact 1
The stele is about 2.25 meters tall. A relief at the top shows the king before a seated god, usually identified as the sun god Shamash, and 44 columns of cuneiform text follow.
--- fact 2
The count of 282 laws is a modern division of a text that has no numbered sections.
--- fact 3
The laws deal with trade, property, family, debt, and injury. Penalties vary with social class, and proportionate punishment applies within a class.
--- fact 4
The stele was carried to Susa, probably as spoil by the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte in the 12th century BCE. A French expedition led by Jacques de Morgan found it in fragments between December 1901 and January 1902, and a translation appeared within about a year.
--- fact 5
It is not the oldest known law collection: the Code of Ur-Nammu is older, from about 2100 BCE.
--- context
The Code of Hammurabi belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] record of [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] and to the reign of [[person/hammurabi|Hammurabi]], and it rests on the writing described in [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. Some of its laws concern the upkeep of dikes and canals, as in [[technology/irrigation-canals|Irrigation Canals]]. Threads: [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer treats the stele as story-glue set in stone: by publishing rules where subjects could see them, the king asked strangers in many cities to accept one shared standard of judgment. The class-based penalties show that the standard was not equal. An alternative reading holds that the text was royal self-presentation and scribal literature more than a code that judges applied, and surviving case records seldom cite it.
--- question 1
Did judges in Babylonian courts apply the laws as written?
--- question 2
How much did the code borrow from earlier Sumerian collections?

=== technology/coinage
title: Coinage
type: technology
date: c. 625 BCE
themes: trade
src: e142710ecee3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Coinage is the issue of stamped pieces of metal of standard weight and value. The earliest known coins are electrum pieces from Lydia in western Anatolia, found in a temple deposit at Ephesus dated to about 625 to 600 BCE, and China and India developed coin traditions of their own.
--- fact 1
The Artemision deposit at Ephesus held stamped coins of electrum, a natural gold and silver alloy. The deposit is dated to about 625 to 600 BCE, and some recent studies place the striking of the coins somewhat earlier, in the mid to late 7th century BCE.
--- fact 2
In the 6th century BCE the kings of Lydia issued coins of pure gold and pure silver, called croeseids after Croesus.
--- fact 3
A stamp reduced the need to weigh and test metal at each payment. Before coins, silver by weight, grain, and other goods served as money in Mesopotamia and Egypt.
--- fact 4
The [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], who conquered Lydia under [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], later issued their own gold and silver coins.
--- fact 5
Greek city-states adopted coinage in the 6th century BCE.
--- fact 6
China (cast bronze spade and knife money) and India (punch-marked silver) developed coin traditions in the first millennium BCE. How far they were independent of Lydia is debated.
--- context
Coinage began in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] in [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] and belongs to the thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]]. It reached the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]] and the Persians, and the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]] later standardized currency across China.
--- observer
The Observer treats coinage as story-glue made portable: a stamp asks strangers to accept that a small piece of metal is worth a stated amount, and the issuing ruler guarantees the claim. That trust rests on force and institutions behind the stamp as much as on the metal. An alternative reading holds that early coins served state payments, such as soldiers and tribute, more than everyday trade, and the record cannot easily separate the two uses.
--- question 1
Were the first coins made for state payments or for market trade?
--- question 2
How independent were the Chinese and Indian coin traditions?

=== technology/crossbow
title: Crossbow
type: technology
date: c. 500 BCE
themes: war, technology
src: 0b388f21a411
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The crossbow is a bow mounted on a stock, with a trigger that holds the string drawn until release. The earliest known bronze crossbow trigger mechanisms come from tombs of the state of Chu in China, dated to about the 5th century BCE, and Greek makers built a comparable weapon around 400 BCE.
--- fact 1
Bronze trigger mechanisms from Chu tombs in Hunan and Hubei date from about the 5th century BCE into the Warring States period.
--- fact 2
A repeating crossbow from a Chu tomb at Qinjiazui in Hubei is dated to the 4th century BCE.
--- fact 3
The trigger let a soldier hold the string at full draw while aiming, and a stock allowed a heavy bow to be spanned with the body or a foot. Crossbows could be used with less training than a hand-drawn bow.
--- fact 4
Crossbows spread widely in the armies of the Qin and [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]].
--- fact 5
Greek writers, notably Heron of Alexandria in the 1st century CE, describe the gastraphetes, or "belly bow," and associate its invention with Syracuse about 399 BCE. The account comes from later authors, and the date is traditional.
--- context
The crossbow of [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] grew from the bow and the bronze casting described in [[technology/bronze-metallurgy|Bronze Metallurgy]]. Armies of the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]] and the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] used it, and the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]] built their own version. It replaced the [[technology/chariot|Chariot]] as a mass weapon in some armies. Threads: [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer treats the crossbow as a tool-lineage step that traded skill for machinery: a mechanism stored the soldier's strength and let a lightly trained conscript match a lifelong archer. That shift favored states able to mass-produce bronze parts and to issue weapons in the thousands, and it lowered the skill needed for sanctioned harm at a distance. An alternative reading gives more weight to drill and command than to the weapon itself.
--- question 1
Were the Chinese and Greek crossbows independent inventions?
--- question 2
How much did the crossbow change the size of armies?

=== technology/crucible-steel
title: Crucible Steel
type: technology
date: c. 300 BCE
themes: technology, trade
src: 0fc159ce7ce7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Crucible steel is high-carbon steel made by melting iron with carbon-bearing material in a sealed clay crucible. The earliest confirmed production sites are in South India, such as Kodumanal in Tamil Nadu, dated to about the 3rd century BCE, and the steel made there is called wootz.
--- fact 1
Excavations at Kodumanal in Tamil Nadu found crucible fragments, slag, and iron objects dated to about the 3rd to 2nd century BCE.
--- fact 2
Wootz production is also documented in Sri Lanka, including at Samanalawewa, and crucible steel was made in Central Asia and Iran in later centuries.
--- fact 3
Wootz ingots were traded to the Near East, where blades made from them became known as Damascus steel. How far particular Damascus blades derive from Indian ingots, and where else similar steel was made, is debated.
--- fact 4
The banded surface of these blades comes from carbide patterns formed as the steel cooled and was forged.
--- fact 5
In the 1740s Benjamin Huntsman developed a separate crucible steel process in Sheffield, and the Bessemer process later replaced crucible methods for bulk steel.
--- context
Crucible steel grew from the iron working of [[technology/iron-smelting|Iron Smelting]] and the furnace skills of [[technology/bronze-metallurgy|Bronze Metallurgy]]. It belongs to [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] and traveled with goods along routes later described in the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer sees crucible steel as a tool-lineage branch that stayed regional for centuries: the recipe depended on particular ores, crucibles, and hands, so when those were disrupted the method could break. The Damascus name is a status-signal that may reflect a place of sale rather than a place of making. The reading would weaken if excavations showed that the craft was widespread and continuous instead of fragile.
--- question 1
Where else was crucible steel made independently?
--- question 2
Why did wootz production end in the 18th to 19th century?

=== technology/cyrus-cylinder
title: Cyrus Cylinder
type: technology
date: c. 539 BCE
themes: language, religion
src: eb54f48683b4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Cyrus Cylinder is a baked clay cylinder inscribed in cuneiform after Cyrus the Great's conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE. It was found at Babylon in 1879 and is in the British Museum.
--- fact 1
Hormuzd Rassam found the cylinder, about 23 centimeters long, during a British Museum excavation at Babylon in 1879. A fragment given to Yale in 1922 was later shown to belong to it.
--- fact 2
Its text presents Cyrus as chosen by the Babylonian god Marduk and describes a peaceful entry into the city.
--- fact 3
It records the return of divine images to their sanctuaries and of deported peoples to their homelands.
--- fact 4
It does not name Jerusalem or the Jewish exiles, although the Book of Ezra credits Cyrus with allowing exiles to return.
--- fact 5
The cylinder has been called the first charter of human rights. The British Museum and many historians of the ancient Near East reject this as anachronistic.
--- context
The Cyrus Cylinder is an [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] record from [[place/babylon|Babylon]] in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] that documents the rise of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] under [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], and it continues the cuneiform tradition described in [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. Threads: [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the cylinder as story-glue written for a local audience: a conqueror told Babylonians that their own god had chosen him, which made his rule easier to accept. A meaning-engine and a political claim are joined in one text, as in earlier Mesopotamian royal inscriptions. The later reading of the text as a modern rights charter is a new story attached to an old object, and every culture reuses objects this way. The reading would weaken if the wording proved to be aimed mainly at a Persian or wider audience.
--- question 1
How was the cylinder used in Babylon, and where was it placed?
--- question 2
Why has this text carried so many later meanings?

=== technology/digital-computer
title: Digital Computer
type: technology
date: c. 1945
themes: technology, science, language
src: 28d3093d800f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The digital computer is a programmable machine that processes information as binary numbers. The ENIAC was completed in 1945, after earlier machines such as Zuse's Z3 (1941) and Colossus (1943).
--- fact 1
The transistor (1947) and the integrated circuit (about 1958) allowed computers to shrink and become cheaper.
--- fact 2
The microprocessor (1971) put a computer on one chip.
--- fact 3
Computing power has roughly doubled every two years, a pattern known as Moore's law.
--- fact 4
Computers now run in phones, cars, appliances, and networks.
--- context
The computer implements ideas of [[person/alan-turing|Alan Turing]] and [[person/ada-lovelace|Ada Lovelace]] and drives the [[event/digital-revolution|Digital Revolution]].
--- observer
The Observer sees a general-purpose tool for manipulating symbols: it is the first machine that can imitate many other machines. It makes memory-outsourcing dynamic instead of static.

Who counts as the inventor of the computer?

--- summary
Electrical power grids distribute electricity from central generators to homes and factories. Thomas Edison opened the first commercial power station in New York in 1882.
--- fact 1
Alternating current, promoted by Westinghouse and Tesla, won because it could be transmitted over long distances.

Niagara Falls began delivering power to Buffalo in 1896.

Electricity powers lighting, motors, communication, and computers.
--- fact 4
Today, grids connect most of the world's population.
--- context
The grid followed the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]] and became the base for the [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a species' night became optional: light at any hour changed sleep, work, and city life. The grid is also one of the largest machines ever built.

=== technology/gunpowder
title: Gunpowder
type: technology
date: c. 850 CE
themes: technology, war, science
src: fd128e858f75
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Gunpowder, a mix of saltpeter, sulfur, and charcoal, was discovered in China around the 9th century and became a military technology under the Song.
--- fact 1
It was probably discovered by alchemists seeking elixirs.
--- fact 2
The Song dynasty used gunpowder in fire arrows, bombs, and early firearms.
--- fact 3
It spread west through the Mongol empire and reached Europe by the 13th century.
--- fact 4
Cannons and guns transformed warfare and state power.
--- context
Gunpowder emerged in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], was developed under the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]], and spread with the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a compound found by people looking for immortality became an instrument of sanctioned harm. Discoveries are not tied to their finders' intentions.
--- question 1
Who first discovered gunpowder?
