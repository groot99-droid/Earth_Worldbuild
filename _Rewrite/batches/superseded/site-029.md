=== theme/war-and-conflict
title: War and Conflict
type: theme
date: 
themes: war
src: 15fe2848bef9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
War and conflict are organized violence between groups: raids, battles and sieges, conquests, rebellions, civil wars, and genocides, together with the treaties and settlements that end them. The vault follows them from the uneven evidence for violence in prehistory, through the chariot armies and iron-armed empires of antiquity, the steppe conquests and gunpowder states of the medieval and early modern worlds, and the colonial and revolutionary wars of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, to the industrial world wars and the nuclear standoff of the twentieth. The scale and character of organized violence has changed with technology, political organization, and the ability to feed, move, and command large forces. This hub records atrocity plainly as atrocity and notes where casualty figures and causes are disputed.
--- observer
The Observer calls this sanctioned harm: violence that a group approves through its story-glue. It notes how the same species that holds strong inhibitions against killing individuals can, under the right story-glue, organize mass killing, and it records the harm plainly as harm. It notes with equal seriousness the counter-tradition of treaties, laws of war, and nonviolent movements, which appears as early as the treaty after the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]] and the edicts of [[person/ashoka|Ashoka]], and which states themselves often write. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]]. This reading would weaken if organized killing proves to be explained mainly by scarcity or fear, with little role for shared stories that make it permissible.
--- question 1
What conditions make large-scale organized violence more or less likely?
--- question 2
When did organized fighting between groups become common, and did settled life or the rise of states change its scale?
--- question 3
How did new technologies, such as the horse and chariot, iron, gunpowder, railways, and nuclear weapons, change who could wage war and how much of a society's effort it took?
--- question 4
Why do some wars end in durable settlements, such as the treaty after the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]] or the [[event/peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]], while others lead to renewed conflict?
--- question 5
How much of the death toll of war comes from famine and disease rather than combat, as in the [[event/thirty-years-war|Thirty Years' War]] and the [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]]?
--- question 6
How do societies remember wars, and who controls the record?
--- extra 1: Key Threads
- Evidence for organized violence is uneven in early prehistory and grows with settled societies. The wall and tower at [[place/jericho|Jericho]] (about 8000 BCE) may have served defense or protection from floods, and the early city of [[place/caral|Caral]] shows no evidence of large-scale warfare.
- Bronze Age states fielded chariots and fortified centers. [[technology/the-wheel|The Wheel]] and [[technology/bronze-metallurgy|Bronze Metallurgy]] supplied vehicles and weapons, the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]] built fortified settlements and the earliest known spoke-wheeled chariots, and at the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]] (about 1274 BCE) Egyptian and [[culture/hittites|Hittites]] armies fought one of the largest chariot battles on record. About fifteen years later they concluded a peace treaty, of which versions survive in both archives. Around 1200 BCE many of these states collapsed within a few decades ([[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]]; see also the [[culture/mycenaean-greeks|Mycenaean Greeks]]).
- Iron Age empires built large armies and administrations. [[technology/iron-smelting|Iron Smelting]] made it easier to arm large populations, the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] used mass deportation and controlled Mesopotamia, the Levant, and briefly Egypt, and the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] under [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]] took Babylon in 539 BCE and built [[place/persepolis|Persepolis]]. [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]] (336 to 323 BCE) ended the Achaemenid Empire.
- Rome and Carthage fought three Punic Wars between 264 and 146 BCE, and Rome destroyed [[place/carthage|Carthage]] in 146 BCE. [[person/augustus|Augustus]] ended a century of civil war and began the Pax Romana; the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] in 476 CE was a long process that historians debate as either collapse or transformation. See [[culture/romans|Romans]].
- In East and South Asia, the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]] in 221 BCE under [[person/qin-shi-huang|Qin Shi Huang]] created the first unified Chinese empire. The [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] faced the [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]] confederation, at times paying it in gifts and marriage alliances, and [[person/ashoka|Ashoka]] of the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]] conquered Kalinga around 261 BCE, an event his own edicts record as killing about 100,000 people and deporting 150,000, after which he supported Buddhism and promoted moral conduct.
- Steppe powers repeatedly confronted settled states. The [[culture/scythians|Scythians]] were horse-riding nomads of the Iron Age, the [[culture/gokturk-khaganate|Göktürk Khaganate]] ruled Inner Asia from about 552 CE, and from 1206 the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]] under [[person/genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]] built the largest contiguous land empire in history, including the sack of Baghdad in 1258; estimates of the deaths run to tens of millions and are uncertain. [[person/timur|Timur]] later campaigned from Persia to India, sacking Delhi in 1398 and defeating the Ottoman sultan at Ankara in 1402.
- Faith, empire, and firepower drove long wars. The [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] and the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]] extended Muslim rule from Iberia to Sindh, the [[event/battle-of-talas|Battle of Talas]] in 751 ended Tang expansion in Central Asia, the [[event/crusades|Crusades]] began in 1095, and [[technology/gunpowder|Gunpowder]], developed in China and used by the Song in fire arrows, bombs, and early firearms, reached Europe by the 13th century. The Ottomans used very large cannon in the [[event/fall-of-constantinople|Fall of Constantinople]] in 1453.
- Conquest and resistance shaped the early modern world. The [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]] (1519 to 1521) relied on Indigenous allies and on smallpox, and the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-inca-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]] began in 1532 after an Inca civil war. The [[culture/mapuche|Mapuche]] resisted the Inca and then the Spanish for centuries, and [[person/nzinga-of-ndongo-and-matamba|Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba]] used diplomacy and war against Portuguese expansion.
- European wars built and tested states. The [[event/thirty-years-war|Thirty Years' War]] (1618 to 1648) killed up to about eight million people, mostly from famine and disease, and ended with the [[event/peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]]; the [[event/seven-years-war|Seven Years' War]] (1756 to 1763) was fought on several continents; and the [[event/napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars]] (1803 to 1815) ended at Waterloo and helped set off Latin American independence movements.
- Revolutions and wars of independence recast who held power. See the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]], the [[event/american-revolution|American Revolution]], the [[event/french-revolution|French Revolution]], the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]], and the [[event/latin-american-wars-of-independence|Latin American Wars of Independence]], and, later, the [[event/mexican-revolution|Mexican Revolution]], the [[event/russian-revolution|Russian Revolution]], and the [[event/xinhai-revolution|Xinhai Revolution]].
- Wars between the Qing state, Britain, and rebels shook Asia in the 19th century: the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]] opened China to Western commerce on unequal terms, the [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]] (1850 to 1864) killed an estimated 20 to 30 million people, and the [[event/indian-rebellion-of-1857|Indian Rebellion of 1857]] ended East India Company rule ([[event/british-raj|British Raj]]).
- Wars of conquest and defense reshaped Africa and the settler colonies. The [[culture/zulu-kingdom|Zulu Kingdom]] under [[person/shaka|Shaka]] became a large military state and the [[event/mfecane|Mfecane]] followed; the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]] raised the share of the continent under European control from roughly 10 percent to about 90 percent between 1881 and 1914; Ethiopia won the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]] in 1896; and [[event/indian-removal-and-the-trail-of-tears|Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]] and the [[event/first-fleet-and-the-colonization-of-australia|First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]] led to dispossession and death for Native American and Aboriginal peoples.
- Industrial war reached unprecedented scale. Railways, telegraphs, and factories shaped the [[event/american-civil-war|American Civil War]]; [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] killed roughly 10 million soldiers and brought down four empires; [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]] killed an estimated 60 to 85 million people, including about six million Jews murdered in the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]]. The [[event/armenian-genocide|Armenian Genocide]] and the [[event/second-sino-japanese-war|Second Sino-Japanese War]], including the killings at Nanjing, show mass killing of civilians.
- [[technology/nuclear-fission|Nuclear Fission]], discovered in 1938, made nuclear weapons possible, and the [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]] began with the first test in July 1945. The [[event/cold-war|Cold War]] (about 1947 to 1991) was fought through arms races, alliances, and proxy wars, and ended with the [[event/collapse-of-the-soviet-union|Collapse of the Soviet Union]]; [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] was won by negotiation and by war, and the [[event/partition-of-india|Partition of India]] in 1947 brought mass migration and violence.
--- extra 2: Through the Eras
- **Paleolithic:** Clear evidence for organized fighting between groups is scarce, and scholars disagree about how common it was. DNA shows interbreeding among [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]], [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]], and [[species/denisovans|Denisovans]], so contact between human groups was not only conflict. See [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]].
- **Neolithic:** Settled life brought walls, stored food, and land to defend ([[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], [[place/jericho|Jericho]]), though the purpose of the early wall at Jericho is debated.
- **Bronze Age:** [[event/unification-of-egypt|Unification of Egypt]] around 3100 BCE, the king of Babylon [[person/hammurabi|Hammurabi]], the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]] and its chariots, the [[culture/hittites|Hittites]] and Egypt at the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]], the [[culture/mycenaean-greeks|Mycenaean Greeks]], the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]], and the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]].
- **Iron Age:** [[technology/iron-smelting|Iron Smelting]], the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]], the [[culture/scythians|Scythians]], the [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]], which ruled Egypt as its 25th Dynasty, the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] under [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], and [[place/carthage|Carthage]].
- **Classical Antiquity:** [[person/alexander-the-great|Alexander the Great]] and [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]], [[place/rome|Rome]] and the [[culture/romans|Romans]] under [[person/augustus|Augustus]], the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]], the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and the [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]], the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]] and [[person/ashoka|Ashoka]], the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]], and the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]].
- **Medieval Period:** The [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]], the [[event/battle-of-talas|Battle of Talas]], [[culture/norse|Norse]] raids from 793, the [[event/crusades|Crusades]], the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], [[person/timur|Timur]], the [[event/fall-of-constantinople|Fall of Constantinople]], and [[technology/gunpowder|Gunpowder]]; the [[event/classic-maya-collapse|Classic Maya Collapse]], in which warfare is among the proposed causes; and the [[culture/chola-dynasty|Chola Dynasty]], whose fleet struck [[culture/srivijaya|Srivijaya]] around 1025.
- **Early Modern Period:** The [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]] and [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-inca-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]], the [[event/thirty-years-war|Thirty Years' War]], the [[event/peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]], the [[event/seven-years-war|Seven Years' War]], the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]], the [[event/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]], the [[culture/mapuche|Mapuche]], and [[person/nzinga-of-ndongo-and-matamba|Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba]]. Japan under the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] enjoyed a long period of peace from 1603 to 1868.
- **Industrial Age:** The [[event/napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars]], [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]], [[event/latin-american-wars-of-independence|Latin American Wars of Independence]], [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]], [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]], [[event/american-civil-war|American Civil War]], [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]], [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]], [[event/world-war-i|World War I]], [[event/armenian-genocide|Armenian Genocide]], [[event/turkish-war-of-independence|Turkish War of Independence]], [[event/second-sino-japanese-war|Second Sino-Japanese War]], [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]], and the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]].
- **Information Age:** The [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]], the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]], [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]], the [[event/partition-of-india|Partition of India]], and the [[event/collapse-of-the-soviet-union|Collapse of the Soviet Union]].
--- extra 3: Regional Patterns
- **[[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]]:** The great powers of the Bronze Age and Iron Age, Egypt, the Hittites, and Assyria, fought over the Levant and left battle and treaty records on more than one side; see the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]] and the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]].
- **[[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]]:** Mobile pastoral powers ([[culture/scythians|Scythians]], [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]], [[culture/gokturk-khaganate|Göktürk Khaganate]], [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]]) repeatedly confronted settled states, from the Han frontier to Baghdad and Eastern Europe.
- **[[region/east-asia|East Asia]]:** Unification under Qin, the Han wars with the Xiongnu, Song use of gunpowder weapons, Ming construction of much of the Great Wall in its present form, and the Qing expansion to its greatest extent were followed by the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]], [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]], [[event/boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]], and [[event/second-sino-japanese-war|Second Sino-Japanese War]]; see [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]], [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]], and [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]].
- **[[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]]:** Wars of state-building ([[culture/zulu-kingdom|Zulu Kingdom]], [[culture/sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]]) and of resistance ([[culture/kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]], [[person/nzinga-of-ndongo-and-matamba|Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba]], [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]], the British conquest of [[culture/kingdom-of-benin|Kingdom of Benin]] in 1897) were followed by the forced labor and mass deaths of the [[event/congo-free-state|Congo Free State]] (1885 to 1908).
- **[[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] and the [[region/andes|Andes]]:** The [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] collected tribute from conquered city-states, whose resentment helped the Spanish; the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] expanded under [[person/pachacuti|Pachacuti]]; and warfare is among the proposed causes of the [[event/classic-maya-collapse|Classic Maya Collapse]], for which no single cause is accepted.
- **[[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]]:** [[culture/champa|Champa]] was gradually conquered by Vietnam until its last remnant was annexed in 1832, the [[culture/pagan-kingdom|Pagan Kingdom]] fell to Mongol armies in 1287, [[culture/ayutthaya-kingdom|Ayutthaya Kingdom]] was destroyed by Burmese forces in 1767, and the [[event/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]] used force to secure monopolies.
- **[[region/oceania|Oceania]]:** The [[event/first-fleet-and-the-colonization-of-australia|First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]] began conflict and dispossession for [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]], and disputes over land and authority after the [[event/treaty-of-waitangi|Treaty of Waitangi]] led to the New Zealand Wars of the 1840s to 1870s.
- **[[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]]:** Russian Cossacks and traders advanced to the Pacific between 1582 and 1639 for furs and tribute ([[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]]), and the [[culture/yakut-sakha|Yakut (Sakha)]] revolted against Russian rule in 1642.
--- extra 4: Debates and Limits
- Scholars disagree about how common organized warfare was in prehistory, and about whether it grew mainly with settled societies and states. The evidence is mostly skeletal injury, fortification, and weapons, and it can be read in more than one way.
- Casualty figures are uncertain and sometimes politically contested: the deaths from the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]] (tens of millions, uncertain), the [[event/congo-free-state|Congo Free State]] (about 1 million to 15 million), the [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]] (20 to 30 million), the killings at Nanjing in the [[event/second-sino-japanese-war|Second Sino-Japanese War]], and the [[event/armenian-genocide|Armenian Genocide]], which the Turkish state denies.
- Causes are often disputed. Historians debate whether the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] was a collapse or a transformation, and many regard the common claim that the [[event/peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]] created the sovereign-state system as a later simplification. They also disagree about the scale and causes of the [[event/mfecane|Mfecane]] and the role of [[person/shaka|Shaka]]. The [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]] and the [[event/classic-maya-collapse|Classic Maya Collapse]] have no single accepted cause.
- Many accounts come from the winners or from hostile writers: the accounts of [[person/shaka|Shaka]] come largely from colonial-era Europeans and oral tradition, later chronicles describe massacres by [[person/timur|Timur]] with contested numbers, and later historians hostile to the Qin described harsh punishments that are debated.
- The vault covers a selection of conflicts. It has notes on only three individual battles ([[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]], [[event/battle-of-talas|Battle of Talas]], [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]]) and none yet on naval, air, or guerrilla warfare as topics, on the Korean War or the Vietnam War beyond their mention under the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]], or on conflicts after 1991.

=== observer-note/frontiers-of-extraction
title: Frontiers of Extraction
type: observer-note
date: 
themes: trade, war, earth-systems
src: e5f5aff4505b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
What happens at the edge of a world economy when a distant demand meets a local resource?
--- observer
The Observer calls these frontiers of extraction: places where a resource is taken for a market far away, often by coercion. The commodity changes (fur, sugar, silver, rubber) but the structure recurs: distant demand, local labor, and a tribute or quota system. The frontier moves as one resource is exhausted or another is wanted. The Observer notes that the same exchange-webs that brought benefits to consumers could impose very large costs on people who never saw the markets they served.
--- extra 1: Question
What happens at the edge of a world economy when a distant demand meets a local resource?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Siberia: European demand for luxury furs drove Russian advance to the Pacific by 1639, and Indigenous peoples paid yasak in fur; see [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] and [[culture/yakut-sakha|Yakut (Sakha)]].
- The Caribbean and Atlantic: demand for sugar drove the forced migration of millions of Africans; see [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]].
- China: American silver, reaching the Ming through the Manila trade, became a major part of the economy; see [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] and [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
- The Congo: demand for rubber and ivory drove forced labor and mass deaths under a private ruler; see [[event/congo-free-state|Congo Free State]].
- In each case the frontier was run by small groups backed by a distant power, and local populations bore heavy costs.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The cases differ greatly in scale, in the forms of coercion, and in the roles of local elites. Treating them as a single pattern risks overlooking those differences, and it can also hide the agency of the people who lived on the frontiers.

=== observer-note/sanctioned-harm
title: Sanctioned Harm
type: observer-note
date: 
themes: war, technology, religion
src: 269c6ecef1c0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Why does a species with strong inhibitions against harming its own kind organize killing on a mass scale?
--- observer
The Observer calls this sanctioned harm: violence that a group approves through its story-glue. The same person who would not kill a stranger may do so under a uniform, a flag, or a doctrine. The Observer notes that the same story-glue that enables cooperation can be aimed against others. It notes with equal seriousness the counter-tradition: laws of war, pacifist movements, and the practice of [[person/mahatma-gandhi|Mahatma Gandhi]]'s nonviolence.
--- extra 1: Question
Why does a species with strong inhibitions against harming its own kind organize killing on a mass scale?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Evidence for organized violence appears in prehistory, but its scale grew with settled societies and states.
- Empires such as the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] and the [[culture/romans|Romans]] used organized force at large scale.
- Industrial states scaled the harm in [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]].
- The Holocaust shows that organized killing can be run as a bureaucratic process.
- The [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]] introduced weapons that could end a civilization.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The Observer records harm without endorsing it. This essay does not aim to explain away responsibility: individuals and institutions made real choices that could have been different.

=== observer-note/second-chances-how-the-species-ran-the-experiment-again
title: Second Chances - How the Species Ran the Experiment Again
type: observer-note
date: 
themes: technology, kinship, trade
src: a5ad4bb19629
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
When populations with no contact face similar problems, do they reach similar solutions?
--- observer
The Observer sees a natural experiment: the species' separation into two hemispheres for millennia acts as a controlled trial. The recurring outcomes, farming, then cities, then states and record-keeping, suggest that population density, storage, and the need to coordinate strangers push societies in similar directions. The differences show that these pressures do not dictate a single form. History has a direction, but it has many roads.
--- extra 1: Question
When populations with no contact face similar problems, do they reach similar solutions?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Farming arose independently in several regions, including the Fertile Crescent, China, New Guinea, and the Americas; see [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] and [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]].
- Cities and states arose separately in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus valley, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes; see [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]] and [[place/caral|Caral]].
- Writing arose independently at least three or four times; see [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]].
- The differences are as informative as the similarities: the Andes ran a large state without conventional writing ([[technology/quipu|Quipu]]), the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]] shows few signs of kings, and the Americas had no wheeled transport.
- The Old and New Worlds were largely out of contact from about 15,000 years ago until 1492, apart from brief Norse voyages around 1000 CE.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The number of cases is small, and some cases may not be fully independent, since ideas can spread across large regions. 'Independent' also should not be taken to mean 'identical': each society made its own choices.

=== observer-note/story-glue-how-strangers-cooperate-at-scale
title: Story-Glue - How Strangers Cooperate at Scale
type: observer-note
date: 
themes: religion, kinship, trade, war
src: 232917d9ecf4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Most animals cooperate with kin or with familiar individuals. How does one species cooperate in groups of millions who will never meet?
--- observer
The Observer calls this story-glue. A corporation, a nation, a currency, and a law code exist chiefly because many people act as though they do. This lets the species coordinate on scales no other animal reaches, and also lets it be led into shared errors. Changing the story can change behavior quickly, without changing anyone's biology.
--- extra 1: Question
Most animals cooperate with kin or with familiar individuals. How does one species cooperate in groups of millions who will never meet?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Small foraging bands relied on personal knowledge and kin ties.
- Cities from about 3500 BCE required cooperation among strangers; see [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]].
- Shared gods, rulers, and laws supplied common stories; see [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] and [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]].
- Universal religions and ethical systems of the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]] addressed strangers as members of one moral community.
- Modern states, corporations, and currencies also rest on shared belief; see [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]].
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The concept can be stretched too far. Material conditions, force, and self-interest also matter, and not every institution is only a story.

=== observer-note/the-fiction-of-claim-lines
title: The Fiction of Claim-Lines
type: observer-note
date: 
themes: war, kinship, trade
src: f730b4906ac9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
How can a line that does not exist on the ground change where millions of people live, work, and die?
--- observer
The Observer calls a border a claim-line. It has no physical form, yet it reliably changes behavior: people stop, present documents, or fight. The line works because enough people believe in it, and because enough force stands behind it. It is a clear example of story-glue made spatial. The Observer notes that the same species has also cooperated across claim-lines, through trade, disease control, and shared science.
--- extra 1: Question
How can a line that does not exist on the ground change where millions of people live, work, and die?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Early states had frontier zones and shifting allegiances more than sharp linear borders, as with the [[culture/romans|Romans]] and the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]].
- Fixed, mapped borders spread with modern nation-states, especially after the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]].
- Multi-ethnic empires such as the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] governed by layering communities rather than by drawing sharp national lines.
- Many current borders were drawn by colonial powers or treaties, sometimes across existing communities.
- Passports, customs, and armies give claim-lines their force.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The Observer's stance is not that borders are illusions to be dismissed. Borders can protect rights and communities. The point is only that they are made and maintained by human agreement.

--- summary
Why does a species with a large, capable memory keep moving its memory outside the body?

=== observer-note/the-settling-and-its-bargain
title: The Settling and Its Bargain
type: observer-note
date: 
themes: technology, kinship, earth-systems
src: 29fae329c594
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Why did foragers who lived well without farms take up a way of life that often looked worse for the individual?
--- observer
The Observer calls this the Settling. Its central bargain was to exchange leisure and variety for numbers and security. Once populations grew, going back was not easy, since fewer people could be fed by foraging. The species became dependent on its own creation. The Observer treats this as a repeated pattern: adopt a technology for advantage, then find that the technology has reshaped the conditions of life.
--- extra 1: Question
Why did foragers who lived well without farms take up a way of life that often looked worse for the individual?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Farming arose independently in several regions; see [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]].
- Skeletons from early farmers often show more disease and shorter stature than those of foragers.
- Farming supported larger populations, storage, and inequality.
- The sites [[place/gobekli-tepe|Göbekli Tepe]] and [[place/catalhoyuk|Çatalhöyük]] show that monuments and towns arose early, and in different ways.
- The [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]] made a second large jump in food production in the 20th century.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The 'bargain' story is contested. Some scholars, such as [[source/the-dawn-of-everything-graeber-and-wengrow|The Dawn of Everything (Graeber and Wengrow)]], stress that societies also chose and changed their arrangements freely and that many early communities experimented widely.

=== observer-note/why-the-species-buries-its-dead
title: Why the Species Buries Its Dead
type: observer-note
date: 
themes: religion, kinship, art
src: 46df994f759f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Why does a species spend large amounts of time, labor, and goods on bodies that can no longer work, fight, or reproduce?
--- observer
The Observer sees the grief-rite as a sign of two related abilities: the species remembers individuals after death, and it imagines that death is not a complete end. The rite also does social work, by marking who belongs to the group, who owes what to whom, and who inherits. A grave is both a statement about the dead and an instruction to the living. The Observer notes that resources committed to the dead are a form of status-signal to other groups, which may help explain why elite tombs grow so large.
--- extra 1: Question
Why does a species spend large amounts of time, labor, and goods on bodies that can no longer work, fight, or reproduce?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Intentional burial is documented among modern humans from at least about 100,000 years ago and is claimed, with debate, for [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] and [[species/homo-naledi|Homo naledi]].
- Graves in many cultures contain goods, ornaments, or food.
- The [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] invested state resources in tombs such as [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]].
- At [[place/catalhoyuk|Çatalhöyük]] the dead were buried beneath the floors of homes.
- Funerary customs range from burial and cremation to exposure and mummification, but nearly every known society has some grief-rite.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The Observer cannot know what mourners felt. Many claims about early burial rest on a few sites, and interpretations differ. The explanation may also vary by culture and era.
