=== technology/iron-smelting
title: Iron Smelting
type: technology
date: c. 1200 BCE
themes: technology, war, trade
src: 43247defe9e4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Iron smelting, extracting iron from ore, began on a limited scale in Anatolia in the early 2nd millennium BCE, possibly earlier, and became widespread across the Near East from the late 2nd millennium BCE, about 1200 BCE onward. Ironworking also developed in sub-Saharan Africa, and whether it did so independently is debated.
--- fact 1
Extractive iron metallurgy is attributed to Anatolia on a limited scale in the early 2nd millennium BCE, with the first major expansion in the late 2nd and early 1st millennium BCE. A small iron lump from Kaman-Kalehoyuk in Turkey, from a layer dated to about 2500 to 2250 BCE, has been argued to be earlier smelted iron, possibly made from ore from elsewhere; this is a single find and remains under study.
--- fact 2
Iron ore is much more common than copper and tin.
--- fact 3
Smelting requires higher temperatures and produced a tougher metal once carbon-steel techniques improved.
--- fact 4
The earliest reported dates for iron smelting in sub-Saharan Africa (about 2000 BCE at Lejja in Nigeria, Oboui in the Central African Republic, and Gbatoro in Cameroon) are contested, because it is unclear that the dated charcoal is securely tied to iron-working remains and because of old-carbon contamination. Iron working in the Nok culture of Nigeria dates between about the 9th century BCE and 550 BCE.
--- fact 5
Scholars are divided on whether African ironworking was independent of the Near East. Older theories of diffusion from Carthage or Meroe have been challenged, and current evidence leans toward independent African development.
--- fact 6
Iron tools made it easier to clear forests and to arm large populations.
--- context
Iron smelting defines the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and followed [[technology/bronze-metallurgy|Bronze Metallurgy]]. In Africa, the [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]] spread ironworking across eastern and southern regions.
--- observer
The Observer notes that a common metal changed who could afford tools and weapons. Cheaper materials often lead to broader participation, in production and in war.
--- question 1
Did iron working arise independently in sub-Saharan Africa? Evidence leans that way, but the very oldest dates are disputed.
--- question 2
How early did smelting begin in Anatolia, and did the 2500 to 2250 BCE lump come from a working smelting tradition or an isolated experiment?

=== technology/irrigation-canals
title: Irrigation Canals
type: technology
date: c. 5500 BCE
themes: technology, earth-systems
src: 0f90677e42ed
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Irrigation canals are channels that carry river water to fields. The earliest known examples are at Choga Mami in Iraq, dated to about the 6th millennium BCE, and canal systems later supported southern Mesopotamian cities and states in China.
--- fact 1
Choga Mami in eastern Iraq, a Samarran settlement occupied about 5600 to 4800 BCE, yielded canals identified by the excavators David and Joan Oates. Their date and irrigation role remain uncertain.
--- fact 2
Southern Mesopotamia has too little rain for grain, so cities such as [[place/uruk|Uruk]] relied on river canals by the 4th millennium BCE.
--- fact 3
The Code of Hammurabi, about 1754 BCE, makes a landholder pay for grain lost when a neglected dike or careless irrigation floods a neighbor's field.
--- fact 4
The Dujiangyan system in Sichuan, built by the state of Qin in 256 BCE, is still in use and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2000).
--- fact 5
Some scholars link salt buildup to declining yields in southern Mesopotamia in the late 3rd millennium BCE. The size of the effect is debated.
--- context
Irrigation canals extended the farming of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] onto dry river plains in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]]. See [[person/hammurabi|Hammurabi]] and the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]].
--- observer
The Observer regards irrigation canals as a running cost of the Settling: whoever depends on a canal must dig, clear, and police it, so shared rules become part of the tool. The Hammurabi liability clause is story-glue in the form of law holding infrastructure together. The reading would weaken if the earliest canals proved to be small local works kept up without wider authority.
--- question 1
Were the Choga Mami channels irrigation canals, and how old are they?
--- question 2
How much did salt buildup, rather than politics or climate, weaken southern Mesopotamian farming?

observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Nuclear fission, the splitting of heavy atomic nuclei, was discovered by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann in December 1938 and explained by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch.
--- fact 1
It releases enormous energy per unit of matter, roughly a million times more than chemical reactions.
--- fact 2
The first controlled chain reaction took place in Chicago in December 1942.

It powers nuclear reactors and nuclear weapons.
--- fact 4
Meitner did not share in the 1944 Nobel Prize awarded to Hahn, a case often cited as an injustice.
--- context
Fission builds on the work of [[person/marie-curie|Marie Curie]] and [[person/albert-einstein|Albert Einstein]] and led to the [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the scientific discovery and its military use came within a few years, a very short lag from knowledge to consequence.

--- summary
Oldowan tools are the earliest widespread stone tools, simple flakes and choppers made by striking cobbles, dating from about 2.6 million years ago.
--- fact 1
The oldest well-dated examples come from Gona in Ethiopia (about 2.6 million years old).
--- fact 2
Older, possibly earlier tools from Lomekwi, Kenya (about 3.3 million years ago), have been reported.

Flakes were used to cut meat and process plants.

Oldowan tools begin the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and are associated with early species such as [[species/homo-habilis|Homo habilis]].

The Observer sees the start of tool-lineage: an object made to make other things. The technique can be copied, which begins cumulative culture.

=== technology/outrigger-canoe
title: Outrigger Canoe
type: technology
date: c. 1600 BCE
themes: technology, trade
src: 431e098bb28e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
An outrigger canoe is a narrow hull steadied by a float attached to one or both sides. It was developed by Austronesian-speaking seafarers, and its early use is dated here to the Lapita expansion of about 1600 BCE, mainly from indirect evidence.
--- fact 1
The float lets a slender hull carry a sail and cargo without capsizing.
--- fact 2
Reconstructed Proto-Oceanic vocabulary includes terms for canoe parts and seafaring, which indicates outrigger canoes in the Lapita period. Wooden hulls rarely survive, so the date rests on the Lapita expansion, not on a preserved canoe.
--- fact 3
The [[culture/lapita-culture|Lapita Culture]] spread from the Bismarck Archipelago to Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa by roughly 1000 to 800 BCE.
--- fact 4
The [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] carried outrigger canoes from Taiwan through the Philippines and Indonesia into the Pacific, and to Madagascar in the first millennium CE.
--- fact 5
Reliefs at [[place/borobudur|Borobudur]] in Java, about 800 CE, show ships with outriggers.
--- context
The outrigger canoe links the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] voyages of [[region/oceania|Oceania]] to the later [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] and to the trade of maritime [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the outrigger as a tool-lineage solution simple enough to rebuild from memory on every island: a hull, a float, and a sail. The knowledge traveled without writing, which is why the early record is linguistic and archaeological. An alternative reading gives the double-hulled canoe, which the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]] also used, the central role in the far Pacific, and the evidence cannot yet rank the two designs.
--- question 1
When and where did the outrigger first appear, and could it be older than the Lapita expansion?
--- question 2
How were outrigger and double-hull designs divided among island groups?

=== technology/papyrus
title: Papyrus
type: technology
date: c. 2900 BCE
themes: language, technology, trade
src: 569dc01ae39d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Papyrus is a writing material made from strips of the papyrus plant, first used in Egypt. A blank roll from the tomb of Hemaka at Saqqara dates to about 2900 BCE, and the oldest inscribed papyri, from Wadi al-Jarf, to about 2560 BCE.
--- fact 1
A blank papyrus roll was found in the tomb of Hemaka, an official under the First Dynasty king Den, at Saqqara.
--- fact 2
A French team led by Pierre Tallet and Gregory Marouard found the oldest inscribed papyri at Wadi al-Jarf on the Red Sea coast in 2013. They include the logbook of an inspector named Merer, from about the 26th year of Khufu, recording limestone shipments for the Great Pyramid.
--- fact 3
Sheets were made from crossed layers of pressed stem strips and joined into rolls.
--- fact 4
Papyrus was exported around the Mediterranean and was a main writing material of Greece and Rome.
--- fact 5
Parchment and, from about the 10th to 11th century CE, [[technology/paper|Paper]] largely replaced it.
--- context
Papyrus is a [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] material of the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] that let the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] keep portable records, carrying the writing of [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. Finds tie it to the [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]] and the [[place/library-of-alexandria|Library of Alexandria]]. Threads: [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]], [[theme/technology|Technology]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer treats papyrus as an early step in memory-outsourcing that was light enough to travel: records kept in one place could be read in another. The oldest text found so far is a work logbook, not literature, which fits the view that administration drove early mark-code use. The reading would weaken if older religious or literary papyri turn up.
--- question 1
Were there inscribed papyri before the reign of Khufu that have not survived?
--- question 2
How much papyrus production did the state control?

=== technology/qanat
title: Qanat
type: technology
date: c. 714 BCE
themes: technology, earth-systems
src: 06ab3a2ce31f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A qanat is an underground channel that carries groundwater by gravity from a water-bearing slope to fields and settlements, reached by a line of vertical shafts. The earliest written record is an Assyrian account of 714 BCE describing qanats near Ulhu in northwestern Iran, and the system spread across much of Asia and North Africa.
--- fact 1
Sargon II of Assyria's account of his 714 BCE campaign against Urartu describes a district near Ulhu that stayed green with no river, and later readers identify the source as qanats. The system may be older, and its origin is debated.
--- fact 2
Workers dig a gently sloping tunnel from the settlement up into the water table, with vertical shafts at intervals for removing soil and for ventilation.
--- fact 3
The qanat of Gonabad in northeastern Iran, one of the longest, has 427 shafts and about 33 kilometers of tunnels, and some accounts date it to 700 to 500 BCE.
--- fact 4
More than 37,000 qanats are recorded in Iran, and UNESCO inscribed eleven of them as "The Persian Qanat" in 2016.
--- fact 5
Similar systems are called karez in Xinjiang, foggara in North Africa, and falaj in Oman.
--- fact 6
In many places, motor pumps in the 20th century lowered water tables and dried up qanats.
--- context
The qanat is an [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] water technology of the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], used under the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] and the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]]. Compare [[technology/irrigation-canals|Irrigation Canals]] and the [[technology/aqueduct|Aqueduct]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the qanat as a tool-lineage branch in which the skill lived in specialist well-diggers and passed on by apprenticeship rather than text. A qanat also binds a community: one tunnel serves everyone below it, so shares of water must be agreed, which makes it story-glue and infrastructure at once. The reading would weaken if records showed that qanats were usually run by landlords or the state instead of by village agreement.
--- question 1
Where and when did the first qanat appear?
--- question 2
How were water shares divided, and who enforced them?

mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A quipu (khipu) is a recording device made of knotted cords, used across the Andes and best known from the Inca Empire.
--- fact 1
The position and type of knots recorded numbers in a decimal system.
--- fact 2
About 900 to 1,000 quipus survive (923 are inventoried in the Khipu Database), and their color and structure may encode more than numbers; this is debated.
--- fact 3
Trained specialists called quipucamayocs kept and read them.
--- fact 4
Spanish colonial authorities banned or destroyed many of them.
--- context
The quipu was central to how the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] governed millions without conventional writing. Compare [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer regards the quipu as a distinct solution to memory-outsourcing. It shows that a complex state can be run on a record system that looks unlike anything in the written tradition.
--- question 1
Did quipus record narrative as well as numbers?

=== technology/railways

type: technology
date: 1825
themes: technology, trade
src: 386303f8f372
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Railways using steam locomotives began with the Stockton and Darlington Railway in England in 1825 and reshaped transport, trade, and the experience of time in the 19th century.
--- fact 1
The Stockton and Darlington Railway opened in 1825 as the first public railway to use steam locomotives.

The Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened in 1830, was the first intercity line run entirely by steam to a timetable.

The locomotive Rocket, built by Robert Stephenson, won the Rainhill Trials in 1829.

The first United States transcontinental railroad was completed at Promontory Summit, Utah, on 10 May 1869.
--- fact 5
Railway timetables led to standardized time zones, as in Britain from the 1840s and North America in 1883.
--- context
Railways applied the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]] to land transport and drove the growth of the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]]. They worked alongside the [[technology/telegraph-and-submarine-cables|Telegraph and Submarine Cables]] to coordinate movement. Railways sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a machine for moving coal changed the way the species measured the hour. Local noon gave way to a shared schedule because trains needed one, and clocks across a country were made to agree.

=== technology/rosetta-stone
title: Rosetta Stone
type: technology
date: 196 BCE
themes: language
src: b109c1375898
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Rosetta Stone is a stele inscribed in 196 BCE with a decree in Egyptian hieroglyphs, Demotic, and Greek, whose discovery in 1799 allowed Egyptian hieroglyphs to be deciphered.
--- fact 1
It records a decree of the priests of Memphis honoring Ptolemy V.
--- fact 2
French soldiers found it in 1799 near Rashid (Rosetta) during Napoleon's Egyptian campaign.
--- fact 3
Britain took it after the French defeat in 1801, and it has been in the British Museum since 1802.
--- fact 4
Thomas Young made early progress on the Demotic text.
--- fact 5
Jean-Francois Champollion announced his decipherment of hieroglyphs in 1822.
--- context
The stone unlocked the writing of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] and is a landmark in the history of [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. Its Greek text allowed comparison with a known language, unlike the more distant [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]]. Rosetta Stone sits in the [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a script unread for about fourteen centuries was recovered because a single administrative decree happened to be repeated in three writing systems. A record survives to be read only if someone else keeps the key.
--- question 1
Where should the stone be housed?

=== technology/sanitation-and-sewers
title: Sanitation and Sewers
type: technology
date: c. 2600 BCE
themes: technology, science
src: a91f82357580
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sanitation and sewers are systems that carry waste water and human waste away from where people live. The cities of the Indus Valley Civilization had drains and wells by about 2600 BCE, and urban sanitation was later tied to public health science in the 19th century.
--- fact 1
Cities of the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], such as [[place/mohenjo-daro|Mohenjo-daro]], had planned streets, brick-lined drains, and wells from about 2600 BCE.
--- fact 2
Minoan palaces such as [[place/knossos|Knossos]] on Crete had drainage channels and clay pipes in the 2nd millennium BCE.
--- fact 3
The Cloaca Maxima, the great drain of [[place/rome|Rome]], is traditionally dated to the 6th century BCE. It was rebuilt several times, and parts of it survive.
--- fact 4
In 1854 John Snow traced a cholera outbreak in Soho, London, to a water pump on Broad Street, an early argument that disease could travel in water.
--- fact 5
London's main sewer system, designed by Joseph Bazalgette, was approved in 1858 after the Great Stink and largely completed by 1875.
--- context
Sanitation links the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] cities of [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] to the public health projects of the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/science|Science]]. The aqueduct, by contrast, brought clean water in.
--- observer
The Observer notes that sanitation is a technology whose success is invisible: when it works, nothing happens. Indus and Roman cities built drains long before anyone could name the diseases they prevented, which shows the tool-lineage running ahead of pattern-taming, until mid-19th-century germ research supplied an explanation. The reading would weaken if the Bronze Age drains proved to be mainly for rainwater and display rather than health.
--- question 1
Were the Indus drains built for health, for rainwater, or for prestige?
--- question 2
Why were sewer systems so often neglected after conquest or collapse?

=== technology/sericulture
title: Sericulture
type: technology
date: c. 2700 BCE
themes: technology, trade
src: 50e0a781e578
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sericulture is the raising of silkworms to produce silk thread. It began in China, where silk fragments from Qianshanyang in Zhejiang date to about 2700 BCE, and silk later became a leading good of Eurasian trade and diplomacy.
--- fact 1
Silk fragments from Qianshanyang, a Liangzhu culture site in Zhejiang, date to about 2700 BCE. Earlier claims (a fabric at Qingtaicun in Henan, about 3630 BCE, and possible silk protein at Jiahu) are treated here with more caution.
--- fact 2
The domesticated silkworm, Bombyx mori, descends from the wild moth Bombyx mandarina.
--- fact 3
From the 2nd century BCE, the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] sent silk to the [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]] as gifts under peace arrangements.
--- fact 4
Silk moved west along the routes described in [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]].
--- fact 5
According to the Byzantine writer Procopius, two monks brought silkworm eggs to Emperor Justinian in 552 CE, hidden in bamboo rods. This account comes from Byzantine sources, and its details are not independently confirmed.
--- context
Sericulture is an [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] technology of the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] whose products shaped the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] as well as Chinese diplomacy. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer sees sericulture as a tool-lineage that could be held as a monopoly of knowledge: a craft that depends on one insect and a few skills can stay within one region for a very long time, and the smuggling stories show how much that knowledge was worth. Silk also worked as a portable status-signal and as a currency of diplomacy. The reading would weaken if the secrecy proved to be later legend rather than practice.
--- question 1
How old is silk in China, and which early claims will hold?
--- question 2
How closely was silkworm rearing guarded, and by whom?

--- summary
The steam engine converts heat into mechanical work. Thomas Newcomen built the first commercially successful engine in 1712, and James Watt's improvements from the 1760s made it far more efficient.
--- fact 1
Early engines pumped water out of coal mines.

Steam power freed factories from dependence on rivers.

The steam engine powered the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and prepared the way for the [[technology/electrical-power-grid|Electrical Power Grid]].

The Observer sees the first practical machine to turn stored sunlight, in the form of coal, into work. It is the first engine of the Great Acceleration.

Why did the steam engine emerge first in Britain?

--- summary
The electric telegraph, first demonstrated commercially in the 1840s, and the submarine cables that followed, allowed messages to cross continents and oceans in minutes instead of weeks.
--- fact 1
Samuel Morse sent the first public long-distance message, 'What hath God wrought', in 1844.
--- fact 2
A transatlantic cable was laid in 1858 but failed within weeks; a durable cable was completed in 1866.
--- fact 3
Cables linked Britain to India in the 1870s, and later most of the world.
--- fact 4
The telegraph changed news, markets, diplomacy, and warfare.
--- context
The telegraph followed the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]] and railways, prepared the ground for the [[technology/electrical-power-grid|Electrical Power Grid]], and is a distant ancestor of the [[event/digital-revolution|Digital Revolution]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that for the first time information traveled faster than any person or object. The species' sense of distance and of the present shrank in a single generation.

=== technology/the-wheel

type: technology
date: c. 3400 BCE
themes: technology, trade, war
src: 3d665b434e9e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Wheeled vehicles appear in the archaeological record around 3500 to 3000 BCE, in Mesopotamia, Europe, and the steppe.
--- fact 1
The oldest surviving wooden wheel, from the Ljubljana Marshes in Slovenia, is about 5,150 years old (radiocarbon dated to roughly 5,100 to 5,350 years ago).
--- fact 2
Pictographs from Uruk show sleds and wheeled carts.
--- fact 3
The wheel arrived in East Asia later, and was not used for transport in the Americas before contact.
--- fact 4
Potter's wheels used rotation for shaping clay.
--- context
The wheel belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and to the horse-and-wheel complex of the [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a great invention can wait a long time for the surrounding pieces: axles, roads, and draft animals. The Americas had wheels on toys but no draft animals large enough to pull them.
--- question 1
Where was the wheel first invented?

--- summary
The Trans-Siberian Railway, begun in 1891 and largely completed by 1916, is a railway line of about 9,300 kilometers linking Moscow with the Pacific port of Vladivostok.
--- fact 1
Construction began in 1891, with a ceremony at Vladivostok attended by the future Nicholas II.
--- fact 2
The full line between Moscow and Vladivostok is about 9,289 kilometers.
--- fact 3
The bridge over the Amur River at Khabarovsk, completed in 1916, closed the last major gap.
--- fact 4
It led to the movement of millions of settlers into Siberia.
--- fact 5
It is the longest continuous railway line in the world.
--- context
The railway completed the process begun in [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]], made possible by the technology of [[technology/railways|Railways]], and altered the lives of the [[culture/yakut-sakha|Yakut (Sakha)]] and other peoples. Trans-Siberian Railway sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a continent once crossed in months by river and sledge could now be crossed in about a week. Technology compresses distance and expands the reach of states that own it.

=== technology/water-clock
title: Water Clock
type: technology
date: c. 1400 BCE
themes: science, technology
src: 05966f3d0427
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A water clock, or clepsydra, measures time by the steady flow of water into or out of a vessel. The oldest surviving example is the alabaster clepsydra from Karnak, made in the reign of Amenhotep III in the 14th century BCE, and a written record points to an earlier Egyptian origin.
--- fact 1
The Karnak clepsydra was found in 1904 and is now in a Cairo museum. Its alabaster body has twelve columns of marks for the hours of the night, and water drips through a small hole in its base.
--- fact 2
A tomb inscription of a court official named Amenemhet, from the 16th century BCE, credits him with devising a water clock. This is earlier written evidence than the Karnak vessel, though it describes no surviving object.
--- fact 3
As the water level falls, the flow slows, so many designs used sloped sides or a constant-level inflow to keep the rate steady.
--- fact 4
In Athens, water clocks timed speeches in the law courts.
--- fact 5
Chinese and Islamic makers built elaborate water-driven clocks, and Su Song's astronomical clock tower of 1088 is among the best known. See [[technology/mechanical-clock|Mechanical Clock]].
--- context
The water clock was a [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] instrument of the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] that measured hours when a sundial could not, alongside the [[technology/calendars|Calendars]] that counted days. It belongs to the threads of [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]], and it later shaped the [[technology/mechanical-clock|Mechanical Clock]].
--- observer
The Observer treats the water clock as pattern-taming turned into a device: an hour becomes something a vessel can count without anyone watching the sky. Where courts used it to fix the length of speeches, a shared measure of time also worked as story-glue, since everyone accepted the count. The reading would weaken if early water clocks proved to be mostly ritual or royal display objects with little practical use.
--- question 1
Was the Egyptian water clock older than the Karnak example?
--- question 2
How accurate were early clepsydras compared with shadow clocks?

=== technology/zero-and-place-value-notation
title: Zero and Place-Value Notation
type: technology
date: c. 300 CE
themes: science, language, trade
src: 7c30f71c7625
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The decimal place-value system, in which zero serves as a digit and placeholder, developed in India in the first millennium CE and spread across the world.
--- fact 1
The Bakhshali manuscript, found in what is now Pakistan, uses a dot as a placeholder zero. It is a composite of birch-bark leaves from different periods: Oxford radiocarbon dating in 2017 gave 224 to 383 CE, 680 to 779 CE, and 885 to 993 CE for three folios, and a 2024 revision widened the overall range to 799 to 1102 CE and moved the oldest tested folio to 931 to 1032 CE. The claim that it holds the earliest zero is therefore contested.
--- fact 2
The Indian mathematician Brahmagupta gave rules for arithmetic with zero in 628 CE.
--- fact 3
Scholars in the Islamic world, including [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]], carried the system westward.
--- fact 4
Fibonacci's 1202 book popularized it in Europe.
--- context
The system emerged in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], possibly during the [[culture/gupta-empire|Gupta Empire]], and is the basis of all modern arithmetic and of the binary numbers used in the [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how a symbol for absence turned out to be one of the most productive ideas in history. Cultures without it could still do arithmetic, but not as easily.
--- question 1
When was zero first used as a number rather than a placeholder?
