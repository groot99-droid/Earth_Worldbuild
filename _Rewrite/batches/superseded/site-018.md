=== person/cixi
title: Cixi
type: person
date: 1835 – 1908
themes: war, kinship
src: b3c21ce93957
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Empress Dowager Cixi (1835 to 1908) was the dominant political figure of late Qing China, ruling as regent for most of the period from 1861 to 1908.
--- fact 1
She became regent in 1861 for her young son, the Tongzhi Emperor, and later for the Guangxu Emperor.
--- fact 2
Her period includes the Self-Strengthening Movement, an effort to modernize China's defenses and industry.
--- fact 3
She supported the Boxers in 1900, and after their defeat she pursued the 'New Policies' reforms.
--- fact 4
Historians debate her legacy; older accounts portray her as a reactionary, and recent work stresses her political skill and the limits she faced.
--- context
Cixi ruled the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]] through the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]] aftermath and the [[event/boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]]. Compare [[person/wu-zetian|Wu Zetian]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that, as with earlier women rulers, the record on Cixi was written largely by her opponents. See [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]].
--- question 1
How should Cixi's role in the Qing decline be assessed?

=== person/claude-monet
title: Claude Monet
type: person
date: 1840 – 1926
themes: art
src: a562288853e0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Claude Monet (14 November 1840 to 5 December 1926) was a French painter, the leader and purest practitioner of Impressionism. His Impression, Sunrise gave the movement its name, and he spent his life painting the changing effects of light.
--- fact 1
Monet was born in Paris and grew up in Le Havre, where as a teenager he sold caricatures. Eugène Boudin persuaded him to paint outdoors. In Paris he met Camille Pissarro and befriended Renoir, Sisley and Bazille.
--- fact 2
The 1860s and 1870s were years of poverty. During the Franco-Prussian War he took refuge in London, where he saw Turner's paintings and met the dealer Paul Durand-Ruel.
--- fact 3
In 1874 he and his friends held an exhibition independent of the official Salon. Critics mocked them, and the name "Impressionist" stuck.
--- fact 4
In 1883 his family settled at Giverny in Normandy, where he created a flower garden and a water garden with a Japanese bridge, which became his main subject.
--- fact 5
He painted the same subject repeatedly in changing conditions: haystacks, poplars and Rouen Cathedral. Key works include Impression, Sunrise (1872), Stacks of Wheat (1890 to 1891) and the Water Lilies.
--- fact 6
In his last decade he fought cataracts and worked on the giant Grandes Décorations, which he gave to the French state as a monument to peace after the First World War. He died at Giverny.
--- fact 7
The Abstract Expressionists rediscovered his late water lilies in the 1950s. His house and gardens opened to the public in 1980, and in 2019 one of his Haystacks sold for $110.7 million.
--- context
Monet worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], learned from [[person/j-m-w-turner|J. M. W. Turner]] and from Japanese prints by [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]] and [[person/utagawa-hiroshige|Utagawa Hiroshige]], and gave his last great work to France after [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter whose subject was the act of seeing, and whose method, the same motif in changing light, shows that one scene is many scenes. A movement was named by critics who meant to mock it, and the name became a badge, so insult and category overlapped. His paintings later became commodities of very high price, $110.7 million for a Haystacks in 2019, a status-signal the critics of 1874 could not have predicted.
--- question 1
How did his cataracts change the colour of his late paintings?

observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Confucius (Kong Fuzi, 551 to 479 BCE) was a Chinese teacher and thinker whose ideas about ethics, family, and government shaped East Asian societies for over two thousand years.
--- fact 1
His sayings were collected by disciples in the Analects.
--- fact 2
Central ideas include ren (humaneness), li (ritual propriety), and filial piety.
--- fact 3
He was not widely influential during his lifetime; his ideas rose to prominence under the Han dynasty.
--- fact 4
Confucian learning later became the basis of the imperial civil service examinations.
--- context
Confucius is one of the figures linked with the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]]. See [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and [[region/east-asia|East Asia]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a teacher without political power supplied the ethical framework of a large state. The kin-lattice, projected outward, became a model for government.
--- question 1
How much of the Analects reflects Confucius himself?

=== person/cyrus-the-great
title: Cyrus the Great
type: person
date: c. 600 – 530 BCE
themes: war, religion
src: c15646d2f371
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cyrus the Great founded the Achaemenid Persian Empire in the 6th century BCE by conquering the Medes, Lydia, and Babylon.
--- fact 1
His birth date is uncertain, with scholars giving about 600 to 576 BCE.
--- fact 2
He defeated the Median king Astyages around 550 BCE and the Lydian king Croesus around 547 BCE.
--- fact 3
He took Babylon in 539 BCE.
--- fact 4
The Cyrus Cylinder, a Babylonian clay cylinder found in 1879, records his account of the conquest and his restoration of gods and deported peoples.
--- fact 5
He died in 530 BCE campaigning on the eastern frontier and was buried at Pasargadae.
--- context
Cyrus created the largest empire the world had yet seen, described in [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]]. He is treated with respect in the Hebrew Bible and by Greek writers, including in the accounts of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]]. Cyrus the Great sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Cyrus is remembered as an unusually tolerant conqueror, mainly through his own inscription and the writings of those he benefited. The reading would change if evidence showed the tolerance was largely a later projection.
--- question 1
How far does the Cyrus Cylinder reflect a real policy rather than propaganda?

=== person/darius-i
title: Darius I
type: person
date: c. 550 – 486 BCE
themes: war, trade, language
src: 086ae5292b8f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Darius I (about 550 to 486 BCE) was king of the Achaemenid Persian Empire from 522 BCE. He organized its provinces, began the building of Persepolis, and recorded his rise to power in the Behistun Inscription.
--- fact 1
Darius I was born about 550 BCE and became king in 522 BCE after the death of Cambyses II. He put down revolts across the empire in his first years.
--- fact 2
The Behistun Inscription, carved on a cliff in western Iran, tells the story of his rise in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian. Scholars debate whether its account of a usurper, Gaumata, is accurate, and Aramaic copies of the text have been found in Egypt.
--- fact 3
He divided the empire into provinces (satrapies) under governors and issued a standard gold coin, the daric.
--- fact 4
He is credited with the Royal Road from Sardis to Susa and began the terrace at Persepolis about 518 BCE.
--- fact 5
His inscriptions honor the god Ahura Mazda.
--- fact 6
After the Ionian Greek cities revolted in 499 BCE, he sent forces against Athens, and the Athenians defeated them at Marathon in 490 BCE.
--- fact 7
Darius died in 486 BCE and was succeeded by Xerxes I.
--- context
Darius ruled the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], founded by [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], from the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]; see [[place/persepolis|Persepolis]]. His wars involved the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Darius told the story of his rise in stone, in three languages, on a cliff few could read, and sent copies across the empire. A multilingual empire needs a story-glue that works in several sound-codes, and a winner's account of a contested succession is a claim as much as a record. The reading would weaken if the usurper story proved accurate, since the inscription would then be closer to plain reporting.
--- question 1
Was Gaumata a real usurper or an invention of Darius's court?

=== person/dido
title: Dido
type: person
date: c. 814 – 760 BCE
themes: trade, kinship
src: 869e5adfc76c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Dido, also called Elissa, is the legendary founder-queen of Carthage, traditionally said to have led Phoenician settlers from Tyre to found the city on the North African coast around 814 BCE; her birth and death dates are not historically fixed.
--- fact 1
The earliest surviving account of her founding of Carthage comes from the Greek historian Timaeus of Tauromenium, writing in the 4th to 3rd century BCE, centuries after the traditional founding date.
--- fact 2
Tradition holds she fled Tyre after her brother, King Pygmalion, murdered her husband to seize his wealth, and that she later took her own life rather than marry a local Libyan king, Iarbas, who threatened war.
--- fact 3
No Phoenician inscription or contemporary record names a founder called Dido or Elissa; archaeology confirms Phoenician settlement at the site of Carthage beginning around the traditional founding period, which lends some support to a folk memory of an organized colonizing expedition.
--- fact 4
Her best-known appearance, as the doomed lover of the Trojan hero Aeneas in Virgil's Aeneid, is a literary invention: the Trojan War's traditional date is centuries before Carthage's traditional founding, so the two could not have met.
--- context
Dido is remembered as the founder of Carthage, a [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]] colony in the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], placed in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] by tradition. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Dido's story likely preserves a genuine memory of Tyrian colonizing voyages, reshaped over centuries into the tale of one heroic founder, the way founding narratives often compress a longer, messier process into a single named person. Virgil's later romance with Aeneas shows how a founding legend keeps being rewritten to serve whichever storyteller's purposes come next.
--- question 1
Does the Dido legend preserve any specific historical event, or is it entirely a later literary construction built around a folk memory of colonization?

=== person/diego-velazquez
title: Diego Velázquez
type: person
date: 1599 – 1660
themes: art
src: 98026b016257
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Diego Velázquez (1599 to 1660) was the leading painter of Spain's Golden Age and court painter to King Philip IV for 37 years. His loose, luminous brushwork and unflattering portraits of kings, courtiers and jesters made him a "painter's painter".
--- fact 1
Velázquez was baptised in Seville on 6 June 1599. At about 11 he entered the workshop of Francisco Pacheco, and in 1618 he married Pacheco's daughter Juana. His early Seville work shows the influence of Caravaggio.
--- fact 2
In 1623 a portrait of the young Philip IV won him the post of court painter, and he moved to Madrid. He rose through court offices to become chamberlain of the palace.
--- fact 3
Peter Paul Rubens, on a diplomatic mission to Madrid in 1628, encouraged him to visit Italy. His first Italian trip (1629 to 1631) transformed his colour and handling. A second (1649 to 1651) produced his portrait of Pope Innocent X.
--- fact 4
He painted Las Meninas in 1656 and in 1659 was admitted to the noble Order of Santiago, a rare honour for a painter. He died on 6 August 1660.
--- fact 5
He painted alla prima, often without drawing, and altered compositions on the canvas; X-rays reveal many changes of mind. His palette was restrained, built on greys, blacks and earth tones.
--- fact 6
His work stayed largely in Spanish royal palaces until the Prado opened in 1819. Manet travelled to Madrid in 1865 and called him "the painter of painters".
--- fact 7
Picasso painted 58 variations on Las Meninas (1957), and Francis Bacon's screaming popes are based on his Innocent X.
--- context
Velázquez served the Spanish court in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and began under the influence of [[person/caravaggio|Caravaggio]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter who recorded a king's heavy jaw and a court dwarf's dignity with the same honesty inside an institution built to display power, so that the candour worked within the status-signal machinery rather than against it. His work was kept in palaces until 1819, which makes his influence largely a nineteenth-century event. The title "painter of painters" is a lineage told by painters about a painter, a kind of ancestor-weight chosen rather than inherited.
--- question 1
What do the changes of mind revealed by X-rays show about how he worked?

=== person/edvard-munch
title: Edvard Munch
type: person
date: 1863 – 1944
themes: art
src: 479face78c99
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Edvard Munch (12 December 1863 to 23 January 1944) was a Norwegian painter and printmaker who turned illness, grief, love and fear into images of inner states. The Scream became an emblem of the modern psyche, and he is a founding figure of Expressionism.
--- fact 1
Munch was born in Løten, Norway, and grew up in Kristiania (now Oslo). His mother died of tuberculosis when he was five and his sister Sophie of the same disease when he was 13. Another sister was later hospitalised for mental illness.
--- fact 2
In the 1890s he lived mostly in Berlin and Paris and developed the Frieze of Life cycle. His 1892 exhibition in Berlin was closed after a week amid scandal, which made him famous in Germany.
--- fact 3
He suffered a breakdown in 1908 and was treated in Copenhagen. From 1916 he lived at his estate, Ekely, outside Oslo. In 1937 the Nazis labelled his work "degenerate" and removed it from German museums.
--- fact 4
He simplified forms into flowing outlines and flat expressive colour, influenced by Van Gogh, Gauguin and Symbolism. His woodcuts and lithographs are as important as his paintings.
--- fact 5
Key works include The Sick Child (1885 to 1886), The Scream (1893), Madonna (1894) and The Dance of Life (1899 to 1900).
--- fact 6
He left about 1,100 paintings, 4,500 drawings and 18,000 prints to the city of Oslo. The Scream has been stolen twice, in 1994 and 2004, and recovered both times.
--- context
Munch worked in Norway, Germany and France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and drew on [[person/vincent-van-gogh|Vincent van Gogh]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter who said he painted what he had seen rather than what he saw, so that memory, not the eye, was the source of the image. The family record of early death and illness is part of how his work is read, which risks turning every picture into biography. That the Nazi label "degenerate" was applied to an artist now central to the canon shows how a state can act as a critic with the power to remove works from view.
--- question 1
How far should the family history of illness govern the reading of his pictures?

=== person/eleanor-of-aquitaine
title: Eleanor of Aquitaine
type: person
date: c. 1122 – 1204
themes: kinship, war
src: a6b34bd6df21
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Eleanor of Aquitaine (about 1122 to 1204) was successively queen of France and queen of England, and one of the wealthiest and most powerful women in 12th-century Europe as ruler of the Duchy of Aquitaine in her own right.
--- fact 1
She inherited the vast Duchy of Aquitaine in southwestern France in 1137 and married King Louis VII of France that same year; the marriage was annulled in 1152 after producing no sons.
--- fact 2
Weeks after the annulment she married Henry of Anjou, who became King Henry II of England in 1154, bringing her extensive French territories into the combined "Angevin Empire" that spanned much of France and England.
--- fact 3
She supported a rebellion by three of her sons against Henry II in 1173 to 1174; after its failure, Henry kept her in confinement for about fifteen years until his death in 1189.
--- fact 4
Freed by her son Richard I ("the Lionheart"), she acted as regent during his crusade and captivity, and later helped secure the throne for her youngest son, John, after Richard's death in 1199.
--- context
Eleanor of Aquitaine ruled territory in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and her marriages linked the crowns of France and England. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Eleanor's own duchy, not either crown she wore by marriage, was the base of her lasting power: it was Aquitaine's wealth and loyalty that gave her leverage over two kings and let her outlast a long confinement to shape the succession in her old age.
--- question 1
How much independent political judgment did Eleanor exercise in backing her sons' rebellion, versus acting on their initiative?

=== person/enheduanna
title: Enheduanna
type: person
date: c. 2285 – 2250 BCE
themes: religion, language, art
src: 246dd228fe20
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Enheduanna (about 2285 to 2250 BCE) was a high priestess of the moon god Nanna at the Sumerian city of Ur and a daughter of Sargon of Akkad. She is often called the earliest author known by name, though her dates and the attribution of her texts are debated.
--- fact 1
Enheduanna served as en-priestess of Nanna at Ur, appointed by her father Sargon of Akkad. Her dates depend on the chronology used.
--- fact 2
A calcite disc found at Ur in 1927 by Leonard Woolley's team shows a figure identified by inscription as Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon, in a ritual scene.
--- fact 3
Sumerian poems are attributed to her, including 42 temple hymns and hymns to the goddess Inanna such as "The Exaltation of Inanna."
--- fact 4
The surviving copies come from schools of the Old Babylonian period, about 1800 BCE, some five centuries after her lifetime, and scholars disagree about whether she wrote every attributed text.
--- fact 5
In "The Exaltation of Inanna" the speaker describes being driven from office by a usurper, Lugal-ane, and restored; the historicity of this is debated.
--- context
Enheduanna wrote in the Sumerian language of the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] while serving a ruler from the Akkadian-speaking north, in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]. Her work belongs to the story of the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a conquering dynasty placed a royal daughter at the head of a conquered city's main cult, a way of joining a new claim-line to old sacred authority through the kin-lattice. The same priestess is the first author recorded by name, so mark-code served a political and a devotional purpose at once. Because the texts survive only in later copies, the reading would weaken if the attribution to her proved to be a later tradition.
--- question 1
Which of the texts attributed to her can be tied to her own time?

=== person/ezana-of-aksum
title: Ezana of Aksum
type: person
date: c. 303 – 350 CE
themes: religion, war, language
src: ad247aa26d0c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ezana (about 303 to about 350 CE) was king of Aksum, in present-day Ethiopia and Eritrea, from about 320. He is known for adopting Christianity and for a campaign in the Nile valley, and his inscriptions and coins record both.
--- fact 1
Ezana was born about 303 CE and ruled the Kingdom of Aksum from about 320 until about 350. Dates for his life and reign are approximate.
--- fact 2
His early inscriptions and coins show older Aksumite religion, including a crescent and disc on his coins.
--- fact 3
Frumentius, a Syrian Christian who rose in the Aksumite court, is reported to have been consecrated Aksum's first bishop by Athanasius of Alexandria in the 330s.
--- fact 4
Ezana converted to Christianity, probably in the 330s or 340s. His later inscriptions invoke a "Lord of Heaven," and his coins replace the crescent and disc with the cross, among the earliest coins to do so.
--- fact 5
His inscriptions are in Ge'ez, Sabaean and Greek.
--- fact 6
About 350 an Aksumite force campaigned in the Nile valley against Kush and neighboring peoples. The campaign is often linked to the end of Kush's power, though historians debate the causes.
--- context
Ezana ruled the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] and campaigned against the [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]] in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a king's change of religion is visible in stone and coin: the same ruler's inscriptions change gods and his coinage changes symbols. A state's meaning-engine was switched from above and announced as a status-signal to the traders who used the coins across the Red Sea world. The reading would weaken if evidence showed that most of his subjects kept older practices for a long time after his conversion.
--- question 1
When did his conversion take place, and how quickly was it announced?

=== person/francisco-goya
title: Francisco Goya
type: person
date: 1746 – 1828
themes: art, war
src: 6af906c428fa
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Francisco Goya (30 March 1746 to 16 April 1828) was a Spanish court painter and printmaker, often called the last of the Old Masters and the first of the moderns. Illness, political repression and the Napoleonic invasion drove him from brilliant royal portraits toward satire, violence and the irrational.
--- fact 1
Goya was born in Fuendetodos in Aragon and grew up in Zaragoza. In 1773 he married Josefa Bayeu, sister of the court painter Francisco Bayeu, who helped him win work designing tapestry cartoons in Madrid. By 1786 he was painter to the king, and in 1799 First Court Painter to Charles IV.
--- fact 2
In 1792 to 1793 an illness left him permanently deaf. In 1799 he published Los Caprichos, satirical etchings mocking superstition and the clergy.
--- fact 3
When Napoleon's armies invaded Spain in 1808 he witnessed atrocities on both sides and recorded them in The Disasters of War, prints published only 35 years after his death. He painted The Second and Third of May 1808 in 1814.
--- fact 4
Disillusioned by the repressive rule of Ferdinand VII, he retreated to a house outside Madrid and covered its walls with the Black Paintings, including Saturn Devouring His Son (1819 to 1823). In 1824 he went into exile in Bordeaux, where he died.
--- fact 5
His portraits, influenced by Velázquez, use loose brushwork and avoid flattery. He mastered aquatint for deep shadows and later applied paint with knives, rags and fingers.
--- fact 6
Goya named Rembrandt as one of his three masters. Manet's Execution of Emperor Maximilian and Picasso's Guernica both descend from The Third of May.
--- fact 7
The Prado holds the greatest collection of his work.
--- context
Goya worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] and lived through the [[event/napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], learning from [[person/diego-velazquez|Diego Velázquez]] and [[person/rembrandt-van-rijn|Rembrandt van Rijn]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a court painter who recorded sanctioned harm from inside the society that practised it, and who kept the record private: The Disasters of War were withheld for 35 years. The same hand produced unflattering royal portraits and images of witches and monsters, which shows that the meaning-engine of the period, Church and crown, did not exhaust what one person could see. Later artists took his war images as a model, a case of ancestor-weight chosen by those who wanted a precedent for witnessing.
--- question 1
Why did he withhold The Disasters of War, and what did he intend for the Black Paintings?

=== person/frank-lloyd-wright
title: Frank Lloyd Wright
type: person
date: 1867 – 1959
themes: art, technology
src: e68fbe03a593
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Frank Lloyd Wright (1867 to 1959) was the most influential American architect of the 20th century. Over seven decades he argued for "organic architecture", buildings designed from the inside out around their site, materials and users.
--- fact 1
Wright was born in Richland Center, Wisconsin, studied engineering briefly at the University of Wisconsin and moved to Chicago, where he worked under Louis Sullivan, whom he later called his "Lieber Meister".
--- fact 2
He set up his own practice in Oak Park in the 1890s and developed the low, wide-eaved Prairie house style. His personal life was turbulent: an affair, a double murder at his Wisconsin home Taliesin in 1914, and bankruptcy during the Depression.
--- fact 3
In his late sixties he designed Fallingwater and the Johnson Wax headquarters (both 1936 to 1939), and finally the Guggenheim Museum (1956 to 1959). He died in 1959 aged 91.
--- fact 4
He designed around open, flowing interior space, with art-glass bands and deep cantilevered overhangs, and used concrete, steel and glass without disguising them as historical stonework. He designed most of the furniture, textiles and stained glass in his buildings himself.
--- fact 5
He designed more than a thousand structures, roughly half built. Fallingwater and several others are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
--- fact 6
Late in life he planned an unrealised city, Broadacre City, of low-density, car-based suburban living. He was also a major collector and dealer of Hiroshige's prints.
--- context
Wright worked in [[region/north-america|North America]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and collected the prints of [[person/utagawa-hiroshige|Utagawa Hiroshige]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes an architect whose principle was that a building grows from its site and its users rather than from a borrowed style, yet whose own name became a style. His open plan, now standard in American houses, shows a design idea spreading until it is no longer credited. Broadacre City, unbuilt, anticipated a pattern of car-based suburban living that later development followed, which suggests a vision can shape a society without being carried out by its author.
--- question 1
How much of postwar suburban design can be traced to his ideas rather than to independent development?

=== person/frederick-barbarossa
title: Frederick Barbarossa
type: person
date: c. 1122 – 1190
themes: war, religion, kinship
src: ead8b3bae4d7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Frederick I, called Barbarossa ("Redbeard"; about 1122 to 1190), was Holy Roman Emperor from 1155 until his death, and one of medieval Europe's most powerful rulers, spending much of his reign contesting control of northern Italy.
--- fact 1
Elected King of Germany in 1152, he was crowned Holy Roman Emperor by the pope in 1155, and repeatedly invaded Italy to assert imperial authority over its wealthy independent city-states.
--- fact 2
The Lombard League, an alliance of northern Italian cities backed by the papacy, defeated his forces at the Battle of Legnano in 1176, forcing him to recognize significant city autonomy in the Peace of Constance of 1183.
--- fact 3
He arranged the marriage of his son Henry to Constance of Sicily, a match that later brought the Kingdom of Sicily under his family's control.
--- fact 4
He drowned in 1190 while crossing a river in Anatolia during the Third Crusade, before reaching the Holy Land; his death caused much of his army to disband or turn back rather than continue the campaign.
--- context
Frederick Barbarossa ruled the Holy Roman Empire in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], contesting authority with the papacy and Italian city-states. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Barbarossa's defeat at Legnano, a rare and decisive loss for an emperor to city militias rather than a rival monarch, shows the limits of imperial authority once wealthy urban communities organized collectively. His accidental death crossing a river, not a battle, then ended a Crusade that a stronger start could not save from being cut short by chance.
--- question 1
Would the Third Crusade's outcome for the German contingent have differed meaningfully had Barbarossa survived to reach the Holy Land?

=== person/frida-kahlo
title: Frida Kahlo
type: person
date: 1907 – 1954
themes: art, kinship
src: 2d0416119d24
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Frida Kahlo (1907 to 1954) was a Mexican painter, best known for small, vivid self-portraits made in Mexico City. She worked through years of pain after a bus accident in 1925 and became a global cultural icon after her death.
--- fact 1
Frida Kahlo was born on 6 July 1907 in Coyoacán, now part of Mexico City. She often gave 1910, the year the Mexican Revolution began, as her birth year.
--- fact 2
She had polio at six. On 17 September 1925 a bus accident broke her spine, pelvis and right leg and ended her plan to study medicine. She began painting during her recovery.
--- fact 3
She married the muralist Diego Rivera in 1929, divorced him in 1939 and remarried him in 1940.
--- fact 4
Estimates of her output run from fewer than 150 to about 200 paintings, most of them self-portraits, including The Two Fridas (1939) and The Broken Column (1944).
--- fact 5
Her first solo show was at the Julien Levy Gallery in New York in November 1938. In 1939 the Louvre bought her painting The Frame, the first work by a Mexican artist in its collection.
--- fact 6
She died in Mexico City on 13 July 1954. The recorded cause was a pulmonary embolism, which some biographers question. Her work was little known until art historians rediscovered it in the late 1970s.
--- context
Frida Kahlo worked in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], in the decades after the [[event/mexican-revolution|Mexican Revolution]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a painter made her own body the subject of most of her work and moved her birth year to 1910, tying her life to the story of a nation's revolution. Since the late 1970s her image has served as story-glue for movements that did not exist in her lifetime. A specialist could argue that this later fame flattens a private, ambivalent artist into an emblem, which would weaken any reading of the icon as what she intended.
--- question 1
How much of her public persona did she shape herself, and how much was added after 1954?

=== person/fu-hao
title: Fu Hao
type: person
date: c. 1250 – 1200 BCE
themes: war, religion, kinship
src: 9e69de0d18da
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Fu Hao (died about 1200 BCE) was a consort of the Shang king Wu Ding, named in oracle bone inscriptions as a military commander and a ritual leader. Her intact tomb, found in 1976, is among the richest of the Shang period. Her birth year is unknown.
--- fact 1
Fu Hao is named in oracle bone inscriptions as a wife of the Shang king Wu Ding, whose reign is dated about 1250 to 1192 BCE, though dates vary by chronology. She is thought to have died about 1200 BCE.
--- fact 2
The inscriptions record her leading military campaigns against neighboring groups and directing ritual sacrifices.
--- fact 3
Her tomb (M5) at Yinxu, the Shang capital site near Anyang in Henan, was found undisturbed in 1976 by a team led by Zheng Zhenxiang.
--- fact 4
It held 468 bronze objects and 755 jade objects, as well as hundreds of bone objects and thousands of cowry shells.
--- fact 5
Inscriptions on many bronzes name her, Fu Hao, or "Mother Xin," which is the basis for identifying the tomb with the woman of the oracle bones.
--- fact 6
Sixteen people and six dogs were buried with her, interpreted as sacrifices.
--- context
Fu Hao lived under the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]; her tomb shows the scale of [[technology/bronze-metallurgy|Bronze Metallurgy]] in the Shang state. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the earliest written evidence for a woman commanding armies comes from divination records made by her husband for other purposes, records that also ask about her illness and childbirth. Her burial, with a large store of bronze and jade and with human sacrifices, uses a grief-rite to display status. The reading of the tomb and the texts as one life would weaken if the oracle bone Fu Hao and the woman in tomb M5 were different persons.
--- question 1
How large were the armies she led, and how reliable are the oracle bone figures?

=== person/georges-melies
title: Georges Méliès
type: person
date: 1861 – 1938
themes: art, technology
src: 372bfa3ae8ca
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Georges Méliès (1861 to 1938) was a Paris stage magician who became a filmmaker and invented cinema's first special effects. Over roughly 500 films in under twenty years, including A Trip to the Moon (1902), he turned the new camera from a device for recording reality into a machine for staging the impossible.
--- fact 1
Méliès was born in Paris to a prosperous shoe-manufacturing family and trained as a painter. In 1888 he bought the Théâtre Robert-Houdin, a famous conjuring theatre.
--- fact 2
After attending the Lumière brothers' first public film screening in December 1895, he tried to buy a camera from them and was refused. He acquired one from a British inventor and built a studio at Montreuil with a glass-walled stage.
--- fact 3
About 1896 he discovered, by accident when his camera jammed mid-shot, that stopping and restarting filming could make objects vanish or transform. This became the "stop trick" behind his trick films.
--- fact 4
His techniques included stop-motion substitution, multiple exposure, dissolves, painted sets built to force perspective, and hand-tinted colour done frame by frame by a team of women colourists.
--- fact 5
American piracy of his films, the rise of narrative studio filmmaking and the disruption of the First World War ruined his business. He was reduced to running a toy stall in a Paris train station.
--- fact 6
He was rediscovered in his last decade, awarded the Légion d'honneur and given a small pension. He died in Paris in 1938. Martin Scorsese's 2011 film Hugo dramatised his life.
--- fact 7
Key works include The Vanishing Lady (1896), A Trip to the Moon (1902), The Kingdom of the Fairies (1903) and The Impossible Voyage (1904).
--- context
Méliès worked in France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], beside the [[person/lumiere-brothers|Lumière Brothers]], and his business collapsed in the aftermath of [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a magician who saw in a recording device a stage: the same machine that recorded ordinary life in the Lumières' hands made the impossible in his. A camera jam is the standard account of the origin of the stop trick, the sort of founding accident a tool-lineage likes to remember, and it should be treated as an account rather than a proven event. That piracy and the market for narrative film ruined him shows an early case of the copy outrunning the maker.
--- question 1
Did the camera jam actually happen as told, and when?

observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Gilgamesh was a king of the Sumerian city of Uruk, probably a real ruler of the Early Dynastic period sometime between about 2800 and 2500 BCE, who became the subject of the Epic of Gilgamesh, the earliest surviving major work of narrative literature.
--- fact 1
The Sumerian King List names him as the fifth king of Uruk's first dynasty and credits him with an implausible 126-year reign, typical of the list's treatment of early rulers.
--- fact 2
Inscriptions credit a king of this name with building the great city walls of Uruk, a claim the Epic itself repeats as Gilgamesh's lasting achievement.
--- fact 3
The Epic, assembled from older Sumerian poems into its best-known Akkadian version around the 12th century BCE, follows Gilgamesh's friendship with the wild man Enkidu, his quest for immortality after Enkidu's death, and his eventual acceptance of mortality.
--- fact 4
No inscription from Gilgamesh's own lifetime has been found; everything historians infer about a real Gilgamesh comes from later king lists, dedicatory inscriptions naming him, and the literary tradition itself.
--- context
Gilgamesh ruled Uruk among the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Gilgamesh sits at an unusual boundary: probably a real king, almost certainly not the demigod of the Epic, yet known to history mainly because later scribes turned him into a literary figure grappling with death rather than because his own deeds were separately recorded. The line between the ruler and the character is not fully recoverable.

How much of the historical Gilgamesh's biography, beyond the city walls, can be separated from the literary tradition built around his name?

=== person/giotto
title: Giotto
type: person
date: c. 1267 – 1337
themes: art, religion
src: 080dbe94c3c4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Giotto di Bondone (about 1267 to 1337) was a Florentine painter who gave medieval figures weight, emotion and space. Many call him the father of Western painting, and his frescoes in the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua are his firmest work.
--- fact 1
Giotto was probably born about 1267 in the Mugello valley north of Florence. Vasari's story of the shepherd boy discovered by Cimabue is legend, but he was almost certainly trained in Florentine workshops.
--- fact 2
Many scholars believe he worked on the Legend of St Francis frescoes at Assisi in the late 1290s; his early career is still debated.
--- fact 3
He painted the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua about 1303 to 1305 for the banker Enrico Scrovegni. It is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
--- fact 4
He worked in Rome, in Santa Croce in Florence, and at the court of King Robert of Anjou in Naples from about 1328 to 1333.
--- fact 5
In 1334 Florence named him master of works for the cathedral, and he designed its bell tower. He died in January 1337 and was buried with public honours.
--- fact 6
He worked in buon fresco, painting into wet plaster one day's section at a time. His figures have volume and react to one another, and his spaces suggest depth more than 100 years before linear perspective was worked out.
--- fact 7
In the chapel's Adoration of the Magi the star is painted as a blazing comet, perhaps inspired by Halley's Comet of 1301. The European Space Agency named its 1986 Halley probe Giotto.
--- fact 8
Vasari opened his Lives of the Artists with Cimabue and Giotto. Masaccio and Michelangelo studied and copied Giotto's frescoes.
--- context
Giotto worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], before the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the subject was fixed, the story of a meaning-engine repeated in every church, and that the change lay in how the story was made felt. Giotto's figures glance and grieve, so that sacred scenes read as human drama. The title "father of Western painting" comes largely from Vasari and later writers who needed a founder for a story of revival, which makes it partly a story-glue for art history rather than a plain finding. His early career and the Assisi attribution remain disputed.
--- question 1
How much of the Assisi Legend of St Francis cycle is by Giotto?
