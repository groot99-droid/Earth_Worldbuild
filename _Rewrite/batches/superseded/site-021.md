=== person/ludwig-ii
title: Ludwig II
type: person
date: 1845 – 1886
themes: art, kinship
src: 18f73db1f6ef
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ludwig II (1845 to 1886), sometimes called the "Fairy Tale King," was King of Bavaria from 1864 until his death, remembered for building extravagant romantic castles and for his patronage of the composer Richard Wagner.
--- fact 1
He became an early and enthusiastic patron of Richard Wagner, paying off the composer's debts and funding productions, though political opposition to Wagner's influence at court eventually forced the composer to leave Munich.
--- fact 2
He commissioned several elaborate castles, most famously Neuschwanstein, begun in 1869 and inspired partly by Wagner's operas; the castles were funded from his personal fortune rather than the Bavarian state treasury but still left him deeply in debt.
--- fact 3
Bavaria's defeat alongside Austria in the 1866 Austro-Prussian War, and its incorporation into the German Empire in 1871, steadily reduced his practical political authority even as he retained the throne.
--- fact 4
In 1886 a government commission, citing his mounting debts and reclusive behavior, had him declared mentally unfit to rule; he died under unclear circumstances in Lake Starnberg the day after being confined, an event still debated as accident, suicide, or possible foul play.
--- context
Ludwig II ruled Bavaria in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], overshadowed by Prussia's rise and German unification. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ludwig's declaration of mental incapacity came from a government that had strong political and financial reasons to remove him, and no independent medical examination confirmed the diagnosis before his death; the castles that nearly bankrupted him now draw more tourist revenue to Bavaria each year than they ever cost to build.
--- question 1
Was Ludwig II's 1886 removal a legitimate response to incapacity, or a political maneuver by ministers and relatives seeking control of Bavaria's government?

=== person/lumiere-brothers
title: Lumière Brothers
type: person
date: 1862 – 1954
themes: art, technology
src: c65df22d3c1b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Auguste Lumière (1862 to 1954) and Louis Lumière (1864 to 1948) were French manufacturers of photographic plates who patented the Cinématographe in 1895 and staged the first paying public screening of projected motion pictures. The dates here run from Auguste's birth to his death.
--- fact 1
The brothers were born in Besançon and grew up in Lyon, where their father Antoine ran a portrait studio and later a factory making dry photographic plates.
--- fact 2
After their father saw a demonstration of Edison's single-viewer Kinetoscope in 1894, they set out to build a device that could project moving images to a seated audience. Louis is generally credited with the key mechanical insight, adapting the sewing machine's intermittent claw movement to advance the film.
--- fact 3
They patented the Cinématographe in February 1895, one device that could shoot, print and project. Their first paying public screening was on 28 December 1895 in the basement of the Grand Café on the Boulevard des Capucines in Paris: ten short films for about thirty-five people.
--- fact 4
Their "actuality" films were usually a single static, unedited shot of under a minute. L'Arroseur arrosé is a rare staged gag, effectively cinema's first fiction film. Key works include Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory (1895) and The Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat (1896).
--- fact 5
They sent camera operators around the world, spreading projected cinema to India, Japan, Australia and the Americas within a year or two.
--- fact 6
Within two years they largely withdrew from film production. In 1907 they introduced the Autochrome, an early commercially successful colour photography process.
--- context
The Lumières worked in Lyon and Paris, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. [[person/georges-melies|Georges Méliès]] attended their first screening. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the decisive step was social rather than mechanical: a device that let a seated crowd watch the same moving image at once is story-glue in miniature, strangers sharing a spectacle in a dark room for a fee. The brothers treated the medium as a product and stepped back from it within two years, while a magician they had refused a camera made it an art. The attribution of the key mechanical idea to Louis alone is common but hard to check when two brothers shared credit.
--- question 1
Which of the two brothers contributed what to the design of the Cinématographe?

=== person/malinche
title: Malinche
type: person
date: c. 1500 – 1529
themes: language, war, kinship
src: 106aeff88fff
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Malinche (Malintzin, called Doña Marina by the Spanish; about 1500 to about 1529) was a Nahua woman who served as interpreter and intermediary for Hernán Cortés during the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire in 1519 to 1521. Her dates are uncertain.
--- fact 1
Malinche's birth is placed around 1500 and her death between about 1527 and 1529; both are estimates.
--- fact 2
The chronicler Bernal Díaz del Castillo, writing decades later, says she was born to a noble Nahua family near Coatzacoalcos and passed into slavery as a child. This account is traditional and cannot be checked.
--- fact 3
In 1519 she was among women presented to Cortés by people of Tabasco. She spoke Nahuatl and a Maya language and learned Spanish.
--- fact 4
She interpreted for Cortés, at first with Gerónimo de Aguilar, including at the meetings with Moctezuma II at Tenochtitlan in 1519.
--- fact 5
She bore Cortés a son, Martín, about 1522, and later married the Spanish soldier Juan Jaramillo.
--- fact 6
In Mexican culture she has been seen as traitor, victim and symbolic mother of a mixed nation.
--- context
Malinche's role sits inside the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]], which ended the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] and its capital [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a young woman who had herself been given as property became the channel that joined several sound-codes, and that what she said is known almost entirely through others, mainly Spanish chroniclers. Later verdicts on her say more about what later nations wanted from a story-glue about their origins than about her choices. A specialist could argue that how much choice she had cannot be recovered, so any judgment of her as agent or victim goes beyond the evidence.
--- question 1
What can indigenous pictorial sources add to the Spanish accounts of her role?

--- summary
Marie Curie (1867 to 1934) was a Polish-born physicist and chemist who pioneered research on radioactivity and is the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.
--- fact 1
She shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel.

--- fact 4

=== person/mary-cassatt
title: Mary Cassatt
type: person
date: 1844 – 1926
themes: art, kinship
src: 48cfc164a3b5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mary Cassatt (22 May 1844 to 14 June 1926) was an American painter who spent most of her adult life in France and was the only American to exhibit with the French Impressionists. She is celebrated for tender, unsentimental images of women and children.
--- fact 1
Cassatt was born in Allegheny City, now part of Pittsburgh, into a wealthy family. Against her father's wishes she studied at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts from 1860, then left for Paris in 1866.
--- fact 2
Women could not enter the École des Beaux-Arts, so she studied privately and copied Old Masters in the Louvre. Frustrated with the Salon's conservative taste, she accepted Degas's invitation in 1877 to exhibit with the Impressionists, and showed with them in 1879, 1880, 1881 and 1886.
--- fact 3
Her parents and sister moved to Paris to live with her, and her family became her favourite models.
--- fact 4
In 1890 an exhibition of Japanese prints in Paris inspired a set of ten colour prints combining drypoint and aquatint. She took from them flat colour, strong outlines and cropped compositions.
--- fact 5
In 1893 she painted the mural Modern Woman for the Woman's Building at the Chicago World's Columbian Exposition; it has been lost.
--- fact 6
She advised Louisine Havemeyer and other Americans on buying Old Masters and Impressionists, many now in the Metropolitan Museum. She supported the women's suffrage movement.
--- fact 7
Failing eyesight forced her to stop working about 1914. Key works include Little Girl in a Blue Armchair (1878), The Child's Bath (1893) and The Boating Party (1893 to 1894).
--- context
Cassatt worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] as an American in the Impressionist circle of [[person/claude-monet|Claude Monet]], drew on Japanese prints, including those of [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]], and supported [[event/women-s-suffrage-movements|Women's Suffrage Movements]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the school she joined kept women out of its formal institution, so she worked through private study and a friend's invitation, the informal networks on which many women's careers depended. Her subject, the daily work of care within the kin-lattice, had rarely been thought worth a large canvas. Her advice to collectors also decided where the movement's pictures ended up: in American museums, a redistribution of art by taste and money as much as by merit.
--- question 1
How did her advice to collectors shape which Impressionist works entered American collections?

=== person/matthias-corvinus
title: Matthias Corvinus
type: person
date: 1443 – 1490
themes: war, art, science
src: b9dc9f45f7d3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Matthias Corvinus (1443 to 1490) was King of Hungary from 1458 to 1490, a Renaissance patron-king who built one of Europe's finest libraries and modernized his kingdom's army and administration.
--- fact 1
He was elected king at age fourteen after his father, the military commander John Hunyadi, and older brother had died, with his mother's political maneuvering and a large bribe to the nobility securing the throne.
--- fact 2
He built the Black Army, a mostly mercenary standing force notable for the period, which he used to campaign against the Ottoman Empire, Bohemia, and the Holy Roman Empire, briefly capturing Vienna in 1485.
--- fact 3
He assembled the Bibliotheca Corviniana, one of the largest Renaissance libraries in Europe outside Italy, employing scribes and scholars to copy classical and contemporary texts; much of it was later dispersed or destroyed after the Ottoman conquest of Hungary.
--- fact 4
He centralized royal justice and taxation, reducing the independent power of the Hungarian nobility, though his reforms were substantially reversed by weaker successors after his death.
--- context
Matthias Corvinus ruled Hungary in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], balancing conflicts with the Ottoman Empire and the Holy Roman Empire. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Matthias's centralizing reforms depended heavily on his own standing army and personal authority rather than durable institutions, so much of his state-building unraveled within a generation of his death, a pattern common to strong individual rulers who do not also build structures that outlast them.
--- question 1
How much of the Bibliotheca Corviniana's contents reflected Matthias's personal intellectual interests versus the conventional prestige-collecting expected of a Renaissance monarch?

=== person/michelangelo
title: Michelangelo
type: person
date: 1475 – 1564
themes: art, religion
src: 542c7a29afa3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Michelangelo Buonarroti (6 March 1475 to 18 February 1564) was an Italian sculptor, painter, architect and poet. His Pietà, David and Sistine Chapel paintings defined the High Renaissance, and he was the first Western artist to have a biography published in his lifetime.
--- fact 1
Michelangelo was born at Caprese in Tuscany and grew up in Florence. At 13 he was apprenticed to Domenico Ghirlandaio, then moved to the sculpture garden of Lorenzo de' Medici, where he studied ancient statues.
--- fact 2
The Pietà (1498 to 1499) made his name in Rome. He carved the David in Florence (1501 to 1504).
--- fact 3
In 1505 Pope Julius II called him to Rome to design a colossal tomb, then diverted him to paint the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508 to 1512). The unfinished tomb haunted him for decades.
--- fact 4
In 1534 he settled permanently in Rome and painted The Last Judgment (1536 to 1541). In 1546 he became chief architect of St Peter's Basilica, and his dome became a model for later domes.
--- fact 5
He wrote hundreds of poems, many addressed to Tommaso de' Cavalieri and Vittoria Colonna. He died in Rome, and his body was secretly taken to Florence for burial in Santa Croce.
--- fact 6
He carved directly, often without full-size models, and studied anatomy through dissection. A cleaning of the Sistine ceiling in the 1980s and 1990s revealed vivid, even acidic colours.
--- fact 7
Vasari's Lives (1550) ended with him, and Ascanio Condivi published a biography in 1553. He studied the frescoes of Giotto.
--- context
Michelangelo worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]], in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. He studied [[person/giotto|Giotto]]'s frescoes. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a man who held that sculpture frees a figure already inside the stone, and who spent decades in the service of popes, the chief patrons of that age's meaning-engine. The heroic nude body became a standard image for sacred and civic subjects alike, and the David turned an object into a story-glue for a city. A biography in his lifetime is an early sign of the artist as a named public figure. The early accounts by Vasari and Condivi were written by admirers, so their portrait of his character is not neutral.
--- question 1
How much of the standard portrait of Michelangelo comes from admiring biographers rather than from his own letters and poems?

=== person/mimar-sinan
title: Mimar Sinan
type: person
date: c. 1489 – 1588
themes: art, technology, religion
src: dd461ef5f258
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mimar Sinan (about 1489 to 1588) was chief royal architect to three Ottoman sultans for nearly fifty years. He is credited with several hundred structures, and his Selimiye Mosque at Edirne is regarded as the peak of classical Ottoman architecture.
--- fact 1
Sinan was born about 1489 or 1490 in Ağırnas, near Kayseri in central Anatolia, into a Christian family. Sources disagree on whether it was Greek or Armenian.
--- fact 2
He was conscripted through the devşirme levy into the Ottoman elite corps, converted to Islam and trained as a Janissary. He rose through military engineering, building bridges and fortifications on campaign for Suleiman the Magnificent.
--- fact 3
Suleiman appointed him chief royal architect in 1538, a post he held into his late nineties. He died in Istanbul in 1588.
--- fact 4
His main buildings are the Şehzade Mosque (1543 to 1548), the Süleymaniye Mosque (1550 to 1557) and the Selimiye Mosque (1568 to 1574). He described them as his apprenticeship, journeyman work and masterpiece.
--- fact 5
He worked to build domes wider and more daring than Hagia Sophia, a Byzantine cathedral converted to a mosque after 1453, using semi-domes, buttressing piers and pendentives. He also built aqueducts and bridges, some still in use.
--- fact 6
He trained a generation of students. Turkey's largest architecture prize carries his name, and the Selimiye Mosque was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2011.
--- context
Sinan served the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] in [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], a century after the [[event/fall-of-constantinople|Fall of Constantinople]] made Hagia Sophia his measure. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a boy taken from a Christian village by a levy who rose to design an empire's mosques, so that the state's demand for people became, in his case, an engine of monumental building. The dome is a claim in masonry against an older building, and his three-stage account of apprenticeship, journeyman work and masterpiece describes a tool-lineage in which each structure teaches the next. That account is his own narrative, and later reverence has added to it.
--- question 1
How many of the several hundred structures credited to Sinan did he personally design?
--- question 2
Which of his own and later accounts of his career and birth can be documented?

=== person/mirabai
title: Mirabai
type: person
date: c. 1498 – 1546
themes: religion, art, kinship
src: 64f0c39dcb70
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mirabai (traditionally about 1498 to about 1546) was a Rajput princess from Rajasthan and one of the best-known poet-saints of the Bhakti movement, devoted to Krishna. Almost nothing about her life is documented, and the surviving poems were written down long after her time.
--- fact 1
By tradition Mirabai was born about 1498 at Kudki in Rajasthan, into the Rathore Rajput family of Merta, and died about 1546 or 1547. Both dates come from later tradition.
--- fact 2
Tradition says she married Bhoj Raj, heir of Mewar, in 1516, and that he died about 1521 of battle wounds.
--- fact 3
Her poems (padas) are in Rajasthani and related Hindi, and address Krishna in the language of love and separation.
--- fact 4
Tradition credits her with a very large number of poems. Scholars think only a few hundred may be authentic, and only two are known from manuscripts older than the early eighteenth century.
--- fact 5
Stories that her in-laws tried to poison her, and that she vanished into an image of Krishna, are hagiographic legends. The earliest records, from the seventeenth century, do not mention her marriage or in-laws.
--- context
Mirabai lived in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], as the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] replaced the [[culture/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]] in northern India in 1526. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a figure who left almost no contemporary trace has become one of the most widely sung voices of her tradition. The songs and legends describe a woman who places devotion to a god above the duties of marriage and household, a claim about the kin-lattice that singers have restated for five centuries. A specialist could argue that a single historical Mirabai may never be recoverable, which would weaken any reading of the poems as her biography.
--- question 1
Which poems attributed to her, if any, can be dated to the sixteenth century?
--- question 2
How far do the persecution stories reflect later debates over women's devotion and family duty?

=== person/montezuma-ii
title: Montezuma II
type: person
date: c. 1466 – 1520
themes: religion, war, kinship
src: 6ddd2959d15c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Montezuma II (Moctezuma; about 1466 to 1520) was the ninth ruler (tlatoani) of the Aztec Empire, reigning at its greatest territorial extent until the arrival of Hernan Cortes and the Spanish conquest.
--- fact 1
He extended Aztec control through further conquests in Oaxaca and along the Gulf coast, and reorganized the empire's tribute and administrative systems.
--- fact 2
Cortes and a small Spanish force reached the capital Tenochtitlan in November 1519; Montezuma received them and was subsequently held as a hostage within his own palace.
--- fact 3
Accounts of his final months differ: Spanish sources say he tried to calm his people during the 1520 Toxcatl massacre and was killed by a stone thrown by his own subjects, while some later Aztec-descended chroniclers say the Spanish killed him directly.
--- fact 4
His death in June 1520 came shortly before the Spanish were driven from the city in the "Noche Triste"; his brother Cuitlahuac and then his nephew Cuauhtemoc took up the resistance that ended with Tenochtitlan's fall in 1521.
--- context
Montezuma II ruled the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], and his capture began the chain of events that ended Aztec independence. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that nearly every detailed account of Montezuma's final year was written by the side that benefited from portraying him as passive or already discredited among his own people. The surviving record cannot fully separate the man from the story his conquerors, and his own later-defeated dynasty, each had reason to tell.
--- question 1
Did Montezuma initially treat the Spanish as returning gods, as later legend holds, or as a more ordinary diplomatic and military problem?

=== person/muhammad-ali-of-egypt
title: Muhammad Ali of Egypt
type: person
date: 1805
themes: war, trade, technology
src: 96d418d2d70a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Muhammad Ali, an Ottoman officer of Albanian family, became governor of Egypt in 1805, destroyed the old Mamluk elite, built a conscript army and state-run economy, and founded the dynasty that ruled Egypt until 1952.
--- fact 1
Muhammad Ali was born in Kavala, in what is now Greece, to an Albanian family from the Korçë region. His father was a tobacco merchant and an Ottoman military commander.
--- fact 2
He rose to power after the French withdrawal from Egypt in 1801. In 1805 prominent Egyptians, including the religious scholars, demanded his appointment as governor (wali), and the Ottomans accepted.
--- fact 3
In 1811 he invited the leading Mamluks to a celebration at the Cairo Citadel, where his forces ambushed and killed them. More Mamluks were then hunted down in Cairo and elsewhere, for a total of perhaps 3,000.
--- fact 4
He introduced long-staple cotton, built an army of conscripts trained by European officers, and set up a state-run industrial economy.
--- fact 5
He founded the dynasty that ruled Egypt until 1952.
--- context
Muhammad Ali governed Egypt as a nominal servant of the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] while pursuing reforms of his own, at the same time as the [[event/tanzimat-reforms|Tanzimat Reforms]] in Istanbul. His rule preceded the building of the [[event/suez-canal|Suez Canal]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a reformer who began by killing a rival elite at a banquet, and who built a modern state on conscription and compulsory production. The order he made was real and lasting, and the cost of it was carried by those who were drafted and those who were killed.
--- question 1
How much of Egypt's later modernization can be credited to Muhammad Ali's programme, and at what cost to the rural population?
--- question 2
How many people were killed in the wave of violence that followed the Citadel massacre?

--- summary
Murasaki Shikibu (about 973 to about 1014) was a lady-in-waiting at the Japanese imperial court in the Heian period. She wrote The Tale of Genji, a 54-chapter novel of court life that is widely called the world's first novel.

"Murasaki Shikibu" is a later nickname. Her personal name is unknown; the nickname combines a character from her novel with her father's court rank.
--- fact 2
She was born about 973 into a minor branch of the Fujiwara clan and learned the Chinese classics, normally reserved for men, by absorbing lessons given to her brother.
--- fact 3
She married a much older provincial governor about 998 and was widowed within a few years. About 1005 or 1006 she entered court service as a lady-in-waiting to Empress Shōshi.
--- fact 4
The Tale of Genji follows a fictional prince and, in its later chapters, his descendants. It was probably begun before she came to court.
--- fact 5
She wrote almost entirely in hiragana, the script associated with women's writing at court, and set hundreds of waka poems into the story as the characters' own compositions.
--- fact 6
She also kept The Diary of Lady Murasaki, which records court life and rivalries. She is thought to have died about 1014; the date, like most of her biography, is uncertain.
--- fact 7
The earliest surviving illustrated version, the 12th-century Genji Monogatari Emaki, is a Japanese National Treasure. She appears on the 2000 yen banknote.
--- context
Murasaki wrote at the Heian court in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the earliest long prose fiction on record comes from a woman writing in the script her society assigned to women, while the mark-code of official record, Chinese characters, belonged to men. The formal script carried authority; the informal one carried private feeling. The novel circulated as copied and illustrated scrolls, a case of memory-outsourcing used for interior life rather than law or scripture. Whether it is the first novel depends on how the term is defined, and her biography rests mostly on her own diary and scattered court records, so the reading should be held loosely.
--- question 1
How much of The Tale of Genji was written before she entered court service?

--- summary
Nefertiti (about 1370 to about 1330 BCE) was the Great Royal Wife of the Egyptian king Akhenaten and is shown with unusual prominence in the art of his reign. Her birth, death and burial are unknown.
--- fact 1
Nefertiti married Akhenaten (then Amenhotep IV) early in his reign, about 1353 BCE.
--- fact 2
The couple promoted the worship of the sun disk Aten and moved the royal capital to a new city, Akhetaten (modern Amarna).
--- fact 3
Reliefs and statues show Nefertiti at a scale and in roles usually reserved for the king, including taking part in rites for the Aten.
--- fact 4
They had six daughters, including Meritaten and Ankhesenamun, who later married Tutankhamun.
--- fact 5
She disappears from the record after about year 12 to 14 of the reign. Whether she died, changed title, or ruled as co-regent under the name Neferneferuaten is debated.
--- fact 6
A painted limestone bust found in 1912 in the workshop of the sculptor Thutmose at Amarna is held in Berlin; Egypt has requested its return.
--- fact 7
Later king lists omitted Akhenaten and his immediate successors.
--- context
Nefertiti's life is tied to [[person/akhenaten|Akhenaten]], among the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] of the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one sculpture made her among the best-known faces of antiquity, yet the surviving record covers a short period that later rulers tried to erase from the king lists. Her size in the reliefs works as a status-signal, and it is unclear whether it records real power or the couple's use of the royal family as a pair of divine images. The reading would weaken if evidence showed such scale was a convention for queens of the period.

=== person/nezahualcoyotl
title: Nezahualcoyotl
type: person
date: 1402 – 1472
themes: art, war, religion
src: 01e4dce3621d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Nezahualcoyotl (1402 to 1472) was the tlatoani, or ruler, of the Acolhua city-state of Texcoco in the Valley of Mexico and a founder of the Triple Alliance with Tenochtitlan and Tlacopan. He is remembered as a lawgiver, builder and poet, though much of what is said about him comes from later writers.
--- fact 1
Nezahualcoyotl was born in 1402 in Texcoco, the son of the ruler Ixtlilxochitl I and a Mexica princess. After the Tepanec state of Azcapotzalco conquered Texcoco and killed his father, he lived in exile.
--- fact 2
With Tenochtitlan and Tlacopan he fought Azcapotzalco. Its defeat, traditionally dated about 1428, created the Triple Alliance. He ruled Texcoco from about 1431 until 1472, when his son Nezahualpilli succeeded him.
--- fact 3
Later sources credit him with a law code and public works, including a dike protecting Tenochtitlan from flooding and the gardens at Tetzcotzingo.
--- fact 4
About 34 poems are attributed to him in sixteenth-century manuscripts. Whether he composed them is debated, since "his song" can mean a song about him.
--- fact 5
Accounts that he opposed human sacrifice and favored a single creator deity come largely from his descendant Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxochitl, writing in the early seventeenth century, and are treated with caution.
--- context
Nezahualcoyotl ruled in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. The Triple Alliance he helped found grew into the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]], centered on [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler whose rise rested on a military alliance is remembered chiefly through poems on the brevity of life. This verse, made in sound-code, survived the Conquest only because writers copied it into mark-code in the sixteenth century. A specialist could argue that both the poems and the portrait of him as a tolerant sage reflect the aims of later writers, which would weaken any reading of him as an exceptional thinker.
--- question 1
Which of the poems attributed to him did he compose himself?
--- question 2
How much of the later picture of his laws and beliefs comes from his descendants?

observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nur Jahan (1577 to 1645) was a Mughal empress and the wife of the emperor Jahangir, who held great political power at court from about 1611 to 1627.
--- fact 1
Nur Jahan was born Mehr-un-Nisa in 1577, the daughter of Mirza Ghiyas Beg, a Persian who served the Mughal court.
--- fact 2
Her first husband, Sher Afghan, died in 1607. She married Jahangir in 1611 and was given the title Nur Mahal, and in 1616 Nur Jahan, "Light of the World."
--- fact 3
As Jahangir's health declined she took a growing share of government. Coins were struck in her name, and imperial orders carried her seal.
--- fact 4
Her father became chief minister and her brother Asaf Khan a leading noble, so her family held key posts.
--- fact 5
She commissioned her father's tomb at Agra (1622 to 1628), an early Mughal building faced in marble with inlaid stone.
--- fact 6
In 1626 the general Mahabat Khan seized Jahangir, and she took part in securing his release.
--- fact 7
After Jahangir died in 1627 her brother backed Shah Jahan, who became emperor. She lived in retirement in Lahore until her death in 1645.
--- context
Nur Jahan's court was the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. Her niece Mumtaz Mahal is commemorated by the [[place/taj-mahal|Taj Mahal]]. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a woman held power through the kin-lattice, by marriage into the ruling house and posts for her father and brother, rather than through an office in her own name. Coins and a seal show the state using her name as a status-signal, while the chroniclers who describe her wrote from within the court. The reading would weaken if evidence showed that her reported power was overstated by writers seeking to explain Jahangir's rule by her influence.

The Observer notes that Pedro II was deposed not despite his support for abolition but partly because of it: the same landowning class that had backed the monarchy for decades turned against it once slavery, the institution their wealth depended on, was ended. === person/pericles
title: Pericles
type: person
date: c. 495 – 429 BCE
themes: art, war, kinship
src: 584250b53edf
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pericles (about 495 to 429 BCE) was the leading Athenian statesman for over three decades in the mid-5th century BCE, a period later called the Golden Age of Athens for its building programs, drama, and democratic institutions.
--- fact 1
He rose to prominence in Athenian democracy through the office of strategos (general), to which he was elected roughly thirty times, using his influence over the Assembly rather than any formal single office to shape policy.
--- fact 2
He directed the building program on the Acropolis, including the Parthenon, funded partly by treasury funds originally collected from the Delian League, an alliance of Greek states organized against Persia.
--- fact 3
He extended pay for jury service and other public roles to poorer citizens, broadening practical participation in Athenian democracy beyond the wealthy.
--- fact 4
He championed the aggressive strategy that led Athens into the Peloponnesian War against Sparta in 431 BCE, and died of plague in 429 BCE during the war's early years, before its outcome was decided.
--- context
Pericles led Athens, part of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]], in the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that most surviving praise of Pericles, including his famous funeral oration on Athenian democracy recorded by Thucydides, comes from a historian writing after Pericles' death and after the war he started went badly; the speech may reflect Thucydides' own idealized case for what Athens should have been as much as anything Pericles actually said.
--- question 1
How much of the funeral oration attributed to Pericles by Thucydides reflects his actual words versus the historian's own composition?
