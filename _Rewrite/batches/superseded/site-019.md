=== person/gitarja
title: Gitarja
type: person
date: c. 1328 – 1350
themes: war, trade, religion
src: 3c4db912727f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tribhuwana Wijayatunggadewi, known as Gitarja, was the third monarch of the Majapahit empire, reigning in Java from 1328 to 1350; her birth and death years are not recorded.
--- fact 1
She became queen after the assassination of her half-brother Jayanegara in 1328, ruling in the name of her mother, Gayatri Rajapatni, who had formally retired to a Buddhist convent.
--- fact 2
Her chief minister, Gajah Mada, is credited with a famous oath to abstain from certain pleasures until he had brought the surrounding archipelago under Majapahit's authority.
--- fact 3
Under her reign Majapahit forces conquered Bali in 1343, defeating the kingdoms of Pejeng and Bedahulu, a major step in the empire's expansion across the Indonesian archipelago.
--- fact 4
She stepped down in 1350 after her mother's death and was succeeded by her son, Hayam Wuruk, under whom Majapahit reached its greatest territorial extent.
--- context
Gitarja ruled [[culture/majapahit|Majapahit]] in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and her minister's campaigns set up the empire's height under her son. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Gitarja's reign is remembered mainly through the ambitions of her minister rather than any directly recorded decision of her own, a common pattern for royal women whose authority depended on men who did the fighting and the writing. Whether she directed Gajah Mada's campaigns or simply did not stop them cannot be recovered from the surviving sources.
--- question 1
How much of Majapahit's expansion under Gajah Mada reflects Gitarja's own policy rather than his independent initiative?

=== person/gorgo
title: Gorgo
type: person
date: c. 518 – 480 BCE
themes: war, kinship
src: 56a52e28ef85
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Gorgo (born about 518 BCE, last attested around 480 BCE) was queen of Sparta as the wife of King Leonidas I, remembered by the historian Herodotus as unusually sharp-witted and politically perceptive for a woman in his account of Greek affairs.
--- fact 1
She was the daughter of the Spartan king Cleomenes I and, according to Herodotus, advised him as a child not to trust a bribe offered by an Ionian envoy seeking Spartan military aid.
--- fact 2
Herodotus credits her with detecting a warning about the Persian king Xerxes's invasion plans hidden on a wax-covered writing tablet sent by the exiled Spartan king Demaratus, when other Spartans could not work out its message.
--- fact 3
She was the wife of Leonidas I, who died leading the defense of Thermopylae against the Persian invasion in 480 BCE, the same year in which she is last clearly attested in the sources.
--- fact 4
Nearly everything known of her comes from Herodotus's Histories, written decades after her lifetime, and no independent Spartan record of her actions survives.
--- context
Gorgo was queen of Sparta, part of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]], in the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Herodotus, writing for a Greek audience that saw Spartan women as unusually outspoken compared to other Greek women, may have shaped Gorgo's anecdotes to fit that reputation as much as to record specific events; the stories flatter both her personal intelligence and a wider Greek stereotype about Spartan women at the same time.
--- question 1
How much of Gorgo's characterization in Herodotus reflects specific memory of her versus a general Greek stereotype about outspoken Spartan women?

=== person/gustav-klimt
title: Gustav Klimt
type: person
date: 1862 – 1918
themes: art
src: f33e1f2be85a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Gustav Klimt (14 July 1862 to 6 February 1918) was an Austrian painter and the leader of the Vienna Secession. His golden, patterned paintings of women and love, above all The Kiss, are icons of turn-of-the-century Vienna.
--- fact 1
Klimt was born at Baumgarten near Vienna, the second of seven children of a gold engraver. He won a scholarship to the Vienna School of Arts and Crafts in 1876 and, with his brother Ernst and Franz Matsch, painted murals and ceilings for the Burgtheater and the Kunsthistorisches Museum.
--- fact 2
In 1897 he became the first president of the Vienna Secession, a group breaking from the official academy with the motto "To every age its art, to art its freedom".
--- fact 3
His ceiling paintings for the University of Vienna, showing Philosophy, Medicine and Jurisprudence, caused a scandal over nudity and pessimism. He withdrew from the commission, and the paintings were destroyed in a fire in 1945.
--- fact 4
He never married, had a lifelong companionship with the designer Emilie Flöge and fathered several children with models. He supported younger artists such as Egon Schiele and Oskar Kokoschka. He died in Vienna after a stroke.
--- fact 5
In his "golden period" (about 1899 to 1910) he applied real gold and silver leaf, drawing on Byzantine mosaics he saw in Ravenna in 1903, and on Japanese and Egyptian art. Key works include The Kiss (1907 to 1908) and the Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I (1907).
--- fact 6
Many of his works were owned by Jewish collectors and looted by the Nazis. The return of the Adele Bloch-Bauer portrait to Maria Altmann made headlines worldwide. Ronald Lauder bought it in 2006 for a reported $135 million.
--- fact 7
In 2023 Lady with a Fan sold for £85.3 million, a record for a painting at auction in Europe.
--- context
Klimt worked in Vienna, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and died in the [[event/1918-influenza-pandemic|1918 Influenza Pandemic]]. Works he painted were later looted in the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a movement whose motto framed art as a claim on institutions, and a painter who left a public commission when it drew scandal, so that a university, a meaning-engine of knowledge, refused images of its own faculties. The gold leaf and the auction records show the status-signal of the object at its extreme. Provenance adds a second biography: paintings taken from Jewish owners by the Nazis and later the subject of restitution cases carry sanctioned harm as part of their history.
--- question 1
How many Klimt works taken from Jewish owners remain unrestituted?

observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hammurabi was the king of Babylon from about 1792 to 1750 BCE, who united much of Mesopotamia and is known for a famous inscribed law code.
--- fact 1
The Code of Hammurabi, carved on a stone stele about 1754 BCE, lists 282 laws.
--- fact 2
It sets penalties that vary by social class and includes the principle of proportionate punishment.
--- fact 3
The stele was displayed publicly, making the law visible.
--- fact 4
The original is in the Louvre in Paris.
--- context
Hammurabi ruled in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], inheriting the writing tradition begun by the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]].
--- observer
The Observer sees a state converting custom into text. Putting law on stone changed what authority could claim: a rule that everyone can read, at least in principle, can bind the ruler as well as the ruled.
--- question 1
How closely did courts follow the code?

=== person/harald-hardrada
title: Harald Hardrada
type: person
date: c. 1015 – 1066
themes: war, trade
src: 8b0d2915ca07
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Harald Hardrada (about 1015 to 1066) was King of Norway from 1046 to 1066, a warrior-king whose career took him from exile through mercenary service in Byzantium to the throne, and whose death at Stamford Bridge is often used to mark the end of the Viking Age.
--- fact 1
After his half-brother King Olaf II was killed in battle in 1030, the teenage Harald fled Norway and eventually served as a commander in the Byzantine Empire's elite Varangian Guard, campaigning in the Mediterranean and Middle East.
--- fact 2
He returned to Scandinavia around 1045 with substantial wealth, and secured a share of the Norwegian throne, becoming sole king after his co-ruler's death in 1046.
--- fact 3
He fought years of inconclusive war against Denmark over rival claims, finally making peace in 1064 without gaining the Danish throne he sought.
--- fact 4
He invaded England in September 1066 to press a rival claim to its throne, but was defeated and killed at the Battle of Stamford Bridge by King Harold Godwinson, days before Godwinson had to march south to face William of Normandy's invasion at Hastings.
--- context
Harald Hardrada led the [[culture/norse|Norse]] as King of Norway, in [[region/europe|Europe]], during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Harald's failed invasion, remembered mainly as a footnote to the Norman Conquest, forced Harold Godwinson's army into an exhausting march across England that arguably weakened it before Hastings; a defeat at Stamford Bridge may have shaped the more famous battle that followed it more than its traditional label as a mere prelude suggests.
--- question 1
How much did the English army's losses and exhaustion at Stamford Bridge actually affect its performance three weeks later at Hastings?

=== person/harriet-tubman
title: Harriet Tubman
type: person
date: c. 1822 – 1913
themes: war, kinship
src: cc9266f55bfe
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Harriet Tubman (about 1822 to 1913) was an American abolitionist who escaped slavery in Maryland in 1849 and then guided others to freedom on the Underground Railroad. She later served the Union Army in the Civil War.
--- fact 1
Tubman was born Araminta Ross into slavery in Dorchester County, Maryland; her birth year is uncertain, with estimates from about 1820 to 1822.
--- fact 2
As a teenager she suffered a severe head injury when an overseer threw a metal weight, and had seizures and headaches for the rest of her life.
--- fact 3
She escaped to Pennsylvania in 1849 and afterward made about 13 trips back to Maryland, guiding roughly 70 people to freedom, according to her biographers.
--- fact 4
During the Civil War she served the Union Army as a cook, nurse, scout and spy. In June 1863 she helped guide the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which freed more than 700 enslaved people.
--- fact 5
After the war she lived in Auburn, New York, where she cared for her family and for elderly people, and she worked for women's suffrage.
--- fact 6
She died in Auburn on 10 March 1913.
--- context
Tubman opposed slavery in the United States, part of the wider history of the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]], and served in the [[event/american-civil-war|American Civil War]] in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See also [[event/women-s-suffrage-movements|Women's Suffrage Movements]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a rescue network that depended on secrecy left little paper, so the record of Tubman's trips rests on her own recollections as told to others, since she could not write. Her escape became a repeated and organized risk carried out through families and congregations, a kin-lattice used as a channel for hidden information. The reading of the counts of trips and people as estimates would weaken if newly found records fixed them.
--- question 1
How well do surviving records support the counts of her rescue trips?

Her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari is a major monument.
--- fact 4
Many of her images were later removed or defaced, possibly after her death.

Hatshepsut is a figure of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]. Her reign shows how kingship in the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] could accommodate a woman ruler.
--- observer
The Observer notes how she was shown with the regalia and beard of a male king. The kingship, a role bound to cosmic order, mattered more than the sex of its holder, until later authorities erased her image.

=== person/henry-ossawa-tanner
title: Henry Ossawa Tanner
type: person
date: 1859 – 1937
themes: art, religion
src: 75cca669551a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Henry Ossawa Tanner (21 June 1859 to 25 May 1937) was an American painter, the first African American artist to win international fame. Frustrated by racism at home, he settled in Paris in 1891 and built his career there, painting dignified genre scenes and luminous biblical subjects.
--- fact 1
Tanner was born in Pittsburgh, the eldest of nine children. His father, Benjamin Tucker Tanner, became a bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and his mother, Sarah, had escaped slavery via the Underground Railroad. At 13 he decided to become an artist.
--- fact 2
In 1879 he enrolled at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts and studied with Thomas Eakins. He was the only Black student and faced harassment. He left for Europe in 1891 and studied at the Académie Julian in Paris.
--- fact 3
On a return visit in 1893 he painted The Banjo Lesson and The Thankful Poor, genre scenes of Black American life that challenged racist stereotypes.
--- fact 4
Back in France he turned to biblical subjects. The Resurrection of Lazarus won a medal at the 1897 Salon, and the French government bought it. His patron Rodman Wanamaker paid for trips to Egypt and Palestine.
--- fact 5
He married the singer Jessie Olssen in 1899. He was made a Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur in 1923 and died in Paris.
--- fact 6
He preferred a limited palette of deep blues and blue-greens with glowing gold highlights, built up in layers of glaze.
--- fact 7
He advised younger painters such as William Edouard Scott and Hale Woodruff, and was a hero to the artists of the Harlem Renaissance. In 1996 his Sand Dunes at Sunset, Atlantic City became the first painting by an African American artist in the White House collection.
--- context
Tanner worked mostly in France, in [[region/europe|Europe]], in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], as an American artist. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter who left a country whose art institutions harassed him and built a career in another, where the salon judged the paintings. His two bodies of work, genre scenes of Black life and biblical scenes using ordinary people as models, both aimed at truth over caricature, and both answered a stereotype without naming it. That the first painting by an African American artist entered the White House collection only in 1996 shows how slowly an institution's memory catches up with a reputation.
--- question 1
How did his French reception differ from what he would have faced in the United States?

=== person/hieronymus-bosch
title: Hieronymus Bosch
type: person
date: c. 1450 – 1516
themes: art, religion
src: 53508eba8a3f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Hieronymus Bosch (about 1450 to 1516) was a painter from 's-Hertogenbosch in the Duchy of Brabant who filled his panels with hybrid monsters and crowds of tiny figures acting out human sin and folly. Only about 25 paintings are firmly attributed to him.
--- fact 1
Bosch was born Jheronimus van Aken about 1450 in 's-Hertogenbosch. His grandfather, father and uncles were painters, and he later signed his works "Jheronimus Bosch" after the town.
--- fact 2
About 1481 he married Aleid van de Meervenne, of a wealthy family. In 1488 he became a sworn member of the Illustrious Brotherhood of Our Blessed Lady, and he died in 1516.
--- fact 3
He seems to have spent almost his whole life in 's-Hertogenbosch, yet his fame spread. Philip the Handsome, Duke of Burgundy, commissioned a Last Judgment from him in 1504.
--- fact 4
He painted in oil on oak panels, often as triptychs. Key works include The Garden of Earthly Delights (about 1490 to 1510), The Ship of Fools (about 1500 to 1510), The Temptation of St Anthony (about 1501) and the Haywain Triptych (about 1510 to 1516).
--- fact 5
King Philip II of Spain later bought every Bosch he could, which is why the Prado in Madrid holds his greatest paintings.
--- fact 6
Pieter Bruegel the Elder built on his crowded moral landscapes. After being partly forgotten, Bosch was claimed in the 20th century by Surrealists such as Salvador Dalí and Max Ernst.
--- fact 7
In 2016, the 500th anniversary of his death, the Bosch Research and Conservation Project re-examined his works, and several were reattributed to his workshop.
--- context
Bosch worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], at the time of the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter who gave visible form to the fears of a meaning-engine, hell, folly and temptation, in crowded panels meant to be read slowly and closely. The same images were later read by Surrealists as dreams, so what the pictures meant changed with the audience. With only about 25 firmly attributed works and reattributions as recent as 2016, the artist himself is still being redrawn, and any account of what he intended should be held loosely.
--- question 1
Which works are by Bosch himself and which by his workshop?
--- question 2
What did the imagery mean to the people who first saw it?

=== person/hilma-af-klint
title: Hilma af Klint
type: person
date: 1862 – 1944
themes: art, religion, science
src: f0968026bedf
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Hilma af Klint (26 October 1862 to 21 October 1944) was a Swedish painter who, from 1906, created hundreds of abstract paintings guided by her spiritual beliefs, several years before Kandinsky, Mondrian and Malevich. She kept them hidden, and they were shown to the public only from the 1980s.
--- fact 1
Af Klint was born at Karlberg Palace in Solna, near Stockholm. She studied at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Stockholm from 1882 to 1887 and made a living painting landscapes, portraits and botanical illustrations.
--- fact 2
The death of her younger sister in 1880 drew her toward spiritualism and Theosophy. In 1896 she and four other women formed "The Five", which held séances and experimented with automatic drawing.
--- fact 3
In 1906, believing she had received a commission from a spiritual guide, she began the Paintings for the Temple, a cycle of nearly 200 works completed in 1915. She considered herself a medium through whom the works were created.
--- fact 4
In 1908 she showed her work to Rudolf Steiner. His cool response contributed to a four-year break in the series. She later joined his Anthroposophical Society and filled some 125 notebooks.
--- fact 5
She believed the world was not ready for the paintings and directed that they not be exhibited until 20 years after her death. She died after a traffic accident, leaving more than 1,200 paintings and drawings to her nephew.
--- fact 6
Her abstract works combine geometric and organic forms with letters and symbols. In her colour code blue often stood for the female, yellow for the male and green for their union. She drew on Theosophy, Christianity, botany and the new sciences of evolution and atomic physics.
--- fact 7
The works first appeared publicly in the 1986 exhibition The Spiritual in Art at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art. The Guggenheim's Paintings for the Future (2018 to 2019) drew more than 600,000 visitors, the most visited exhibition in its history.
--- context
Af Klint worked in Sweden, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes work made for a temple of a meaning-engine rather than for a market, and withheld by its maker because she thought the world was not ready, so that art history was written for about eight decades without it. She framed authorship as mediumship, which sits awkwardly with the modern habit of crediting an individual inventor. The claim that she "invented abstraction" before Kandinsky depends on dating the works and defining abstraction, and the question of who was first is a story-glue of art history.
--- question 1
How should the date and priority of her abstraction be weighed against those of Kandinsky, Mondrian and Malevich?

=== person/ho-chi-minh
title: Ho Chi Minh
type: person
date: 1890 – 1969
themes: war, kinship
src: ebf797f3becd
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ho Chi Minh (1890 to 1969) was a Vietnamese revolutionary who led the movement against French rule and became the first president of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam in 1945. He held the office until his death, during the Vietnam War.
--- fact 1
He was born on 19 May 1890 in Nghệ An province, in French-ruled Vietnam, as Nguyễn Sinh Cung. He later used many names, including Nguyễn Ái Quốc, and took Hồ Chí Minh about 1943. He also gave other birth years at various times.
--- fact 2
He left Vietnam in 1911 and lived in Britain, France, the Soviet Union and China. In early 1930 in Hong Kong he chaired the merger of Vietnamese communist groups into one party.
--- fact 3
He returned to Vietnam in 1941 to lead the Việt Minh independence front. On 2 September 1945 in Hanoi he read the declaration of independence, which opened by quoting the American and French declarations of rights.
--- fact 4
The First Indochina War ended with the Việt Minh victory at Điện Biên Phủ in 1954. Vietnam was then partitioned at the 17th parallel, and the elections planned for 1956 to reunify it were never held.
--- fact 5
In the land reform of 1953 to 1956 in the North, many people were wrongly executed. Estimates range from about a thousand to tens of thousands, and he apologized in 1956 for serious errors.
--- fact 6
He died in Hanoi on 2 September 1969. Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City after reunification in 1975.
--- context
Ho Chi Minh worked in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], in the years of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] and the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a man who used dozens of names became the named symbol of a nation, and that his declaration borrowed the mark-code of the very powers he opposed. His name now marks the largest city of the country he did not live to see reunified. A specialist could argue that party and army leaders, more than one man, drove decisions in the 1960s, which would weaken a reading that centers his person.
--- question 1
How many people were executed in the land reform, and what did he know of it?
--- question 2
How much did he direct the war after the early 1960s?

=== person/hojo-tokimune
title: Hojo Tokimune
type: person
date: 1251 – 1284
themes: war, religion
src: 964082dfa0dd
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hojo Tokimune (1251 to 1284) was the eighth regent (shikken) of Japan's Kamakura shogunate, ruling as its de facto military leader during the Mongol invasions of Japan in 1274 and 1281.
--- fact 1
He became regent in 1268 at about age seventeen, shortly after the Mongol court of Kublai Khan first demanded Japan's submission.
--- fact 2
He refused all Mongol diplomatic demands and reportedly had several Mongol envoys executed, a decision that made war effectively certain.
--- fact 3
Japanese defenders repelled the 1274 and 1281 Mongol invasion fleets; in both cases severe storms, later remembered as kamikaze ("divine wind"), destroyed large parts of the Mongol fleets after the defenders had already inflicted losses on the invading forces.
--- fact 4
He was a patron of Zen Buddhism, inviting the Chinese monk Wuxue Zuyuan to Kamakura and founding the temple Engaku-ji, reportedly to honor the dead of both sides after the invasions.
--- context
Hojo Tokimune governed Kamakura Japan in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], defending against the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] at the height of its westward and eastward expansion. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the "divine wind" that ended both invasions has often overshadowed the more mundane fact that Japanese coastal fortifications and defenders, built and organized under Tokimune's government, had already stalled both landings before the storms struck. Crediting the weather alone understates the preparation his regency undertook.
--- question 1
How much of the invasions' failure owed to the storms versus the defensive walls and tactics Tokimune's government had built along Hakata Bay?

=== person/ibn-khaldun
title: Ibn Khaldun
type: person
date: 1332 – 1406
themes: science, kinship, war
src: ff95b07915b8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ibn Khaldun (1332 to 1406) was a North African historian whose 'Muqaddimah' (1377) proposed a general theory of how societies and states rise and fall.
--- fact 1
He was born in Tunis and served in several courts in North Africa and Andalusia.
--- fact 2
He argued that group solidarity (asabiyyah) helps found dynasties, and that as rulers grow comfortable it weakens.
--- fact 3
He wrote about economics, urban life, and the role of climate and environment.
--- fact 4
He is sometimes called a founder of sociology and historiography.
--- context
Ibn Khaldun wrote in the world of [[person/ibn-battuta|Ibn Battuta]] and recorded facts about the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]]. His approach is close to the Observer's own.

=== person/j-m-w-turner
title: J. M. W. Turner
type: person
date: 1775 – 1851
themes: art, technology
src: 1c402dafa9ff
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Joseph Mallord William Turner (April 1775 to 19 December 1851) was the greatest British landscape painter and a bold colourist. He began as a precise topographical watercolourist, and his late paintings dissolve ships, trains and cities into light, mist and weather.
--- fact 1
Turner was born in Covent Garden, London, the son of a barber and wigmaker. At 14 he entered the Royal Academy Schools and at 15 exhibited his first watercolour. He was elected a full Royal Academician in 1802, at 26.
--- fact 2
He travelled tirelessly in Britain, the Alps, France, Germany and Italy, especially Venice, filling hundreds of sketchbooks.
--- fact 3
He never married but had two daughters with Sarah Danby. John Ruskin championed him in Modern Painters (1843). He died in Chelsea and was buried in St Paul's Cathedral.
--- fact 4
By the 1830s he painted natural forces themselves, storms, fire, sunlight and steam. He worked thin washes over white grounds, scraped and rubbed his canvases, and applied paint thickly with knives.
--- fact 5
He painted steamships and railways alongside Venetian lagoons. Rain, Steam and Speed (1844) is one of the first great paintings of modern technology.
--- fact 6
The Slave Ship (1840) was inspired by the Zong massacre of 1781, when enslaved Africans were thrown overboard so owners could claim insurance. He showed it during the 1840 campaign to end slavery worldwide.
--- fact 7
He left around 300 oil paintings and some 30,000 sketches and watercolours to the nation, held mainly at Tate Britain. Monet and Pissarro studied his work in London in 1870 to 1871, and the Turner Prize has been awarded since 1984.
--- context
Turner painted the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and its [[technology/railways|Railways]] in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and in The Slave Ship confronted the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter who put a steam train and a slave ship into the same body of work, the two great tool-lineages of his century, one celebrated and one condemned, both moving people and goods across water and land. Critics who mocked his late style were reading a change in how the species pictures weather and speed. That he left his work to the nation and gave a prize its name shows a personal reputation converted into an institution, a story-glue for national art.
--- question 1
How did his late style change how contemporaries saw landscape, given the criticism it drew?

--- summary
Jadwiga (about 1373 to 1399) was crowned "King" of Poland in 1384 at about age ten, the first woman to rule Poland in her own right, and her marriage to the Grand Duke of Lithuania began a dynastic union between the two states.
--- fact 1
She was crowned using the male title "rex" (king) rather than "regina" (queen) to underscore that she held full sovereign authority in her own right, not merely as a royal consort.
--- fact 2
In 1386 she married Jogaila, the pagan Grand Duke of Lithuania, who converted to Christianity and was crowned co-ruler as Wladyslaw II Jagiello, founding the Jagiellonian dynasty and beginning the Christianization of Lithuania.
--- fact 3
She personally funded the restoration of the Krakow Academy, later the Jagiellonian University, endowing it in her will and helping revive an institution that became a major center of learning in Central Europe.
--- fact 4
She died in 1399, days after giving birth to a daughter who also died in infancy, ending her direct line; she was canonized by the Catholic Church in 1997.
--- context
Jadwiga ruled Poland in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and her marriage began the Polish-Lithuanian union. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Jadwiga's reign is remembered largely for a marriage alliance and a religious conversion she enabled rather than personal political acts, yet the union of Poland and Lithuania that resulted reshaped Central European power for centuries, an outsized consequence for a reign that ended before she turned thirty.

=== person/jan-van-eyck
title: Jan van Eyck
type: person
date: c. 1390 – 1441
themes: art, religion
src: 4c746ce3b46d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Jan van Eyck (about 1390 to 1441) was the leading painter of the Burgundian Netherlands and a founder of Early Netherlandish painting. He refined a method of thin, translucent oil glazes that let him paint light and texture with unprecedented realism.
--- fact 1
Van Eyck was probably born about 1390 near Maaseik, in present-day Belgian Limburg. Nothing is known of his training. He appears in records about 1422 at The Hague, working for John of Bavaria.
--- fact 2
From 1425 he was court painter and valet de chambre to Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, who sent him on confidential missions, including a trip to Portugal in 1428 to 1429 to paint Princess Isabella.
--- fact 3
About 1430 he settled in Bruges. He completed the Ghent Altarpiece, begun by his brother Hubert, in 1432, and died in Bruges in July 1441.
--- fact 4
He did not invent oil paint, as Vasari later claimed. He built up many thin, transparent glazes, and he often signed and dated his work, sometimes with the motto Als ich can ("As I can").
--- fact 5
Key works include the Ghent Altarpiece (1432), the Arnolfini Portrait (1434) and the Madonna of Chancellor Rolin (about 1435).
--- fact 6
He did not use the mathematical perspective being developed in Italy. He built space through observation of light and atmosphere.
--- fact 7
The Ghent Altarpiece survived iconoclasm, fire and Napoleon's looters, was seized by the Nazis, and was recovered from an Austrian salt mine. A restoration begun in 2012 revealed the Lamb's strikingly human face in 2020.
--- context
Van Eyck worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], at the time of the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a patron who pays for close detail is also buying status-signal, since a jewel-like surface shows that its owner can command rare materials and slow labour. The candles, fruit and mirrors link everyday objects to the meaning-engine, so the same careful looking serves both prestige and belief. That a whole technique was long credited to one man shows how a tool-lineage tends to be compressed into a single name. His role and his brother's on the Ghent Altarpiece is only partly recoverable.
--- question 1
How much of the Ghent Altarpiece was painted by Hubert van Eyck rather than Jan?

=== person/jane-austen
title: Jane Austen
type: person
date: 1775 – 1817
themes: art, language, kinship
src: ae16caf7c34f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Jane Austen (1775 to 1817) was an English novelist whose six completed novels of courtship, inheritance and class anxiety among the gentry remain among the most widely read in the language. All were published anonymously in her lifetime.
--- fact 1
Austen was born in 1775 in Steventon, Hampshire, the seventh of eight children of a country clergyman, in a family that encouraged reading, amateur theatre and writing.
--- fact 2
Sense and Sensibility appeared in 1811, credited only to "A Lady", followed by Pride and Prejudice (1813), Mansfield Park (1814), Emma (1815) and, after her death, Northanger Abbey and Persuasion (1817).
--- fact 3
Only close family knew of her authorship. She fell ill, probably with Addison's disease though the cause is debated, and died in 1817 at 41. Her authorship was publicly acknowledged in a biographical notice her brother attached to her last two novels.
--- fact 4
She worked within a deliberately narrow social canvas, mainly the English gentry, courtship, income and inheritance, rather than the wider political events of her era.
--- fact 5
Her distinctive technique is free indirect discourse: narrating in the third person while letting the prose absorb a character's own irony or blind spots, so the reader senses a character's error before the character does.
--- fact 6
Her novels have remained continuously in print and are widely adapted for film, television and stage. Her reputation grew after her death rather than during her modest anonymous success.
--- context
Austen wrote in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], during the [[event/napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars]], but kept to the gentry's household world. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a body of fiction in which the kin-lattice, marriage, inheritance and income, is the whole plot, so that the machinery of who may marry whom and who inherits is made a subject of comedy rather than law. Her authorship was hidden while she lived, and her fame is a later assignment. The claim that she chose a narrow canvas over the wars of her age is an interpretation of her silence, and a reader could equally see the wars as absent because the household itself was her subject.

=== person/jayavarman-vii
title: Jayavarman VII
type: person
date: c. 1122 – 1218
themes: religion, war, art
src: 3ed2ee39445e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jayavarman VII (about 1122 to about 1218) was king of the Khmer Empire from about 1181 until his death. He drove out Cham invaders who had sacked Angkor and then led a large building program that included Angkor Thom and the Bayon.
--- fact 1
Champa forces sacked Angkor in 1177. Jayavarman VII defeated them and was crowned about 1181; his birth and death years are estimates.
--- fact 2
Khmer forces later brought parts of Champa under Khmer control.
--- fact 3
He was a Mahayana Buddhist, while most earlier Khmer rulers had favored Hindu cults.
--- fact 4
His projects include Angkor Thom, the Bayon with its towers carved with large faces, Ta Prohm (dedicated to his mother) and Preah Khan (dedicated to his father).
--- fact 5
An inscription of 1186 at Ta Prohm credits him with 102 hospitals; the figure comes from the king's own inscriptions.
--- fact 6
The faces on the Bayon towers are variously read as a Buddhist deity, the king, or both.
--- context
Jayavarman VII ruled the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] from [[place/angkor|Angkor]] in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and fought [[culture/champa|Champa]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Jayavarman VII took power after Angkor was sacked from outside and answered with building on an unusual scale: temples for his parents, hospitals, and a walled city whose towers carry giant faces. His inscriptions present these works as merit-making by a Buddhist king, which also served as a claim to legitimacy. The labor behind them appears in the record as the king's gift, and the workers are almost invisible. A specialist could argue that the program strained Angkor's resources, which would weaken a reading of it as simply benevolent.

Whom do the faces of the Bayon depict?

=== person/jeong-seon
title: Jeong Seon
type: person
date: 1676 – 1759
themes: art
src: f04c4f2f1b81
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Jeong Seon (1676 to 1759), pen name Gyeomjae, was a Korean painter of the Joseon dynasty who transformed landscape painting by depicting the real scenery of Korea instead of imagined Chinese scenery. His "true-view landscape" (jingyeong sansuhwa) expressed a growing pride in Korean culture.
--- fact 1
Jeong Seon was born in 1676 in Hanyang (modern Seoul), into a scholarly family of modest means. His father died when he was young. He trained in painting and in Neo-Confucian learning.
--- fact 2
Through recommendations he held government posts, including county magistrate at Hayang and Yangcheon, and rose to high official rank in old age.
--- fact 3
He lived near Mount Inwang in Seoul. His friend the poet Yi Byeong-yeon exchanged poems and paintings with him for decades.
--- fact 4
In 1711 and 1712 he travelled to the Diamond Mountains (Geumgangsan), and the albums he made there made his name. He died in 1759 at 83.
--- fact 5
For centuries Korean painters had followed Chinese models. He painted the actual scenery of Korea and invented his own brushstrokes: sharp vertical strokes for granite peaks and soft wet dots for forested earth mountains.
--- fact 6
He often combined a bird's-eye view with exaggeration. His paintings are not maps but expressions of the experience of real places.
--- fact 7
Two works, Geumgang jeondo and Inwang jesaekdo, are National Treasures of South Korea. Kim Hong-do and Sim Sa-jeong followed him, and the Gyeomjae Jeong Seon Art Museum opened in Seoul in 2009.
--- context
Jeong Seon worked in Joseon Korea in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a shift in whose mountains count. For centuries Korean painters followed Chinese models of idealised scenery, and Jeong Seon painted the granite of the actual peninsula. The change was carried by a network of friends who exchanged poems and paintings, and it expressed a pride in local place, a story-glue of landscape. Because the paintings are expressions rather than surveys, "true view" should be read as an intention, not a measurement of the terrain.
--- question 1
How closely do the true-view paintings follow the real landscape?
