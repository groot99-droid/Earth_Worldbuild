=== culture/srivijaya
title: Srivijaya
type: culture
date: c. 650 CE – 1377
themes: trade, religion
src: 6a119d16d426
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Srivijaya was a Buddhist maritime kingdom based at Palembang on Sumatra that dominated the Strait of Malacca and its trade from the 7th to the 13th century.
--- fact 1
The Chinese monk Yijing stayed in Srivijaya in 671 and reported on Buddhist study there.
--- fact 2
The oldest known Srivijayan inscription, the Kedukan Bukit inscription, dates to 683.
--- fact 3
It controlled shipping through the Strait of Malacca and the Sunda Strait.
--- fact 4
A naval expedition of the Chola king Rajendra I struck Srivijaya around 1025.
--- fact 5
Its power faded in the 13th and 14th centuries, and it was absorbed by Java-based powers.
--- context
Srivijaya was a hub of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]], linking China and India. It was a Buddhist counterpart to the Hindu-Buddhist [[place/angkor|Angkor]] and [[place/borobudur|Borobudur]] world, and clashed with the [[culture/chola-dynasty|Chola Dynasty]]. Srivijaya sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the state's power rested on control of a strait and on the loyalty of port chiefs, not on a large territory. Power over movement can be more durable than power over land, until someone builds a faster route.
--- question 1
How centralized was Srivijaya's rule?

=== culture/sumerians
title: Sumerians
type: culture
date: c. 4500 – 1900 BCE
themes: language, religion, trade
src: 114e8e245f93
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Sumerians were the people of southern Mesopotamia who built some of the world's first cities and developed cuneiform writing.
--- fact 1
Their city-states included Uruk, Ur, and Lagash.
--- fact 2
They used cuneiform script on clay tablets for administration, literature, and law.
--- fact 3
They built temple towers called ziggurats.
--- fact 4
The Sumerian language is unrelated to any known living language family.
--- context
The Sumerians are central to [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] and [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]]. See [[place/uruk|Uruk]] and [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a people who left no continuing language nonetheless left the technology of writing to the world. Cultural inheritance does not require survival of the culture.

Where did the Sumerians come from?

=== culture/taino
title: Taino
type: culture
date: c. 1000
themes: kinship, religion
src: 8c62bec1c543
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Taino were an Arawakan-speaking people of the Greater Antilles and the Bahamas, who were the first Indigenous Americans encountered by Columbus in 1492.
--- fact 1
They lived in villages ruled by chiefs called caciques, and grew cassava, maize, and sweet potato.
--- fact 2
They carved wooden and stone figures called zemis, associated with ancestors and spirits.
--- fact 3
English words including canoe, hammock, hurricane, and barbecue come from Taino.
--- fact 4
After 1492 their populations fell sharply because of disease, forced labor, and violence.
--- fact 5
Genetic studies show Indigenous Caribbean ancestry survives in present-day Caribbean populations, contrary to the older idea that the Taino vanished.
--- context
The Taino were the first to experience the impact of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. The plantation economy that followed is described in [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. Taino sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that several everyday words in English come from a language whose speakers were largely destroyed within a few decades. The words traveled as far as the people did not.
--- question 1
What was the Taino population in 1492?

themes: art, trade, religion
src: 3d8c9ff5c6fd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Tang dynasty ruled China from 618 to 907 CE and is regarded as a high point of Chinese poetry, art, and cosmopolitan culture.
--- fact 1
Its capital Chang'an was one of the world's largest cities, with about a million people.
--- fact 2
Poets such as Li Bai and Du Fu wrote in this period.
--- fact 3
The earliest dated printed book, the Diamond Sutra, was made in 868; see [[technology/woodblock-printing|Woodblock Printing]].
--- fact 4
The An Lushan Rebellion (755 to 763) weakened the dynasty; death-toll estimates range from about 13 to 36 million and are disputed.
--- context
The Tang followed the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] tradition and preceded the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]]. [[person/wu-zetian|Wu Zetian]] ruled during it.
--- observer
The Observer notes a capital that hosted merchants, monks, and envoys from across Eurasia. A very connected city produces many new ideas, and also many new dependencies.
--- question 1
What was the scale of the An Lushan Rebellion's toll?

The Timurids claimed the heritage of the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] and passed their court culture to the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]].

src: 34f48261ac0b
mode: epic-fantasy

--- summary
The Tokugawa shogunate was the military government that ruled Japan from 1603 to 1867, a long period of peace and restricted foreign contact.
--- fact 1
Tokugawa Ieyasu became shogun in 1603 and ruled from Edo (now Tokyo).

The policy known as 'sakoku' limited foreign trade, mainly to Dutch and Chinese merchants at Nagasaki.

Edo grew to about a million people, one of the world's largest cities.
--- fact 4
The shogunate collapsed in 1867, and imperial rule was restored the following year in the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]].

The shogunate ruled through the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]] and [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] for the neighboring Chinese state.
--- observer
The Observer notes a society that had a long internal peace and a developed urban culture with limited foreign contact. Peace let art and commerce flourish, and left the state unprepared for outside pressure.

How isolated was Tokugawa Japan in practice?

date: 661 – 750 CE

src: 4bf4bd02680f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Umayyad Caliphate, ruling from Damascus from 661 to 750 CE, was the first hereditary dynasty of Islamic rulers and extended from Iberia to Sindh.

Muawiya I founded the dynasty in 661 after the first civil war among Muslims.

Its armies conquered North Africa, most of Iberia (from 711), and Sindh (711).

An Umayyad survivor, Abd al-Rahman I, founded a new Umayyad state in Cordoba in 756.
--- context
The Umayyad Caliphate built the first large state of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] and was succeeded by the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]]. Umayyad Caliphate sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that within a century of [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]], a religious movement from the Arabian Peninsula had become an administrative empire that changed the language of government across three continents. Meaning-engines can also become bureaucracies.

How quickly did conversion follow conquest?

=== culture/xiongnu
title: Xiongnu
type: culture
date: c. 209 BCE – 93 CE
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 8e13296bfb39
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Xiongnu were a confederation of steppe peoples united under Modu Shanyu in 209 BCE, which became the first great steppe empire and the main rival of Han China.
--- fact 1
Chinese sources report that in 200 BCE Modu surrounded the Han emperor Gaozu at Baideng.
--- fact 2
The Han then adopted the 'heqin' policy: marrying a princess to the ruler and sending annual gifts of silk, grain, and other goods.
--- fact 3
The treaty was renewed many times until about 135 BCE.
--- fact 4
The confederation split around 51 BCE, and the northern Xiongnu were defeated by Han and allied forces around 89 to 93 CE.
--- context
The Xiongnu shaped the frontier policy of the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], following the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]]. They are the first in a line of steppe powers that culminated in the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how a mobile people without cities forced a large agrarian state to pay them for peace. Wealth flowed from the settled to the mobile, and that shaped both.
--- question 1
How centralized was the Xiongnu confederation?

observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Sakha (Yakut) are a Turkic-speaking people of northeastern Siberia who developed horse and cattle herding in one of the coldest inhabited regions on Earth; the timing of their northward migration is debated, but is often placed around the 13th to 15th centuries.
--- fact 1

--- fact 2
Russian penetration of the region began in the 17th century, and a major revolt against Russian rule occurred in 1642.

The Sakha supplied Russian expeditions with food, horses, and guides.

The Sakha were incorporated in [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]]. See [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a herding culture built around horses in a land where winter temperatures fall far below what most of the species could endure. Cultural adaptation replaced biological adaptation.

=== culture/zagwe-dynasty
title: Zagwe Dynasty
type: culture
date: c. 1137 – 1270
themes: religion, art
src: 2485fc9c1792
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Zagwe dynasty ruled much of the Ethiopian highlands from about 1137 to 1270. Its best-known king, Lalibela, is remembered for a set of rock-hewn churches carved from living stone.
--- fact 1
The Zagwe dynasty ruled large parts of the Ethiopian highlands from about 1137 until 1270, when the last Zagwe king, Za-Ilmaknun, was killed in battle by the forces of the Amhara ruler Yekuno Amlak.
--- fact 2
King Lalibela reigned about 1185 to 1225 and directed the carving of eleven monolithic churches from living rock at the Zagwe capital, which was later renamed for him. The churches are cut down into the rock below ground level and are a UNESCO World Heritage site.
--- fact 3
In 1270 Yekuno Amlak founded a new dynasty, often called Solomonic, which ruled until 1974. Its rulers traced their descent to the biblical King Solomon through the royal house of Aksum, and dismissed the Zagwe as usurpers, describing their own rule as a restoration.
--- context
The Zagwe followed the decline of the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] and preceded the long Solomonic line whose later rulers faced Italy at the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the dynasty that replaced the Zagwe wrote them down as usurpers and claimed an unbroken older lineage, so the account of the Zagwe survives largely through the story of their successors. The churches, by contrast, are a record the successors could not rewrite; see [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]].
--- question 1
How did the Zagwe understand their own legitimacy, given that the surviving account comes from their successors?
--- question 2
How were the Lalibela churches designed and built, and over how many years?

type: culture
date: c. 1000 BCE
themes: religion
src: 8ab415105d5b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Zoroastrianism is an Iranian religion based on the teachings of Zarathustra, centered on the god Ahura Mazda and a moral struggle between truth and falsehood.
--- fact 1
Its oldest texts, the Gathas, are hymns attributed to Zarathustra in an archaic form of Iranian.
--- fact 2
The date of Zarathustra is uncertain; scholars have proposed dates from about 1500 to about 600 BCE.
--- fact 3
Its sacred collection is the Avesta.
--- fact 4
The religion became the state religion of the Sasanian Empire (224 to 651 CE).
--- fact 5
Zoroastrian communities persist in Iran and India, where they are called Parsis.
--- context
Zoroastrianism shaped the religious world of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], and is often discussed in connection with the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]]. After the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] it became a minority faith. Zoroastrianism sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that its emphasis on a cosmic struggle between order and disorder, and on a final judgment, resembles ideas found in later traditions, though the direction of influence is debated. The claim of borrowing would be weakened if independent origins were demonstrated.
--- question 1
When did Zarathustra live?
--- question 2
How much did Zoroastrianism influence later religions?

type: event
date: 1918 – 1920
themes: science, kinship
src: d25b0eb7022b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The 1918 influenza pandemic, caused by an H1N1 virus, infected roughly a third of the world's population and killed an estimated 50 million people from 1918 to 1920.
--- fact 1
Estimates of deaths range widely, from about 17 million to 100 million, with 50 million often cited.
--- fact 2
It is called the Spanish flu because neutral Spain reported openly on it during wartime censorship in other countries.
--- fact 3
It killed an unusually high share of healthy young adults.
--- fact 4
Troop movements during World War I helped spread it.
--- fact 5
Researchers reconstructed the virus from preserved tissue, publishing the genome in 2005.
--- context
The pandemic coincided with the end of [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and is the closest comparison for the [[event/covid-19-pandemic|COVID-19 Pandemic]]. It was a deadly pandemic like the [[event/black-death|Black Death]] in a much more connected world. 1918 Influenza Pandemic sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a disease killed more people than the war it accompanied, yet is much less commemorated. Memory favors events with an enemy and a narrative over events with only a cause.
--- question 1
Why was mortality so high in young adults?

type: event
date: c. 1807 – 1888
themes: war, religion, trade
src: 9e0c6fdf4048
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between 1807 and 1888, the transatlantic slave trade and then slavery itself were legally abolished across the Atlantic world, ending a system that had lasted for centuries.
--- fact 1
Britain banned the slave trade in 1807 and abolished slavery in its colonies in 1833. The government paid £20 million in compensation to slaveholders and nothing to the people who had been enslaved.
--- fact 2
The United States ended slavery in 1865 with the 13th Amendment.
--- fact 3
Brazil abolished it on 13 May 1888 with the Lei Áurea, the last country in the Americas to do so.
--- fact 4
Forced labor and human trafficking persist; recent estimates suggest about 50 million people live in modern slavery.
--- context
Abolition followed resistance by enslaved people, including the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]], and campaigns by abolitionists. See [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that an institution once defended by law, economics, and religion was, within a few generations, declared a moral wrong. Moral norms can shift within lifetimes, though the practice does not vanish.
--- question 1

=== event/age-of-revolutions
title: Age of Revolutions
type: event
date: c. 1775 – 1848
themes: war, kinship, religion
src: 9f88bb3200e9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Age of Revolutions is a period of political upheaval around the Atlantic world, beginning in the 1770s and running to the revolutions of 1848. Its dates are a matter of historians' choice, and this note uses 1775 to 1848.
--- fact 1
Historians periodize the era differently. Eric Hobsbawm's *The Age of Revolution* covers 1789 to 1848; Robert Palmer's *The Age of the Democratic Revolution* starts from about 1760; some accounts begin as early as 1765.
--- fact 2
Major events: the American Revolution (to 1783), the French Revolution (1789 to 1799), the Haitian Revolution (1791 to 1804), the Spanish American wars of independence (beginning about 1808 to 1810), and the revolutions of 1848.
--- fact 3
The Haitian Revolution began with a mass uprising of enslaved people in northern Saint-Domingue on 22 August 1791. Independence was declared on 1 January 1804, and Haiti is widely described as the first state founded by formerly enslaved people. It was the second independent country in the Americas, after the United States.
--- fact 4
Ideas of popular sovereignty, citizenship, and nationalism spread widely.
--- fact 5
Slavery and the rights of women were contested and largely excluded at first.
--- context
The revolutions overlap the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and reshape claim-lines and legitimacy; see [[observer-note/the-fiction-of-claim-lines|The Fiction of Claim-Lines]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a change in the story-glue of legitimacy: authority increasingly claimed to come from 'the people' rather than from tradition or the divine.
--- question 1
How connected were the revolutions to each other? Older histories treated them as separate episodes; recent scholarship increasingly treats them as a linked chain, and the question of causation is still argued.

=== event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths
title: Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths
type: event
date: c. 500 BCE – 1600
themes: earth-systems, technology, kinship
src: 518efe246c14
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Amazonian urbanism is the growing evidence that parts of the Amazon basin supported large, planned settlements linked by roads and earthworks, from about 500 BCE to the 1500s CE, and that Indigenous peoples enriched the forest soils and landscape.
--- fact 1
Aerial laser survey (lidar) has revealed settlement networks at [[place/upano-valley-settlements|Upano Valley Settlements]] in Ecuador, [[culture/casarabe-culture|Casarabe Culture]] sites in Bolivia, and [[place/xingu-garden-cities|Xingu Garden Cities]] in Brazil.
--- fact 2
The [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]] built large mounds at the mouth of the Amazon.
--- fact 3
Dark, fertile soils (terra preta, or Amazonian Dark Earths), rich in charcoal and often found with pre-Columbian pottery, occur across the basin and are associated with settlement. Whether people created them or only used natural deposits is disputed; a 2021 study of one site in the Brazilian Amazon argued for a largely natural, alluvial origin.
--- fact 4
These settlements were 'low-density' compared with cities elsewhere: dispersed households, plazas, roads, and gardens rather than compact walled towns.
--- context
The evidence changes the picture of the [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]] before the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] and links to wider questions in [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a city need not look like a city elsewhere. Dispersed, garden-based settlements challenge the assumption that dense stone towns are the mark of complexity.
--- question 1
How large were Amazonian populations before 1500, and how much of the forest was managed?

=== event/american-civil-war
title: American Civil War
type: event
date: 1861 – 1865
themes: war, kinship, technology
src: ee96cbd5bb42
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The American Civil War (1861 to 1865) was fought between the United States and eleven Southern states that seceded and formed the Confederacy. Historians overwhelmingly agree that slavery was central to secession. The war ended with the Confederate defeat, and the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery later in 1865.
--- fact 1
The war began on 12 April 1861 with the Confederate bombardment of Fort Sumter. General Robert E. Lee surrendered the main Confederate army at Appomattox on 9 April 1865; some fighting and surrenders continued into the following weeks.
--- fact 2
Eleven states formed the Confederacy: seven seceded first, and four joined after the war began.
--- fact 3
Estimates of the dead vary by method. The traditional figure is about 620,000. A 2011 census-based study by J. David Hacker put it at about 750,000, with a range of 650,000 to 850,000. A 2024 study using full-count census records estimated about 698,000 deaths, with a bounding interval of roughly 50,000 either way. It found a much higher death rate in the Confederate states than in the Union: about 13 percent of military-age native-born white men in the core Confederate states against about 5 percent in the core Union states, although the Union lost more men in absolute numbers (more than 400,000 against about 300,000). These are estimates of excess mortality, and no side kept standardized records.
--- fact 4
The Emancipation Proclamation took effect on 1 January 1863. It was a wartime measure that freed people held in states in rebellion. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified on 6 December 1865, abolished slavery throughout the United States.
--- fact 5
Railways, telegraphs, and industrial production shaped the conduct of the war.
--- fact 6
It was the deadliest conflict in American history.
--- context
The war is central to the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]] and shows the effects of the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] on warfare. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a modern state fought its own citizens over the ownership of other human beings. The war also displays a technology-driven scale of killing that presaged [[event/world-war-i|World War I]].
--- question 1
How reliable are the casualty estimates? The figures range from about 620,000 to 750,000 or more depending on method, and civilian deaths are counted inconsistently.

--- summary
The Assembly of Pangaea was the joining of nearly all of Earth's continents into one supercontinent, mostly in the Carboniferous, about 335 to 300 million years ago. It stayed together until it began to split about 200 million years ago.
--- fact 1
The southern continent Gondwana collided with the northern continent Laurussia, raising mountain belts including the Appalachians in North America and the Variscan belt in Europe. Siberia and Kazakhstan joined at the Ural Mountains about 300 to 290 million years ago.
--- fact 2
Pangaea, meaning "all Earth", was surrounded by a single ocean, Panthalassa, with the Tethys Sea cutting into its eastern side.
--- fact 3
The evidence includes matching mountain belts, rock types and fossils across ocean basins, such as the plant Glossopteris and the reptile Lystrosaurus, and magnetic data from ancient rocks.
--- fact 4
The supercontinent had a vast, dry, strongly seasonal interior and less shallow-sea shelf area. The latter is one proposed contributor to the marine losses of the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]].
--- fact 5
Its break-up is described in [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]].
--- context
The Assembly of Pangaea runs through the [[era/carboniferous-period|Carboniferous Period]] and into the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]], and it precedes the [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]] in the Mesozoic. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the case for a single landmass was assembled from fossils and rock types decades before a mechanism was known, and was rejected for decades because it lacked one. It reads this as an example of how a well-supported pattern can wait for its explanation. The reading would weaken if the pattern had been dropped once the mechanism was studied.

--- observer

=== event/banded-iron-formations
title: Banded Iron Formations
type: event
date: 3.8 – 1.8 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: 34c9f7666890
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Banded iron formations are ancient sedimentary rocks of alternating iron-rich and silica-rich layers, deposited mostly between about 3.8 and 1.8 billion years ago. They are a major source of the world's iron ore.
--- fact 1
The layers are usually millimetres to centimetres thick. The oldest are about 3.8 billion years old, in Greenland, and deposition peaked about 2.7 to 2.4 billion years ago, with great deposits in Western Australia, South Africa and the Lake Superior region of North America.
--- fact 2
The leading explanation is that oxygen-poor seawater held dissolved iron, which was oxidized and settled out when oxygen or other oxidants appeared. Whether oxygen-producing bacteria or iron-using photosynthetic bacteria did most of the work is debated.
--- fact 3
Deposition largely ended about 1.8 billion years ago, probably as the deeper ocean lost its dissolved iron, and a few late formations appear during the Cryogenian ice ages; see [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]].
--- fact 4
Ore derived from these rocks is a major source of the iron used in steelmaking today.
--- context
Banded iron formations record the slow oxidation of the oceans before and during the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]], and they tie the [[event/origin-of-photosynthesis|Origin of Photosynthesis]] and the work of [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]] to changes in ocean chemistry. They belong to the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and continue into the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]], and to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the raw material of the species' steel is the residue of an ocean that rusted over about two billion years, a process driven by microbes with no aim beyond their own metabolism. The reading concerns only the link across time, and the debate over which microbes did the work does not affect it.
--- question 1
Which organisms or processes oxidized the dissolved iron?
--- question 2
Why did banded iron formations largely stop forming about 1.8 billion years ago?
