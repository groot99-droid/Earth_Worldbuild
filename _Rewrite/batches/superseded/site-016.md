=== event/thirty-years-war
title: Thirty Years' War
type: event
date: 1618 – 1648
themes: war, religion
src: f8b451789fbb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Thirty Years' War (1618 to 1648) was a set of linked wars in central Europe, begun as a religious conflict in the Holy Roman Empire and ended by the Peace of Westphalia.
--- fact 1
It began with a revolt in Bohemia in 1618, after the Defenestration of Prague.
--- fact 2
The Holy Roman Empire, Spain, France, Sweden, Denmark, and the Dutch Republic took part at different times.
--- fact 3
Much of the fighting took place in the German lands, where armies lived off the land.
--- fact 4
Estimates of deaths, mostly from famine and disease, run up to about eight million.
--- fact 5
The war ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648.
--- context
The war was one of the deadliest wars in European history and was rooted in the divisions of the [[event/protestant-reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. It was settled by the [[event/peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]]. Thirty Years' War sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a war that began over the correct form of a shared religion was ended by agreeing that rulers could choose. The settlement moved the claim-line from belief to territory.
--- question 1
What was the death toll in the German lands?

=== event/toba-eruption
title: Toba Eruption
type: event
date: c. 74 ka
themes: earth-systems
src: 2658da376265
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Toba eruption, about 74,000 years ago in Sumatra, was one of the largest volcanic eruptions of the past two million years, and its effects on human populations are debated.
--- fact 1
The eruption formed the caldera now occupied by Lake Toba in northern Sumatra, Indonesia.
--- fact 2
It ejected on the order of 2,800 to 3,000 cubic kilometers of material, and ash fell across South Asia and beyond.
--- fact 3
The Toba catastrophe hypothesis proposed that a volcanic winter reduced human populations to a small number of survivors.
--- fact 4
Archaeological sites in India, such as Jwalapuram, show human occupation continuing through the ash layer, which challenges the hypothesis.
--- fact 5
Most researchers now consider a severe global population bottleneck unlikely.
--- context
The eruption falls in the period when [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] were dispersing, near the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]]. It is often discussed with climate swings leading to the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. Toba Eruption sits in the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that people have long wanted a single catastrophe to explain a genetic feature of the species. The evidence so far suggests the population was more resilient than the story requires.
--- question 1
How large was the climatic cooling, and how long did it last?

=== event/tunguska-event
title: Tunguska Event
type: event
date: 1908
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 62dc174e368a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Tunguska event was an explosion in the atmosphere above the Podkamennaya Tunguska River in Siberia on 30 June 1908, which flattened about 2,000 square kilometers of forest.
--- fact 1
The blast is estimated at roughly ten to fifteen megatons of TNT equivalent.
--- fact 2
It occurred several kilometers above the ground and left no impact crater.
--- fact 3
About 80 million trees were knocked down over an area of about 2,000 square kilometers.
--- fact 4
The first scientific expedition to the site was led by Leonid Kulik in 1927.
--- fact 5
The cause is generally thought to be the airburst of a stony asteroid or a comet fragment tens of meters across.
--- context
The Tunguska event is the largest impact event in recorded history, a small-scale relative of the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]] and the [[event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon|Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon]]. It happened in a region newly reachable by the [[technology/trans-siberian-railway|Trans-Siberian Railway]]. Tunguska Event sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that an explosion greater than any nuclear device then in existence occurred in an empty forest and was noticed mostly by reindeer herders. Planetary hazards do not consult human population maps, and a few kilometers of difference in location would have changed the outcome.
--- question 1
Was the object an asteroid or a comet?

=== event/turkish-war-of-independence
title: Turkish War of Independence
type: event
date: 1919 – 1923
themes: war
src: 94dd17f6b339
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Turkish War of Independence (1919 to 1923) was fought by a nationalist movement under Mustafa Kemal against the Allied occupation of Anatolia and the invading armies of Greece and Armenia. It ended the Ottoman sultanate and established the Republic of Turkey.
--- fact 1
After World War I, Allied forces occupied parts of the Ottoman Empire, and Greece and Armenia moved to claim territory.
--- fact 2
Mustafa Kemal, later known as Atatürk, organized a nationalist movement centred at Ankara. He convened congresses at Erzurum and Sivas, and a national parliament met in Ankara on 23 April 1920. He was elected commander in chief and created a new army from remaining Ottoman forces and irregular units.
--- fact 3
On 1 November 1922 the new parliament abolished the sultanate, ending about 623 years of Ottoman rule.
--- fact 4
The Treaty of Lausanne of 24 July 1923 recognized Turkey's borders and sovereignty, and a population exchange between Greece and Turkey followed. The Republic of Turkey was proclaimed on 29 October 1923.
--- context
The war grew out of the defeat and breakup of the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] after [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and took place in the same region as the earlier [[event/armenian-genocide|Armenian Genocide]]. It belongs to the wider postwar reordering that includes [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] in later decades.
--- observer
The Observer notes that the same movement that fought off partition ended the dynasty in whose name the war began. An old legitimacy was set aside in three years, and a new one, built on a defined homeland, took its place.
--- question 1
How should the population exchange between Greece and Turkey be judged as an event that displaced very large numbers of people?
--- question 2
How did the new republic's founders treat the memory of the Ottoman period?

The Observer notes that a vast body of texts was preserved for centuries with no writing, in the trained memory of specialists. The species had built a very accurate storage device before it built any mark-code for the purpose.

src: c88c060ed6ec
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Movements to win women the right to vote succeeded country by country between the 1890s and the late 20th century.
--- fact 1
New Zealand granted women the vote in national elections in 1893, the first self-governing country to do so.
--- fact 2
Australia's Commonwealth Franchise Act of 1902 let women vote and stand for federal office, but it excluded Aboriginal people and many other non-European people. --- fact 4
Switzerland granted women the vote in national elections in 1971.
--- context
The movements grew out of the ideas of the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]] and gained ground after [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that for most of recorded history, half the species was excluded from formal political life. Its inclusion in the last century may be one of the largest changes in the species' social structure.

--- summary
The Xinhai Revolution of 1911 to 1912 began with an army mutiny at Wuchang and ended the Qing dynasty, which had ruled China since 1644, replacing imperial rule with the Republic of China.

On 10 October 1911 members of the New Army mutinied in Wuchang, in Hubei province. The rebellion spread to surrounding regions, and by the end of 1911 revolutionaries were forming a new government.

--- context
The revolution ended the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]] after decades of internal rebellion and foreign pressure, including the [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]] and the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]]. The republic that followed was unstable, with power passing quickly to military leaders.
--- observer
The Observer notes a transfer of authority that was arranged by a compromise between a movement and an army, and not by the movement alone. A story-glue as old as the imperial title dissolved in a few months, but what replaced it had to be built while the guns were still in the hands of others.

=== event/younger-dryas
title: Younger Dryas
type: event
date: c. 10.9 ka – 9700 BCE
themes: earth-systems
src: 8e2c481c66ed
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Younger Dryas was a sudden return to near-glacial cold in the Northern Hemisphere that lasted from about 12,900 to 11,700 years ago, just before the start of the Holocene.
--- fact 1
It interrupted the warming that followed the last glacial period and ended abruptly, with several degrees of warming within decades.
--- fact 2
A leading explanation is a disruption of Atlantic Ocean circulation by a large pulse of glacial meltwater.
--- fact 3
A proposed extraterrestrial impact trigger, the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis, is disputed and not widely accepted.
--- fact 4
Its end is used to define the start of the Holocene, dated to about 11,700 years before 2000 CE.
--- fact 5
It overlaps the early stages of plant management in Southwest Asia, though the causal link is debated.
--- context
The Younger Dryas is the last major cold snap before the stable climate of the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]], within which [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] took place. It follows the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. Younger Dryas sits in the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the climate stability in which farming and cities arose was not inevitable. It was preceded by a violent flicker between cold and warm that lasted about a thousand years, and the Observer wonders how populations adapted to such swings within a few generations.
--- question 1
Did the cold snap influence the first cultivation of crops?

=== event/zanclean-flood
title: Zanclean Flood
type: event
date: c. 5.33 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: fa300039c4d1
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Zanclean flood was the refilling of the Mediterranean Sea by Atlantic water through the Gibraltar region about 5.33 million years ago. It ended the Messinian salinity crisis and coincides with the start of the Pliocene.
--- fact 1
The Mediterranean had been cut off from the Atlantic and largely evaporated; estimates of how far the sea level had fallen range from hundreds of metres to more than a kilometre.
--- fact 2
Seismic images show a buried erosional channel running from the Gulf of Cádiz through the Strait of Gibraltar into the Alboran Sea, read as the path of the flood.
--- fact 3
A 2009 study modelled a catastrophic flood with a peak discharge on the order of a hundred million cubic metres per second, refilling the basin within months to a couple of years.
--- fact 4
Other studies propose a slower or two-stage refilling, and the speed of the flood is debated.
--- fact 5
The return of the Atlantic ended the isolation of the basin and restored marine life to it.
--- context
The Zanclean Flood ends the [[event/messinian-salinity-crisis|Messinian Salinity Crisis]] within the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] and falls at the start of the Pliocene. It belongs to the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one of the largest floods in Earth's history left no human witness and survives only as a buried channel and seismic images. The largest events in the record can be the ones least available to any memory. The reading would weaken if the refilling proved to have been slow and gradual.
--- question 1
Was the refilling a single catastrophic flood or a slower, staged process?
--- question 2
How dry did the Mediterranean become before the flood?

Aisha bint Abi Bakr (about 614 to 678 CE) was a wife of the Prophet Muhammad and a daughter of the first caliph, Abu Bakr. She is credited with transmitting more than two thousand hadith and took part in the Battle of the Camel in 656.
--- fact 1
Aisha was born about 614 in Mecca to Abu Bakr and Umm Ruman. She died in Medina in July 678 (58 AH), a date that is uncertain.

In 656 she opposed Ali ibn Abi Talib, demanding punishment of the killers of the caliph Uthman. Her forces lost the Battle of the Camel near Basra. Ali pardoned her, and she withdrew from politics.
--- fact 5
Sunni tradition honors her as a scholar. Shia tradition views her critically because of her opposition to Ali.
--- context
Aisha lived in the [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]] in the early [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], in the years of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. The Observer notes that one woman's memory of a household became a source of guidance for millions, carried first by sound-code and written down generations later. Marriage into the Prophet's household made the kin-lattice a route to authority. A specialist could argue that attributions to her were shaped by later sectarian debate and that a chain of transmission does not show who spoke, which would weaken any reading that treats the reports as her own words.

Ajuricaba (died about 1727) was a leader of the Manaos people of the Rio Negro, in the Brazilian Amazon, who resisted Portuguese colonization and the enslavement of Indigenous people in the 1720s. Almost everything known about him comes from Portuguese sources.

He is remembered in Brazil as a symbol of Indigenous resistance. The city of Manaus takes its name from the Manaos people.
--- context

If the Dutch alliance in those reports is accurate, Amazonian leaders were bargaining inside an exchange-web of European rivalries rather than outside it. The reading would weaken if Manaos oral accounts described his aims differently.

=== person/al-biruni
title: Al-Biruni
type: person
date: c. 973 CE – 1048
themes: science, language
src: 9a26d7c666b5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Al-Biruni (973 to after 1048) was a scholar from Khwarazm in Central Asia who wrote on astronomy, mathematics, geography, chronology and India. His Kitab al-Hind (about 1030) is a major medieval study of Indian science, religion and customs.
--- fact 1
Al-Biruni was born in 973 in Kath, in Khwarazm in present-day Uzbekistan, and died after 1048, perhaps about 1050.
--- fact 2
After the Ghaznavid ruler Mahmud conquered Khwarazm in 1017, he was taken to Ghazni, where he worked under Mahmud and his successors.
--- fact 3
He spent years in northwestern India, learned Sanskrit, and wrote the Kitab al-Hind, describing Indian science, religion, society and customs from Sanskrit texts and conversations with Indian scholars.
--- fact 4
His Chronology of Ancient Nations (about 1000) compares the calendars and dating systems of many cultures.
--- fact 5
He calculated the radius of the Earth as about 6,340 km by measuring the angle of a mountain horizon, close to the modern value.
--- fact 6
He discussed whether the Earth rotates on its axis and treated it as a question that astronomy alone could not settle.
--- fact 7
He exchanged questions on natural philosophy with Ibn Sina.
--- context
Al-Biruni's career ran from Khwarazm in the [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]] to India during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. He corresponded with [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] and worked in a region that had also produced [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]]. His India book discusses numerals; see [[technology/zero-and-place-value-notation|Zero and Place-Value Notation]]. See [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Biruni described a foreign meaning-engine and its sciences in their own terms, learning its sound-code first and stating that his book was a report and not an argument, an aim the Observer method shares. His work shows an exchange-web of scholars linking Central Asia and South Asia that could carry ideas even as armies crossed the same routes. A specialist could argue that he read Indian tradition through Islamic and Greek categories, which would weaken the reading of his account as a neutral report.
--- question 1
How far did he read Indian thought through Islamic and Greek categories?

=== person/albrecht-durer
title: Albrecht Dürer
type: person
date: 1471 – 1528
themes: art, technology
src: b47a785a3aca
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Albrecht Dürer (21 May 1471 to 6 April 1528) was a painter and printmaker from Nuremberg who brought the Italian Renaissance north. His woodcuts and engravings, sold across Europe, made him the first artist with an international reputation built largely on prints.
--- fact 1
Dürer was born in Nuremberg, the son of a Hungarian-born goldsmith. From 1486 he was apprenticed to Michael Wolgemut, whose workshop made woodcuts for book illustration.
--- fact 2
He travelled to Venice in 1494, and again in 1505 to 1507, where Giovanni Bellini treated him as an equal. In 1498 he published the Apocalypse, 15 large woodcuts that made him famous.
--- fact 3
From 1512 Emperor Maximilian I employed him and granted him a pension. On a trip to the Netherlands in 1520 to 1521 to have it confirmed by Charles V, his diary recorded his amazement at Aztec treasures sent from Mexico.
--- fact 4
He sympathised with Martin Luther's reforms. In his last years he published treatises on measurement, fortification and human proportion.
--- fact 5
His "AD" monogram served as a trademark, and he took action against copyists who used it.
--- fact 6
Key works include Self-Portrait at the Age of Twenty-Eight (1500), Young Hare (1502), Melencolia I (1514) and The Rhinoceros (1515).
--- fact 7
His prints were copied by artists from Italy to Persia and Mughal India, and Raphael exchanged drawings with him. In the 19th century German Romantics made him a national hero.
--- context
Dürer worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]], in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the years of the [[event/protestant-reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. His diary touches the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]], and his prints reached the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a maker who saw that a picture reproduced on paper travels farther than one hung in a room. Prints turned a workshop skill into a good on the exchange-web, and his monogram worked as a trademark he was ready to defend. His self-portraits present the artist as a learned, almost god-like creator, a status-signal claimed for the maker rather than the patron. The 19th-century cult of him as a German hero is a later story-glue, not a fact about his own time.
--- question 1
How far did his prints, rather than his paintings, set the terms of his fame in his own lifetime?

=== person/alphonse-mucha
title: Alphonse Mucha
type: person
date: 1860 – 1939
themes: art, trade
src: 43da647f6bee
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Alphonse Mucha (1860 to 1939) was a Moravian-born designer whose decorative posters for Sarah Bernhardt and commercial clients defined the visual style of Art Nouveau. He resisted that label and saw his true work in a cycle of paintings on the history of the Slavic peoples.
--- fact 1
Mucha was born in Ivančice in Moravia, then part of Austria-Hungary, and worked as a theatrical scene painter before moving to Paris in 1887.
--- fact 2
In December 1894 a Paris printing house needed a poster for Sarah Bernhardt's play Gismonda at short notice over the holidays and turned to Mucha as the only designer available. Its success led Bernhardt to sign him to a six-year contract for posters, sets and costumes.
--- fact 3
In 1902 he published Documents décoratifs, a pattern book of his ornamental style for other designers and craftsmen.
--- fact 4
From about 1910 he devoted much of his career to The Slav Epic, a cycle of twenty monumental canvases on Slavic history, which he donated to the city of Prague. It is now on permanent public display there.
--- fact 5
His formula was elongated women within ornamental frames, reproduced in muted colour lithography on theatre posters, cigarette papers, bicycles and calendars, reaching shops, cafés and middle-class homes across Europe.
--- fact 6
After Nazi Germany occupied Czechoslovakia in 1939 he was arrested and interrogated by the Gestapo, and died months later, reportedly of complications following his detention.
--- fact 7
"Mucha style" is often used interchangeably with Art Nouveau, and his flowing hair and ornamental frames were borrowed by 1960s psychedelic concert posters.
--- context
Mucha worked in Paris and Prague, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and was arrested just before the outbreak of [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a designer whose fame began with a printing house's emergency, a poster needed over a holiday, so that a whole style began as a scheduling accident. Mass printing carried fine decoration into shops and cafés, while the artist thought his real work was a cycle on the history of a people, a story-glue of nationhood. The label "Art Nouveau", which he resisted, shows how a category can outrun the intention of the person it names. The link between his detention and his death is reported rather than established.
--- question 1
What caused Mucha's death in 1939, and how far was it connected to his interrogation?

=== person/amir-khusrau
title: Amir Khusrau
type: person
date: 1253 – 1325
themes: art, language, religion
src: 6535da21c322
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Amir Khusrau (1253 to 1325) was the leading court poet of the Delhi Sultanate, writing in Persian while drawing on the Indian world around him. He is regarded as the founding figure of Indo-Persian literature and is traditionally credited with founding qawwali, the devotional music of South Asian Sufi shrines.
--- fact 1
Khusrau was born in 1253 near Delhi, to a Turkic father who had migrated from Central Asia and an Indian mother. He referred to this mixed heritage throughout his work.
--- fact 2
He served as court poet under a rapid succession of Delhi sultans over more than four decades, through palace coups and dynastic changes.
--- fact 3
He wrote in Persian, the language of the Indo-Islamic courts. His five long narrative poems, dated about 1298 to 1302, answer the Khamsa of the Persian poet Nizami Ganjavi and retell Shirin and Khusrau, Layla and Majnun, and the legend of Alexander the Great.
--- fact 4
He was a devoted follower of the Sufi mystic Nizamuddin Auliya. Legend says Khusrau died within months of his master in 1325; their shrines stand together in Delhi.
--- fact 5
He is traditionally credited with founding qawwali, still performed at Sufi shrines across India, Pakistan and Bangladesh. Later legend also credits him with inventing the sitar and tabla, without firm historical evidence.
--- context
Khusrau lived in the [[culture/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer sees a poet who survived several dynasties by being useful to each, with a mixed parentage that he treated as a fact to be stated openly. Persian was the sound-code and mark-code of the courts, while the Indian setting supplied his material. What outlasted the sultans was the devotional music, which needed no palace, only a shrine and singers. Credit for instruments and forms rests on legend, so the picture of him as founder is partly the work of later admirers.
--- question 1
Which of the musical forms and instruments credited to him did he actually introduce?
