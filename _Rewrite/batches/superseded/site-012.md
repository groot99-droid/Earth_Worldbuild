=== event/boxer-rebellion

date: 1899 – 1901

mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Boxer Rebellion (1899 to 1901) was an anti-foreign, anti-Christian uprising in northern China, supported by the Qing court, that ended with defeat by an eight-nation alliance.
--- fact 1
The Boxers (the 'Righteous and Harmonious Fists') attacked foreign missionaries and Chinese Christians.
--- fact 2
The Empress Dowager Cixi eventually backed the movement and declared war on foreign powers.
--- fact 3
An eight-nation alliance relieved the besieged legations in Beijing.
--- fact 4
The Boxer Protocol of 1901 imposed a large indemnity on China.
--- context
The uprising followed the pressures begun by the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]] and weakened the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]]. See [[person/cixi|Cixi]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a movement that promised its followers invulnerability to bullets was defeated by machine guns. Belief and technology met in the most direct way possible.
--- question 1
How much of the Boxer movement was popular, and how much directed by the court?

=== event/breakup-of-pangaea
title: Breakup of Pangaea
type: event
date: c. 200 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 5031d86703ff
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pangaea, the supercontinent that had assembled by about 300 million years ago, began to split about 200 million years ago, opening the Atlantic Ocean and reshaping the map of the world.
--- fact 1
Rifting began in the Late Triassic to Early Jurassic, and the Central Atlantic opened first.
--- fact 2
Large volcanic outpourings, the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province, coincided with the Triassic-Jurassic extinction, about 200 million years ago.
--- fact 3
The South Atlantic opened much later, in the Early Cretaceous.
--- fact 4
Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912; plate tectonics gained wide acceptance in the 1960s.
--- fact 5
Continental motion continues today.
--- context
The breakup of Pangaea isolated continental faunas and set the stage for the later biogeography of the [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]] and mammals. Its opening overlaps the [[event/triassic-jurassic-extinction|Triassic-Jurassic Extinction]]. Breakup of Pangaea sits in the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the continents the species draws its borders on are a temporary arrangement, moving at about the speed fingernails grow. Human claim-lines are drawn on a surface that is itself in motion.
--- question 1
How closely was the timing of rifting tied to the end-Triassic extinction?

=== event/british-raj
title: British Raj
type: event
date: 1858 – 1947
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 9b542dcbf7a9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The British Raj was the period of direct British rule over the Indian subcontinent, from 1858 to 1947. It began when the Crown took over from the East India Company after the Rebellion of 1857 and ended with the independence and partition of India and Pakistan.
--- fact 1
The Government of India Act, passed on 2 August 1858, transferred authority over India from the East India Company to the British Crown, as a response to the Indian Rebellion of 1857.
--- fact 2
The Company lost all its administrative powers, and its territories and armed forces passed to the Crown. The Company itself was formally dissolved by an Act of Parliament in 1874.
--- fact 3
The Act created a cabinet-level Secretary of State for India and a fifteen-member Council of India in London. In Calcutta the Governor-General became head of the Government of India and was called the Viceroy.
--- fact 4
The Raj lasted until the independence of India and Pakistan in 1947.
--- context
The Raj followed the [[event/indian-rebellion-of-1857|Indian Rebellion of 1857]] and ended with the [[event/partition-of-india|Partition of India]] in 1947. It included the [[event/bengal-famine-of-1943|Bengal Famine of 1943]] and a long nationalist movement associated with [[person/mahatma-gandhi|Mahatma Gandhi]]. It succeeded the earlier rule of the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] over much of the subcontinent.
--- observer
The Observer notes a change of ownership: an enterprise that had governed as a company was replaced by a state that governed through a ministry, and the change followed a rebellion that both sides remember as an atrocity committed by the other. A rule of few over many depended on shared administration, and ended when that administration was no longer accepted.
--- question 1
How should the Raj's effects on the economy, famine, and infrastructure be weighed against each other, and by whose standards?
--- question 2
How did Indian institutions and elites shape the Raj rather than only being shaped by it?

=== event/cambrian-explosion
title: Cambrian Explosion
type: event
date: 538.8 – 520 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: fe783ed5d992
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Cambrian Explosion is the relatively rapid appearance, over roughly 13 to 25 million years starting 538.8 million years ago, of most major animal body plans in the fossil record.
--- fact 1
Animals with hard parts, such as shells and exoskeletons, appear widely.
--- fact 2
Famous fossil sites include the Burgess Shale in Canada and Chengjiang in China.
--- fact 3
Ediacaran precursors show that animals existed before the Cambrian.
--- fact 4
Oxygen levels, ecological arms races, and developmental genetics are all proposed contributors.
--- context
The event defines the base of the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]]. Signature animals include [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]].
--- observer
The Observer sees a burst of experimentation with body design, followed by long conservatism. Innovation clusters, and so does its aftermath.
--- question 1
Is the 'explosion' partly an artifact of fossil preservation?

=== event/carboniferous-coal-forests
title: Carboniferous Coal Forests
type: event
date: 323 – 299 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 2d1da0273c7d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Carboniferous coal forests were swamp forests of wet tropical lowlands, mostly in the later Carboniferous, about 323 to 299 million years ago. Their buried plant matter became much of the world's coal.
--- fact 1
The forests grew mainly on the lowlands of the northern continent of Euramerica and in parts of China, and included tree-sized club-mosses such as Lepidodendron, tree ferns, giant horsetails and early conifers.
--- fact 2
Repeated rises and falls of sea level, linked to the Late Paleozoic Ice Age, drowned and re-exposed the swamps and stacked many separate coal seams.
--- fact 3
At Joggins in Nova Scotia, hollow fossil tree stumps contain remains of early amphibians and reptiles.
--- fact 4
Burial of plant carbon lowered atmospheric carbon dioxide and probably raised oxygen. Coal from these rocks fuelled the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] in Britain and other regions.
--- fact 5
About 305 million years ago the wet tropical forests collapsed into isolated patches as the climate became drier and cooler, an event linked to declines among amphibians and the rise of reptiles.
--- context
The Carboniferous coal forests follow the [[event/devonian-forests-and-the-rise-of-trees|Devonian Forests and the Rise of Trees]] and belong to the [[era/carboniferous-period|Carboniferous Period]]. Their end coincides with the [[event/assembly-of-pangaea|Assembly of Pangaea]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer records the difference in rates: the forests stored their carbon over tens of millions of years, and the species has burned much of it in a few centuries. The reading concerns rates only, and it would not be affected by the debate over why so much coal formed in this period.
--- question 1
Why did so much coal accumulate in this period and not in others?
--- question 2
What caused the collapse of the tropical forests about 305 million years ago?

The collapse ended the height of the [[culture/maya|Maya]] city-states in the southern lowlands and shifted power to the north, as at [[place/chichen-itza|Chichen Itza]]. === event/closure-of-the-isthmus-of-panama
title: Closure of the Isthmus of Panama
type: event
date: c. 3 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: b6f1821bc2b2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Isthmus of Panama, traditionally dated to about 3 million years ago, joined North and South America, closed a sea passage between the Pacific and Atlantic, and triggered a major exchange of land animals.
--- fact 1
The land bridge allowed the Great American Biotic Interchange, in which mammals crossed between the continents.
--- fact 2
Ground sloths, armadillos, and opossums moved north; cats, dogs, horses, and other groups moved south.
--- fact 3
The closure ended the exchange of Pacific and Atlantic waters at low latitudes and is thought to have strengthened Atlantic circulation.
--- fact 4
The timing is debated: some studies argue for a much earlier start to the land connection, while others support about 3 million years ago.
--- fact 5
Northern glaciation began at about the same time.
--- context
The isthmus's closure links ocean circulation, climate, and animal geography in the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]], shortly before the earliest members of the genus Homo such as [[species/homo-habilis|Homo habilis]]. Closure of the Isthmus of Panama sits in the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a strip of land a few dozen kilometers wide altered the fates of thousands of species on two continents. The same narrow crossing would later matter to humans as a migration route and, much later, as a canal.
--- question 1
When did the land bridge become continuous, and how did that relate to ocean changes?

The exchange underpinned the growth of European empires and the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]].

The exchange opens the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and ended a long separation of the two hemispheres. See [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]], [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] and [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]].

=== event/deccan-traps-eruptions
title: Deccan Traps Eruptions
type: event
date: 66.3 – 65.5 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 8672a231f276
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Deccan Traps eruptions were a huge outpouring of lava in west-central India, with the main phases at about 66.3 to 65.5 million years ago. They overlap the end-Cretaceous mass extinction, and their role in it is debated.
--- fact 1
The Deccan Traps are among the largest volcanic provinces on Earth; the lava flows pile up to more than two kilometres thick in the Western Ghats and once covered well over a million square kilometres.
--- fact 2
Radiometric dating places most of the volume within about a million years around the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary, with large pulses both before and after the Chicxulub impact. The relative timing is debated.
--- fact 3
The eruptions released carbon dioxide and sulfur gases, which could have caused warming, cooling episodes and acid rain, and stressed ecosystems before the impact.
--- fact 4
Some recent studies conclude that the impact, not the volcanism, was the main driver of the extinction; the question remains open.
--- fact 5
The eruptions are linked to the Réunion hotspot, which India passed over as it drifted north.
--- context
The Deccan Traps Eruptions fall at the end of the [[era/cretaceous-period|Cretaceous Period]] and overlap the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]. === event/devonian-forests-and-the-rise-of-trees
title: Devonian Forests and the Rise of Trees
type: event
date: 385 – 359 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 3474fe159fa6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Devonian forests were the first forests on Earth, appearing about 385 million years ago and spreading through the rest of the period. Trees evolved independently in several plant lineages, and their roots and litter changed soils, rivers and the carbon cycle.
--- fact 1
The Gilboa fossil forest in New York, about 385 million years old, holds some of the oldest known fossil trees, including cladoxylopsids with hollow trunks.
--- fact 2
Archaeopteris, a tree with woody trunks and fern-like foliage that grew more than ten metres tall, formed forests across several continents in the Late Devonian.
--- fact 3
Trees arose independently in several plant lineages during the Devonian and Carboniferous, including club-mosses, relatives of horsetails and ancestors of seed plants.
--- fact 4
Deep roots speeded the chemical weathering of rock and built soils, and atmospheric carbon dioxide fell over the Devonian, which may have contributed to later cooling.
--- fact 5
Some researchers link the spread of forests to nutrient runoff and ocean oxygen loss at the time of the [[event/late-devonian-extinction|Late Devonian Extinction]]; the link is debated.
--- context
The Devonian forests are a central part of the [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]] and lead into the [[event/carboniferous-coal-forests|Carboniferous Coal Forests]]. They belong to the [[era/devonian-period|Devonian Period]] and the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the tree form was reached by unrelated plant groups, which suggests that some solutions are strongly favoured by the problem, here competition for light, and are not tied to one lineage. The reading would weaken if the different tree forms proved to share a single evolutionary origin.
--- question 1
How large was the effect of forests on Devonian carbon dioxide and climate?
--- question 2
How much did forests contribute to the Late Devonian extinction?

=== event/earliest-continental-crust
title: Earliest Continental Crust
type: event
date: 4.4 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: 59b5945ccf72
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The earliest continental crust is the first granite-like crust, lighter than the ocean floor, that could stand above sea level. It may have formed before 4.0 billion years ago, but almost none of it survives as rock, so its extent and timing are uncertain.
--- fact 1
Some researchers read the chemistry of Jack Hills zircons, up to about 4.4 billion years old, as showing granite-like magmas, a sign of evolved crust; others dispute that reading. See [[place/jack-hills-zircons|Jack Hills Zircons]].
--- fact 2
The Acasta Gneiss in northwestern Canada, about 4.0 billion years old, is the oldest known intact rock body. Some studies date rocks of the Nuvvuagittuq belt in Quebec to as much as about 4.3 billion years, but that age is disputed.
--- fact 3
Continental crust is less dense than oceanic crust and is made by partial melting of older rocks. Because it is buoyant it survives recycling far better than the ocean floor.
--- fact 4
How fast the continents grew is debated: some models place most of the crust in existence by about 3 billion years ago, and others favour slower growth.
--- context
The earliest continental crust belongs to the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] and continues into the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]], when the first stable continental cores formed. It is tied to the [[event/formation-of-the-oceans|Formation of the Oceans]] and to the question of the [[event/onset-of-plate-tectonics|Onset of Plate Tectonics]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the earliest crust is known almost entirely from grains that survived billions of years of recycling. The first half billion years of the record is thin by nature, and the absence of rock is not evidence of the absence of crust. The reading would weaken if new discoveries of ancient rock gave a very different picture.
--- question 1
How much continental crust existed in the Hadean?
--- question 2
Are the oldest claimed rock ages, such as those from Nuvvuagittuq, reliable?

=== event/early-hominin-dispersals-from-africa
title: Early Hominin Dispersals from Africa
type: event
date: 2.1 Ma – 500 ka
themes: earth-systems, technology
src: 90d7376a2d67
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hominins first left Africa between about 2.1 and 1.8 million years ago and spread across Eurasia in several dispersals over the next million years. The oldest hominin fossils outside Africa, at Dmanisi in Georgia, are about 1.85 to 1.78 million years old, and stone tools from the Loess Plateau of China are about 2.1 million years old.
--- fact 1
Dmanisi has yielded five well-preserved skulls and many body bones, dated to about 1.85 to 1.77 million years ago, with simple Oldowan-style stone tools.
--- fact 2
The skulls have small brains, about 546 to 775 cubic centimeters. A 2013 study argued that their variation fits one population, which doubts separate early Homo species.
--- fact 3
Stone tools at Shangchen on the Chinese Loess Plateau date from about 2.1 to 1.3 million years ago. No hominin fossils were found there, so the toolmakers are unidentified.
--- fact 4
Homo erectus was on Java by about 1.5 to 1.3 million years ago, and hominins were in Spain by about 1.4 to 1.2 million years ago and in England about 0.9 million years ago.
--- fact 5
Whether the first dispersers were Homo erectus is debated; small brains and simple tools at Dmanisi suggest they left before that form was typical.
--- context
The early dispersals follow the [[event/emergence-of-the-genus-homo|Emergence of the Genus Homo]] and the [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]] and belong to the [[era/lower-paleolithic|Lower Paleolithic]]. They center on [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]]. [[species/homo-floresiensis|Homo floresiensis]] on Flores may descend from an early dispersal; its ancestry is debated. Later dispersals are separate; see [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the first hominins outside Africa had small brains and simple stone tools, which suggests that leaving Africa did not require the large brain of later humans. The Observer reads dispersal as repeated range expansion into new habitats, of which the species' later spread is one more instance. The reading would weaken if the Dmanisi hominins proved an isolated dead end.
--- question 1
Who made the 2.1-million-year-old stone tools at Shangchen?
--- question 2
Did the first hominins to leave Africa belong to Homo erectus or to an earlier form?

=== event/emergence-of-the-genus-homo
title: Emergence of the Genus Homo
type: event
date: 2.8 – 1.8 Ma
themes: earth-systems, technology
src: 274629825d1e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The genus Homo emerged in East Africa between about 2.8 and 1.8 million years ago from an Australopithecus-like ancestor. The oldest fossil assigned to Homo is a partial jaw from Ledi-Geraru, Ethiopia, about 2.8 million years old, but fossils from this interval are sparse and disputed.
--- fact 1
The Ledi-Geraru jaw, LD 350-1, is dated to about 2.80 to 2.75 million years ago and was assigned to Homo in 2015. Some later analyses stress its resemblance to Australopithecus afarensis.
--- fact 2
A maxilla from Hadar, Ethiopia, about 2.3 million years old, and fossils attributed to Homo habilis and Homo rudolfensis from Olduvai Gorge and Lake Turkana, roughly 2.4 to 1.4 million years old, are assigned to Homo by many researchers. Some place the latter two outside the genus.
--- fact 3
Homo erectus appears about 1.9 to 1.8 million years ago with a larger body and brain; see [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]].
--- fact 4
Oldowan tools appear about 2.6 million years ago, and older tools from Lomekwi, Kenya, are about 3.3 million years old. Australopithecus and early Homo overlapped, so the earliest toolmaker is uncertain.
--- fact 5
East African climate grew drier and more variable across this interval; a link to the origin of Homo is debated.
--- context
Homo emerges after [[species/australopithecus-afarensis|Australopithecus afarensis]] and the [[event/divergence-of-the-hominin-lineage|Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]], during the [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]]. It runs through the early [[era/lower-paleolithic|Lower Paleolithic]] with the [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]] and [[species/homo-habilis|Homo habilis]], and leads to the [[event/early-hominin-dispersals-from-africa|Early Hominin Dispersals from Africa]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' own genus has no clean starting line: a jaw at 2.8 million years, scattered skulls near 2 million, and a well-defined body plan only by 1.9 million. The Observer reads this as slow, patchy change in a lineage already walking upright, with no single moment to call a beginning. The reading would weaken if a well-dated skeleton showed a rapid change.

Is the Ledi-Geraru jaw an early Homo or a late Australopithecus?
