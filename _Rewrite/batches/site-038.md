=== culture/buddhism
title: Buddhism
type: culture
date: c. 500 BCE
themes: religion
src: 39d2e256e9fb
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Buddhism began in northeastern India between the sixth and fourth centuries BCE with the teaching of Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, and spread across Asia into several major traditions, teaching a path out of suffering through insight and practice rather than through a creator god.
--- fact 1
Siddhartha Gautama, born a member of a ruling family in the region of present-day Nepal and India, is said to have reached enlightenment and began teaching in his mid-thirties.
--- fact 2
His core teaching, the Four Noble Truths, describes suffering, its cause in craving, its end, and a path to that end, aiming at release from the cycle of rebirth, called nirvana.
--- fact 3
The faith spread through missionary activity, trade routes and royal patronage, notably under the Mauryan emperor Ashoka.
--- fact 4
It developed into three major living traditions: Theravada, dominant in Sri Lanka and mainland Southeast Asia; Mahayana, dominant in China, Japan, Korea and Vietnam; and Vajrayana, dominant in Tibet, Bhutan and Mongolia.
--- fact 5
Buddhism has roughly 500 million adherents today, about 6 percent of the world's population.
--- context
Buddhism arose in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] with the teaching of [[person/siddhartha-gautama|Siddhartha Gautama]], part of the broader [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]] of new philosophical and religious movements, and spread widely under patrons such as [[person/ashoka|Ashoka]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that this meaning-engine spread across a continent not primarily by conquest but by teaching, trade and a ruler's patronage, and diversified into traditions different enough in practice that observers sometimes debate whether to treat them as one religion. A specialist could argue that Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana differ enough in text, practice and cosmology that grouping them under one name obscures more than it reveals, which would weaken any single account of "Buddhist belief."
--- question 1
How much unity of belief and practice exists across its major traditions today?
--- question 2
How much of its early spread depended on state patronage such as Ashoka's, versus independent teaching and trade?

=== culture/celts
title: Celts
type: culture
date: c. 800 – 50 BCE
themes: war, language
src: 4d33f75f6191
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Celts were related peoples across Iron Age Europe, sharing related languages and, later, the La Tène artistic style, without forming a single state. Rome absorbed most continental Celts, and Celtic language and culture lasted longest in Ireland and parts of Britain.
--- fact 1
The Celts were many related tribal groups, including the Gauls of mainland Europe, speaking branches of the Celtic language family such as Gaulish.
--- fact 2
The La Tène culture, known for swirling metalwork, flourished from about 450 BCE until Roman conquest, following the earlier Hallstatt culture.
--- fact 3
Druids held major religious and political influence in many Celtic societies before Roman conquest, though their role varied by region and period.
--- fact 4
Rome attacked the Arverni in 125 BCE, and Julius Caesar completed the conquest of Gaul by 50 BCE despite resistance under leaders such as Vercingetorix.
--- fact 5
Rome targeted the druids directly, and continental and southern British Celts were largely assimilated, while Celtic culture lasted longer in Ireland and northern Britain.
--- context
The Celts inhabited much of [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] before conquest by the [[culture/romans|Romans]], in a period of wider contact across the ancient world that included [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that "Celtic" names a shared language family and later decorative style across many separate societies, not one people with one story-glue, and that Rome's targeting of the druids suggests it saw their meaning-engine as a source of resistance. Celtic identity lasted where terrain made conquest harder, in Ireland and the far north. A specialist could argue that the umbrella label was applied more consistently by later scholars than by the peoples themselves, which would weaken any reading of the Celts as one self-aware group.
--- question 1
How much shared identity did Celtic-speaking peoples recognize among themselves?
--- question 2
What was the druids' actual political role, and how real was the threat Rome saw in them?

=== culture/cherokee
title: Cherokee
type: culture
date: c. 1540
themes: language, kinship
src: c9331c0e8fc5
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Cherokee are a people native to the southeastern United States whose nation adopted a written syllabary in the 1820s and survived the forced removal known as the Trail of Tears, continuing today as several federally recognized nations including the Eastern Band in North Carolina.
--- fact 1
At European contact in the sixteenth century the Cherokee held a homeland spanning parts of present-day North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama and Tennessee.
--- fact 2
Sequoyah completed an 85-character syllabary for Cherokee in 1821, after twelve years of work, enabling mass literacy within a short time.
--- fact 3
In 1838 and 1839 United States troops under General Winfield Scott forcibly removed most Cherokee to territory west of the Mississippi, a journey remembered as the Trail of Tears, in which many died.
--- fact 4
Some Cherokee avoided removal by hiding in the North Carolina mountains or claiming state citizenship. Their descendants formed the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, based on the Qualla Boundary.
--- fact 5
The Cherokee Nation in Oklahoma and the Eastern Band are among the largest Native American nations in the United States today.
--- context
The Cherokee homeland lay in [[region/north-america|North America]]. [[person/sequoyah|Sequoyah]]'s syllabary gave the nation a written mark-code shortly before the [[event/indian-removal-and-the-trail-of-tears|Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a nation given a new mark-code for its own language in the 1820s kept records and petitions with it through the removal that tried to end its presence in its homeland. The split into an Eastern Band and a western nation shows one people's story continuing along two claim-lines after a single act of state violence. A specialist could argue that literacy's role in Cherokee survival is overstated relative to land, kinship networks and legal strategy, which would weaken a reading that credits the syllabary alone.
--- question 1
How much did literacy, as opposed to other factors, shape Cherokee resistance to removal?
--- question 2
How do the histories of the Eastern Band and the Oklahoma nation compare since their separation?

=== culture/christianity
title: Christianity
type: culture
date: c. 30 CE
themes: religion
src: 9afe81445806
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Christianity began among followers of Jesus of Nazareth in first-century Roman Judea and spread through the Mediterranean world to become the most widely practiced religion in the world.
--- fact 1
Jesus of Nazareth was executed by Roman authority in Jerusalem, most likely between 29 and 33 CE. His followers believed he had risen from death, and this belief became the basis of the new movement.
--- fact 2
Paul of Tarsus, a former persecutor of the movement, became its most active early missionary, founding communities among non-Jews across the Roman Empire and shaping much of its early theology.
--- fact 3
Within about a century, Christian communities existed across Greece, Syria, Armenia, North Africa and beyond.
--- fact 4
The faith split into Eastern Orthodoxy and the Catholic Church in the Great Schism of 1054, and the Reformation of 1517 produced Protestant churches, giving three main branches today.
--- fact 5
Christianity has about 2.4 billion adherents, roughly a third of the world's population, the Catholic Church alone counting about 1.3 billion members.
--- context
Christianity arose in the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] within the [[culture/romans|Roman Empire]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], later becoming the state religion of the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] and, centuries afterward, existing alongside the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] in the same region. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a movement built around the death of one man became, through a convert who had once opposed it, a story-glue that crossed the ordinary claim-lines of ethnicity within the Roman world within a single century. Two later splits, in 1054 and 1517, show the same meaning-engine repeatedly dividing over authority and practice while its central claims persisted. A specialist could argue that its early rapid growth owed as much to Roman roads and common language as to the message itself, which would weaken a reading that credits the spread to doctrine alone.
--- question 1
How much of Christianity's early spread reflects the message versus the infrastructure of the Roman Empire it grew within?
--- question 2
How do its many branches today compare in how they trace authority back to the earliest community?

=== culture/confucianism
title: Confucianism
type: culture
date: c. 551 BCE
themes: religion, kinship
src: 2b6b02e6d902
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Confucianism is a Chinese tradition of ethics and social order built on the teaching of Confucius (551 to 479 BCE), which became the official ideology of imperial China under the Han dynasty and shaped government and family life across East Asia.
--- fact 1
Confucius was an itinerant teacher during China's Warring States period who sought but largely failed to gain lasting influence as a government adviser in his own lifetime.
--- fact 2
His disciples compiled his sayings after his death as the Analects, the central text of the tradition.
--- fact 3
A key teaching, filial piety, extends genuine respect and care for parents into a model for loyalty within the wider political order.
--- fact 4
Under the Han dynasty, Confucianism became the state's official ideology, and an imperial examination system tested officials on Confucian texts, a practice that shaped Chinese government for centuries.
--- fact 5
The tradition spread beyond China to Vietnam, Korea and Japan, shaping their systems of government and social norms, including their own examination systems modeled on China's.
--- context
Confucianism developed in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] through the teaching of [[person/confucius|Confucius]], part of the wider [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]], and became state orthodoxy under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a teacher who failed to secure influence in his own time became, centuries after his death, the ancestor-weight behind an empire's entire method of selecting officials, with filial duty inside the kin-lattice serving as the template for loyalty to the state itself. A specialist could argue that the Han state's adoption of Confucianism reflects the state's own need for a stabilizing ideology as much as the teaching's inherent merit, which would weaken a reading of its rise as owed to its ideas alone.
--- question 1
How much did the Han state's adoption of Confucianism serve the state's own interests, apart from the teaching's content?
--- question 2
How uniformly was Confucianism practiced across China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam?

=== culture/daoism
title: Daoism
type: culture
date: c. 600 BCE
themes: religion
src: ed8c8104e0a6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Daoism is a Chinese philosophical and religious tradition centered on living in harmony with the Dao, "the Way," traditionally traced to the sage Laozi and later developed into organized religious movements alongside its philosophical school.
--- fact 1
Laozi, traditionally placed in the sixth century BCE, is credited as the founding figure, though modern scholars think the text attributed to him, the Dao De Jing, was likely compiled by multiple hands after his time.
--- fact 2
Zhuangzi (about 369 to 286 BCE) was a major later interpreter, and the book bearing his name is considered even more wide-ranging than the Dao De Jing.
--- fact 3
Its central principle, wuwei, "nonaction," describes spontaneity and noninterference with the natural order rather than passive idleness.
--- fact 4
Organized religious Daoism began later, marked by the Celestial Masters movement founded when Zhang Daoling reported revelations from a deified Laozi in 142 CE.
--- fact 5
Daoist philosophy and religious practice both continued alongside Confucianism in Chinese life, sometimes overlapping with folk religion, making a precise adherent count difficult to give.
--- context
Daoism developed in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] through the teaching attributed to [[person/laozi|Laozi]], continuing as a distinct tradition alongside [[culture/confucianism|Confucianism]] into the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and after. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a philosophy built around noninterference and yielding became, centuries after its founding figure's traditional dates, the ancestor-weight behind an organized religious movement claiming revelation from that same figure now treated as a deity, a shift from teaching to meaning-engine. A specialist could argue that "Daoism" groups together a philosophical school and a family of religious movements with looser connections than the shared name implies, which would weaken any single account of Daoist belief.
--- question 1
How much continuity exists between the philosophical Daoism of Laozi and Zhuangzi and the later religious movements that claimed their authority?
--- question 2
How separable is Daoist practice from Chinese folk religion in everyday life?

=== culture/franks
title: Franks
type: culture
date: c. 250 – 987 CE
themes: war, religion
src: a72f46caf1ff
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Franks were a Germanic people who moved into Roman Gaul and built one of early medieval Europe's most powerful kingdoms, first under the Merovingian dynasty of Clovis I and later under the Carolingians, whose most famous ruler was Charlemagne.
--- fact 1
The Franks appear in Roman records in the third century CE on the lower Rhine and expanded into Gaul as Roman power weakened.
--- fact 2
Clovis I of the Merovingian dynasty defeated the last major Roman official in Gaul in 486 and had united the Frankish tribes by about 501.
--- fact 3
Clovis converted to Catholic Christianity, and his people's adoption of it helped unite the Franks and set them apart from neighboring Germanic kingdoms that followed other Christian traditions.
--- fact 4
The Merovingians ruled until the eighth century, when Pepin the Short, with papal support, founded the Carolingian dynasty.
--- fact 5
The Carolingian kingdom peaked under Charlemagne and later divided among his descendants. Its territories became the basis of medieval France, Germany and the Holy Roman Empire (not yet a vault note).
--- context
The Franks built their kingdom in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the early [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], filling the space left by the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]]. Their empire under [[person/charlemagne|Charlemagne]] was an ancestor of the Holy Roman Empire (not yet a vault note). See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler's personal conversion to his Roman subjects' majority faith became story-glue that welded conquerors and conquered into one people, setting the Franks apart from rival kingdoms that kept a minority creed. Two centuries later a change of dynasty was legitimized by the same meaning-engine, papal blessing replacing a discredited bloodline. A specialist could argue that Clovis's conversion served political advantage with his Gallo-Roman subjects as much as belief, which would weaken a reading of it as faith alone.
--- question 1
How much of Clovis's conversion reflects conviction versus calculated alliance with his Gallo-Roman subjects?
--- question 2
How unified was Frankish identity across the peoples the Merovingians and Carolingians ruled?

=== culture/hinduism
title: Hinduism
type: culture
date: c. 1500 BCE
themes: religion
src: 49a1cc61684f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Hinduism is a family of religious traditions native to South Asia that grew out of the ancient Vedic religion, has no single founder or founding event, and today counts about 1.2 billion adherents, most of them in India.
--- fact 1
Its earliest texts, the Vedas, were composed roughly in the mid-second millennium BCE, and its philosophical Upanishads later explored ideas of the soul and the cycle of rebirth.
--- fact 2
Rather than one founder, it developed over centuries by fusing regional and cultural traditions, so no single origin date or figure can be given.
--- fact 3
A central concept, brahman, describes an ultimate reality identified with the individual soul, or atman.
--- fact 4
Its two largest branches are Vaishnavism, centered on devotion to Vishnu, and Shaivism, centered on Shiva, each containing many further traditions.
--- fact 5
About 1.2 billion people practice Hinduism today, roughly 15 percent of the world's population, with about 1.1 billion living in India.
--- context
Hinduism's roots reach into the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]] and the [[event/vedic-period|Vedic Period]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], and the tradition was later patronized and contested under states such as the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a tradition without a single founder or a single scripture managing to organize a sixth of humanity's population under one broad name suggests story-glue that operates by accretion rather than by a single unifying claim, closer to a family of related meaning-engines than one system. A specialist could argue that grouping such varied regional practices under one word "Hinduism," itself coined by outsiders, imposes a unity the traditions did not claim for themselves, which would weaken any single account of "what Hindus believe."
--- question 1
How far can practices as different as regional Vaishnava and Shaiva traditions be treated as one religion?
--- question 2
How much of the modern term "Hinduism" reflects a category imposed from outside rather than one adherents used for themselves?

=== culture/islam
title: Islam
type: culture
date: c. 610 CE
themes: religion
src: 2b1947b2269d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Islam is the religion founded by Muhammad in seventh-century Arabia, centered on the Quran as the word of God and on the practices set out in its five pillars, and today the second-largest religion in the world.
--- fact 1
Muslims hold that Muhammad, an Arabian merchant, began receiving revelations from God around 610 CE, later compiled as the Quran, and that he united the tribes of Arabia under the new faith before his death in 632.
--- fact 2
A dispute over his successor split his followers soon after his death: most accepted the elected caliphs (becoming Sunni), while a smaller group held that leadership belonged to his family through his son-in-law Ali (becoming Shia).
--- fact 3
The faith spread rapidly under the early caliphates, then under empires such as the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates, from Spain to Central and South Asia within a few centuries.
--- fact 4
Sunni Muslims make up roughly 85 percent of the world's estimated 1.9 billion Muslims, and Shia Muslims about 15 percent, with Shia forming the majority in Iran, Iraq, Bahrain and Azerbaijan.
--- context
Islam began in the [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]] with [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] at the start of the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], part of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], and spread through empires including the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]] and [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a succession dispute settled within a generation of the founder's death created a claim-line, Sunni against Shia, that fourteen centuries of later history have not erased, showing how a single kin-lattice question about legitimate leadership can outlast the empires built around it. A specialist could argue that many local and political factors, not the original dispute alone, kept the split alive in specific regions, which would weaken a reading of the divide as a single continuous grievance.
--- question 1
How much of the continuing Sunni-Shia distinction reflects the original succession dispute versus later political history?
--- question 2
What let the faith spread so quickly across such different regions within its first century?

=== culture/jainism
title: Jainism
type: culture
date: c. 599 BCE
themes: religion
src: d5f4072b373c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Jainism is an ancient Indian religion centered on non-violence toward all living things, taught by a line of teachers called tirthankaras and traditionally traced to Mahavira in the sixth century BCE, though the tradition itself holds its teaching to be eternal.
--- fact 1
Mahavira, traditionally dated about 599 to 527 BCE, is remembered as the twenty-fourth and final tirthankara of the current cosmic cycle, and is sometimes called the founder of Jainism as practiced today. Jain tradition itself considers its teaching timeless rather than founded by any one person.
--- fact 2
Mahavira reportedly renounced a noble upbringing at about 30 and practiced severe asceticism for over a decade before reaching what Jains call perfect knowledge.
--- fact 3
Its central principle, ahimsa, non-violence toward all living beings, is practiced to an unusual degree by Jain monks and nuns, some of whom sweep their path and cover their mouths to avoid harming small creatures.
--- fact 4
The tradition split into two main branches, Digambara and Svetambara, by about the fourth century CE, differing over practices such as monastic clothing.
--- fact 5
Roughly four to five million Jains live today, the great majority in India, with smaller communities in East Africa, North America and Europe.
--- context
Jainism developed in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]], in the same broad period as [[person/siddhartha-gautama|Siddhartha Gautama]]'s teaching and the wider [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]] of new religious movements. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a small community has sustained one of the most demanding versions of nonviolence recorded among the world's meaning-engines for over two thousand years, treating harm to the smallest creature as morally weighty. A specialist could argue that Mahavira's dates and the very idea of Jainism as a distinct tradition beginning with him are themselves later constructions over a longer, less clearly bounded history of ascetic teaching, which would weaken any precise founding date.
--- question 1
How much of the traditional account of Mahavira's life reflects history versus later hagiography?
--- question 2
How consistent has the practice of ahimsa been across Jain communities and periods?

=== culture/judaism
title: Judaism
type: culture
date: c. 1800 BCE
themes: religion
src: 05829dcf0cf3
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Judaism is the religion and communal tradition of the Jewish people, tracing its origins to the patriarch Abraham and the covenant given to Moses at Sinai, and organized for most of its history around a body of law and commentary rather than a central authority.
--- fact 1
Tradition dates Abraham, regarded as the first Hebrew and forefather of the Jewish people, to about 2000 to 1800 BCE, though this is traditional rather than documented history.
--- fact 2
Its founding text is the Torah, the five books attributed to Moses, later joined by other scriptures collected as the Hebrew Bible.
--- fact 3
The Second Temple in Jerusalem was destroyed by Rome in 70 CE. Without a central temple, rabbis took over interpreting the Torah, producing extensive commentary later compiled as the Talmud.
--- fact 4
Today's main movements, Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and Reconstructionist, range from traditional to progressive in how they apply Jewish law.
--- fact 5
Worldwide adherents numbered about 15 million as of the early 2020s, most living in Israel and the United States.
--- context
Judaism's traditional origins lie in the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], among the empires of ancient Mesopotamia such as the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]], and its history includes conquest by the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] and later the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] in the same region. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the destruction of a single building in 70 CE redirected an entire tradition's meaning-engine from temple sacrifice toward text and interpretation, a shift in memory-outsourcing that let the community persist without a capital. Adherents hold the tradition's core claims as historical and covenantal; a specialist could argue that its earliest patriarchal accounts describe an ancestor-weight built up over centuries of retelling rather than a documented individual, which would weaken any confident dating of its earliest layer.
--- question 1
How much of the patriarchal narrative reflects documented history versus later tradition?
--- question 2
How did the loss of the Temple change the tradition's practice over the following centuries?

--- summary
The Kalinago, called Caribs by Europeans, are an Indigenous people of the Lesser Antilles who resisted colonization for nearly two centuries after Columbus, and today live mainly in the Kalinago Territory on Dominica.
--- fact 1
The Kalinago descend from earlier inhabitants of the Lesser Antilles and were there before Columbus reached the region in 1493.

They gained a reputation as effective resistance fighters, and their raids helped keep Europeans from permanent settlement on some islands for close to two centuries.

--- fact 4
France claimed Dominica in 1635, but Kalinago resistance blocked permanent settlement for a time. Catholic missionaries arriving in 1642 recorded Kalinago language and life.
--- fact 5
Kalinago raids on plantations on neighboring islands continued into the eighteenth century, and their descendants remain in the Kalinago Territory today.
--- context
The Kalinago inhabited the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]] into the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], sharing the region with the [[culture/taino|Taino]] and resisting the disruptions of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] longer than most neighbors. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that difficult terrain, more than any single tactic, let one Caribbean people hold off colonization for two centuries after most of the region had fallen, then drew refugees from neighboring islands, making a stronghold a shared last refuge. Their presence today contradicts accounts that treat the Caribbean's Indigenous peoples as vanished. A specialist could argue that European rivalries left gaps in colonization as much as Kalinago strength did, which would weaken a reading that credits resistance alone.
--- question 1
How much of the Kalinago's long resistance reflected their own strength versus European powers being divided or distracted?
--- question 2

=== culture/manichaeism
title: Manichaeism
type: culture
date: c. 240 CE – 1300
themes: religion
src: 349e0eee4a93
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Manichaeism was a dualistic religion founded in third-century Persia by Mani, teaching a cosmic struggle between light and darkness. It spread from North Africa to China within four centuries and died out under persecution by the 14th century.
--- fact 1
Mani, born about 216 CE in the Persian Empire, called himself the Apostle of Light and taught that the material world imprisons divine light in darkness, with salvation lying in freeing that light.
--- fact 2
He drew on Zoroastrian, Christian, Buddhist and Gnostic ideas and presented himself as the final prophet after Jesus and the Buddha.
--- fact 3
The religion spread from Roman North Africa to Central Asia and China, helped by trade routes. A missionary reached the Tang court in 694, and freedom of worship followed in 732.
--- fact 4
In 296 the emperor Diocletian ordered Manichaean leaders burned with their scriptures, and Roman and Persian persecution largely ended the faith in the west by the sixth century.
--- fact 5
In China the anti-Buddhist persecution of 843 also seized Manichaean property and executed clergy, and the religion faded into Buddhism and Daoism by the 14th century.
--- context
Manichaeism arose in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], within the Sasanian Empire (not yet a vault note), drawing on ideas shared with [[culture/zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]] before spreading as far as the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]]'s China. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a meaning-engine assembled from older traditions crossed Eurasia in four centuries along trade routes, then was suppressed by Roman, Persian and Chinese states, each for its own stated reasons. It survived roughly a thousand years under official persecution nearly everywhere it took root. A specialist could argue that its decline in China reflects absorption into Buddhism and Daoism as much as suppression, which would weaken a reading of persecution as the sole cause of its extinction.
--- question 1
How much of Manichaeism's decline in China was suppression versus absorption into other traditions?
--- question 2
How consistent was Manichaean teaching across regions as different as Rome, Persia and China?

=== culture/marajoara-culture
title: Marajoara Culture
type: culture
date: c. 400 CE – 1300
themes: art, kinship, religion
src: 7b050568cac0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Marajoara culture flourished on Marajo Island at the mouth of the Amazon, known for large earthen mounds and richly decorated ceramics, with its ceramic tradition dated about 400 to 1300 CE.
--- fact 1
Marajo Island is roughly the size of Switzerland, and mound building there began around 300 CE.
--- fact 2
Mounds reach up to about 20 meters in height, and the largest multi-mound sites may have housed several thousand people.
--- fact 3
Ceramics include burial urns, figurines, and elaborately painted and incised vessels, suggesting specialized craft production.
--- fact 4

=== culture/minoans
title: Minoans
type: culture
date: c. 2000 – 1450 BCE
themes: trade, art
src: f7a15de42f72
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Minoans were a Bronze Age maritime civilization on the island of Crete, known for palaces such as Knossos and vivid frescoes. Their palatial culture flourished from about 2000 BCE until about 1450 BCE.
--- fact 1
They traded across the eastern Mediterranean, and their cultural influence reached Egypt, Cyprus, Canaan, and Anatolia.
--- fact 2
They used scripts, Linear A and Cretan hieroglyphs, that have not been deciphered. Linear B, adapted from Linear A, records Mycenaean Greek and was deciphered in 1952.
--- fact 3
The eruption of the Thera volcano damaged settlements in the Aegean. Radiocarbon dates put it around 1600 BCE, while links with Egyptian chronology suggest about 1500 BCE. The century-long gap is unresolved.
--- fact 4
Around 1450 BCE, Mycenaean Greeks became dominant on Crete and Linear B was used at Knossos. Scholars debate whether this was a conquest or a gradual absorption. Knossos remained a palace centre after the other Minoan palaces declined, until its own destruction some time later.
--- context
The Minoans are part of the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] trading world that collapsed in the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a culture known mainly through images of bull-leaping and sea life, since its writing cannot yet be read. What survives shapes how a culture is remembered.
--- question 1
Can Linear A be deciphered?
--- question 2
When exactly did Thera erupt, and how much did it contribute to Minoan decline compared with Mycenaean expansion?
