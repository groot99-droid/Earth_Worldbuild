=== species/anomalocaris
title: Anomalocaris
type: species
date: 515 – 505 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: cfd23b87fd34
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anomalocaris was a large swimming Cambrian animal with spiny grasping appendages, a round mouth ringed by plates, and large compound eyes. It lived about 515 to 505 million years ago, is known from Canada and Australia, and is a stem-group relative of arthropods.
--- fact 1
Anomalocaris is securely known from the Burgess Shale of British Columbia (about 505 million years old) and the Emu Bay Shale of South Australia (about 515). Chinese fossils once assigned to it have mostly been moved to other genera.
--- fact 2
Its appendages were described in 1892 as a crustacean tail, its mouth as a jellyfish, and its body as a sea cucumber. Harry Whittington and Derek Briggs showed in 1985 that they belonged to one animal.
--- fact 3
Complete Burgess specimens are about 20 to 25 cm long. Estimates of 35 to 50 cm include appendages and tail, and a claim of about 1 m rests on fragments.
--- fact 4
Compound eyes from the Emu Bay Shale, up to 3 cm long, preserve at least 16,000 lenses, comparable to the sharpest-sighted living arthropods.
--- fact 5
The mouth looks too weak to crush trilobite shells, and studies propose suction feeding or soft prey. Whether it was the top predator of its seas is debated.
--- context
Anomalocaris lived in the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]], a signature animal of the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], sharing seas with [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]], [[species/hallucigenia|Hallucigenia]], and [[species/pikaia|Pikaia]]. Its sites are the Burgess Shale and the Emu Bay Shale; fossils from the Chengjiang Fossil Site in China once assigned to it have since been moved to other genera. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Anomalocaris was first assembled from three fossils believed to be three animals, and that the apex predator of popular pictures depends on how its mouth is read. It is one of many body plans that arose and vanished in the Cambrian, when nothing marked in advance which would leave descendants. The reading would weaken if radiodonts were shown to be ancestral to a living animal group.
--- question 1
What did Anomalocaris eat?
--- question 2
How many valid species does the genus include?

=== species/barley
title: Barley
type: species
date: c. 9000 BCE
themes: trade, kinship
src: 24258996195d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Barley is a cereal grass domesticated in the Fertile Crescent from around 9000 BCE, one of the earliest crops farmed by humans and, in ancient Mesopotamia, a staple used as food, drink and even a standard of value.
--- fact 1
Barley was domesticated from wild barley in the Fertile Crescent, with early domesticated forms recorded in the Jarmo region of modern Iraq from about 9000 to 7000 BCE; grinding stones with starch traces from wild barley at Ohalo II, near the Sea of Galilee, show it was eaten well before domestication, by about 23,000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Domestication produced grain heads that hold together instead of shattering, making the plant far easier to harvest but dependent on deliberate replanting.
--- fact 3
Barley was likely among the first grains brewed into beer, one of the earliest alcoholic drinks made by Neolithic farmers.
--- fact 4
In Mesopotamia, barley served as a de facto currency, with the shekel functioning as a standard unit of its weight, and cuneiform records from around 2350 BCE, under the ruler Urukagina, describe monthly barley rations issued to adults.
--- fact 5
Barley spread from the Fertile Crescent across Eurasia, reaching eastern Finland by about 4200 BCE and the Indus civilization region between roughly 5,700 and 3,300 years ago; its use had spread throughout Eurasia by about 2000 BCE, and it was grown on the Korean peninsula from about 1500 to 850 BCE.
--- fact 6
Global production reached about 146 million tonnes in 2023, the fourth largest cereal harvest after maize, rice and wheat; about 70 percent goes to animal feed and about 30 percent to malting for beer and whisky.
--- context
Barley was domesticated alongside [[species/wheat|Wheat]] as one of the founding crops of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent, and it underwrote the economies of early Mesopotamian cities such as [[place/ur|Ur]] and, later, [[place/babylon|Babylon]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a grain used as both food and a unit of account collapses two functions that many later societies kept separate, food and money, and that Mesopotamian rationing records turn barley into a record of a kin-lattice's obligations, who was owed what and by whom. Brewing barley into beer is one of the oldest recorded transformations of a raw crop into a socially significant product, tying farming to gathering and celebration. The reading would weaken if barley's early monetary role proves to have been more local and limited than the shekel-based accounts suggest.
--- question 1
How standard was barley's role as a unit of value across different Mesopotamian cities and periods?
--- question 2
How soon after domestication did brewing become widespread, as opposed to barley being eaten mainly as porridge or bread?

=== species/cassava
title: Cassava
type: species
date: c. 4600 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: cbd686d32535
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cassava is a starchy root crop domesticated in west-central Brazil, likely within the last 10,000 years, that requires processing to remove natural toxins and that Portuguese and Spanish traders carried to Africa and Asia in the 16th century, where it became a staple for hundreds of millions of people.
--- fact 1
Cassava was domesticated from wild relatives centered in west-central Brazil; the date of domestication is imprecise, likely within the last 10,000 years, and cassava pollen appears in Gulf of Mexico lowland sediment by about 4600 BCE, with the oldest direct evidence of cultivation coming from the Maya site of Joya de Cerén in El Salvador, about 1,400 years old.
--- fact 2
Raw cassava contains cyanogenic compounds, linamarin and lotaustralin, that release toxic hydrogen cyanide; bitter varieties can carry up to 1,000 milligrams of cyanide per kilogram of fresh root, and communities developed soaking and fermentation methods, some reducing cyanide content by 85 to 99 percent, to make the crop safe to eat.
--- fact 3
Portuguese traders introduced cassava from Brazil to West Africa in the 16th century, and Portuguese and Spanish traders also carried it to their colonies in Goa, Malacca, eastern Indonesia, Timor and the Philippines; it reached East Africa by about 1850 through Arab and European traders.
--- fact 4
Cassava is now a primary food staple for an estimated 500 million to 800 million people, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, and it ranks as the tropics' third-largest carbohydrate source after rice and maize, valued for growing on poor soils with little rainfall.
--- fact 5
Global cassava production reached about 330 million tonnes in 2022, with Nigeria the largest producer and Thailand the largest exporter of cassava starch.
--- context
Cassava's domestication belongs to the wider [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Americas, and its 16th-century spread to Africa and Asia is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; in West and Central Africa it became a staple food during the era of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that cassava is unusual among staple crops in requiring a tool-lineage of processing, soaking or fermenting, before it can be safely eaten, so its adoption in Africa and Asia carried with it a body of practical knowledge as well as the plant itself, an example of an exchange-web moving techniques alongside seeds. Its tolerance of poor soil made it valuable precisely where colonial and later national agricultural systems demanded a reliable staple with little investment, which the Observer reads as an example of a crop's usefulness under stress shaping which regions came to depend on it. The reading would weaken if processing knowledge is shown to have traveled independently of the crop rather than together with it.
--- question 1
How did processing knowledge for detoxifying cassava spread alongside the crop itself in Africa and Asia?
--- question 2
How much does modern reliance on a narrow range of cassava varieties leave current staple supplies vulnerable to disease, as happened with the potato in 19th-century Europe?

=== species/coffee
title: Coffee
type: species
date: c. 1450
themes: trade, religion
src: 2dcb4441fccd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Coffee is a shrub whose beans, first documented as a drink among Sufi communities in Yemen by the late 15th century, spread through the Ottoman Empire and Europe and were then grown on slave and colonial plantations across the tropics.
--- fact 1
Coffee's popular origin story, in which a 9th-century Ethiopian goatherd named Kaldi discovers the plant's effects on his flock, does not appear in writing before 1671 and is likely a later legend; there is no confirmed evidence of coffee as a drink before the 15th century, when it was well established among Sufi communities in Yemen.
--- fact 2
The first known coffeehouse opened in Istanbul in 1555. A story that Pope Clement VIII blessed coffee as a Christian drink, usually dated 1600, is often credited with helping it spread in Catholic Europe, but it is not clear that the story is true; the first European coffeehouse outside Ottoman territory opened in Venice in 1645 by one account and 1647 by another.
--- fact 3
French naval officer Gabriel de Clieu brought coffee plants to Martinique in the 1720s, and cultivation began in the French colony of Saint-Domingue (Haiti) in 1734; by 1788 Saint-Domingue, worked by enslaved laborers, supplied roughly half the world's coffee, and the harsh conditions on its plantations were a contributing factor in the Haitian Revolution.
--- fact 4
Coffee reached Brazil in 1727, but large-scale cultivation there did not take off until after independence in 1822.
--- fact 5
Coffee cultivation across its colonial-era expansion generally displaced and exploited Indigenous and enslaved populations, a pattern recorded here as sustained harm rather than as an incidental cost of production.
--- fact 6
Global coffee production reached about 11 million tonnes in 2023, with Brazil producing about 31 percent and Vietnam the second-largest share.
--- context
Coffee's early spread from Yemen through the Ottoman Empire followed routes tied to the wider [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]], and its colonial-era plantation economy in the Caribbean is linked to the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and helped provoke the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]; its global spread after the 16th century belongs to the broader [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] of plants and practices between continents. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that coffee began its documented history bound up with a meaning-engine, used by Sufi communities to stay alert for nighttime devotions, before becoming a secular drink of Ottoman and European coffeehouses that themselves became new sites of conversation and commerce, a shift in the substance's story-glue from devotion to sociability. Its later spread onto slave-worked plantations shows the same plant enabling both a set of religious practices and one of the most extractive labor systems in early modern history, and the Observer treats the suffering on those plantations as inseparable from the substance's global rise. The reading would weaken if the popular Kaldi legend proves to rest on an older oral tradition rather than being a 17th-century invention.
--- question 1
How much of coffee's early diffusion outside Yemen followed Sufi religious networks specifically, as opposed to general trade contacts?
--- question 2
How did the labor conditions on 18th-century coffee plantations compare with those on sugar plantations in the same colonies?

=== species/goat
title: Goat
type: species
date: c. 8000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: cdde9ad070e0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic goat was bred from the wild bezoar ibex in southwestern Asia around 10,000 years ago, one of the first animals kept by farming communities, and it remains valued today for milk, meat and fiber, especially on marginal land unsuited to other livestock.
--- fact 1
Domestic goats descend from the bezoar ibex, a wild goat still found in the Zagros Mountains of Iran and formerly widespread in Anatolia, and domestication is dated to about 10,000 years ago in Iran.
--- fact 2
The earliest clear archaeological evidence for goat domestication comes from Ganj Dareh in Iran, about 10,000 years ago, and goats from further early sites at Jericho, Choga Mami, Djeitun and Çayönü date to between about 8,000 and 9,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Neolithic herders used goats for milk and meat and also made use of their dung as fuel and their bones, hair and sinew for tools, building materials and clothing.
--- fact 4
Goats remain economically important today for dairy, providing about 2 percent of the world's total annual milk supply, and for fiber such as cashmere and mohair, valued in part for their hardiness on land too marginal for cattle or sheep.
--- fact 5
More than 1.1 billion goats were living worldwide as of 2022, of which about 150 million were in India, the largest single-country population; India was also the largest goat-milk producer that year, followed by Bangladesh and South Sudan.
--- context
Goats were among the earliest animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]], a pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent alongside [[species/sheep|Sheep]], domesticated in a similar place and period and often herded together. Goats sit in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: that the goat's continuing importance on land too poor for cattle or sheep suggests its domestication gave early farming communities a way to draw food and materials from marginal ground that would otherwise have supported no livestock at all, extending the reach of the Settling into harsher terrain. That goats and sheep were domesticated in the same general region and period, and are still often herded together, points to an early kin-lattice of pastoral practice that treated the two species as complementary rather than as competitors for the same land. The reading would weaken if goat and sheep domestication are shown to have occurred at meaningfully different times or in different communities rather than in close succession.
--- question 1
How closely linked were the domestication of goats and sheep, and did one community likely tame both, or did the practices spread separately?
--- question 2
How has goat herding on marginal land changed as climate change alters which regions count as marginal?

=== species/rice
title: Rice
type: species
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 39d3186f9de6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Rice is a cereal grass domesticated from a wild ancestor in the Yangtze valley of China from about 9,000 years ago, and it is now the staple food of more than half the world's population.
--- fact 1
Rice was domesticated from wild _Oryza rufipogon_ by Neolithic communities in the Upper and Lower Yangtze valley, in what is now China, about 9,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
Genetic evidence for the gene that stops rice grains from shattering is identical in the two main cultivated groups, japonica and indica, which points to a single original domestication event rather than two separate ones; indica rice is thought to have arisen later, when japonica reached India about 4,500 years ago and hybridized with a local wild or semi-wild rice.
--- fact 3
Rice farming reached Korea and Japan between about 5,500 and 3,200 years ago, and island Southeast Asia and Taiwan between about 5,500 and 4,000 years ago, carried in part by Austronesian-speaking migrations, and mainland Southeast Asia by about 5,000 years ago.
--- fact 4
Rice is the staple food of more than half the world's population, and China and India together consumed several hundred million tonnes of it in the early 2020s.
--- fact 5
The Green Revolution rice variety IR8 was bred in 1966 at the International Rice Research Institute by crossing an Indonesian and a Chinese variety, producing a short, strong-stemmed plant that resisted falling over under heavy fertilizer use.
--- fact 6
Global rice production reached about 800 million tonnes in 2023, with about 90 percent grown in Asia, and less than 8 percent of the crop traded internationally.
--- context
Rice's domestication is described in more detail under [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]] and belongs to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] alongside cereals domesticated elsewhere; its 20th-century breeding is part of the [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]]. Rice farming shaped the population and economy of the Yangtze valley and other East Asian river valleys; Neolithic farming in the [[place/yellow-river|Yellow River]] region rested mainly on millet, with rice grown there to a lesser extent. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that rice farming, especially the flooded-field methods used across much of Asia, ties a community to sustained cooperative labor: building and maintaining paddies, canals and terraces is a task larger than any one household, which the Observer reads as a kin-lattice pressure toward dense, cooperative settlement. That most of the world's rice is grown and eaten within Asia, with little traded across borders, shows a staple that remains tied to the regions that farm it rather than becoming a fully global exchange-good in the way wheat has. The reading would weaken if paddy-field cooperation proves to have mattered less to social organization than dry-field cereal farming elsewhere.
--- question 1
Was there truly a single domestication event for rice, or did separate lineages contribute more than current genetic evidence suggests?
--- question 2
How did paddy irrigation systems shape the social organization of the communities that built and maintained them?

=== species/sheep
title: Sheep
type: species
date: c. 9000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 93eef9de62c2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic sheep was bred from the wild mouflon in Mesopotamia between about 11,000 and 9000 BCE, first kept for meat, milk and skins, and only later selectively bred for wool, and it now numbers well over a billion animals worldwide.
--- fact 1
Sheep were domesticated from the mouflon, a wild sheep of southwestern Asia, in an estimated window between 11,000 and 9000 BCE in Mesopotamia, and the practice had spread to Mehrgarh in the Indus Valley by about 7000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Early domestic sheep were kept mainly for meat, milk and skins rather than fleece; archaeological evidence, including statuary from Iran, suggests deliberate selection for woolly sheep began around 6000 BCE, several thousand years after initial domestication, a shift sometimes called the secondary products revolution.
--- fact 3
The earliest known woven wool garments date to around 4000 BCE, and Neolithic farmers in what is now France, among the Castelnovien people, were keeping domestic sheep by about 6000 BCE.
--- fact 4
Sheep are raised in large numbers today across Australia, New Zealand, the southern regions of South America, South Africa and the British Isles, and China and India also hold very large flocks by absolute count; the world's domestic sheep population was estimated at around 1.2 billion in 2019.
--- context
Sheep were among the earliest animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] and, alongside crops such as wheat and barley, formed one pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent; they were commonly herded alongside the [[species/goat|Goat]], domesticated in a similar place and period. Sheep sit in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: that the gap of several thousand years between sheep domestication and deliberate wool breeding suggests early herders did not domesticate the animal with fleece production already in mind, but discovered and then bred for a secondary use only once the animal was already established in their kin-lattice, a pattern the Observer reads as evidence that a domesticated species' eventual economic role can differ substantially from the reason it was first tamed. The animal's spread across so much of the pastoral world since then shows a tool-lineage of herding techniques traveling with the species itself. The reading would weaken if evidence of early wool use is found that predates the currently accepted timeline for woolly-sheep breeding.
--- question 1
What use did early herders make of sheep in the several thousand years before wool breeding began, beyond meat, milk and skins?
--- question 2
How much did sheep domestication in Mesopotamia influence, or draw on, the roughly contemporary domestication of goats in the same region?

=== species/soybean
title: Soybean
type: species
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 1dc1e287ecdf
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The soybean is a legume domesticated in the region between the Yellow and Huai rivers in China between roughly 9,000 and 6,000 years ago, long central to East Asian diets through foods such as tofu and soy sauce, and now grown worldwide chiefly as animal feed and oil.
--- fact 1
The soybean was domesticated in north-central China, between the Yellow River and the Huai River, with earliest evidence from the site of Jiahu in Henan province dated to between about 9,000 and 7,800 years ago.
--- fact 2
Chinese tradition credits the legendary Emperor Shennong, dated in myth to 2853 BCE, with naming the soybean one of five sacred grains alongside rice, wheat, barley and millet.
--- fact 3
Fermented soy foods such as soy sauce, fermented bean paste, natto and tempeh, along with tofu made from soy milk, developed over many centuries in East Asia, except tempeh, which comes from Java in Southeast Asia; the earliest Japanese textual reference to soybeans appears in the Kojiki, compiled in 712 CE.
--- fact 4
The soybean was introduced to the Americas and Europe in the 18th century, with the first recorded North American cultivation in 1765 on Skidaway Island, Georgia, and the first French cultivation in 1779.
--- fact 5
Genetically modified, herbicide-tolerant soybean varieties were introduced commercially in the United States in 1996 and made up about 17 percent of United States soybean acreage by 1997, rising to about 93 percent by 2010.
--- fact 6
In 2019 Brazil, the United States and Argentina were the largest producers, together accounting for roughly four-fifths of world output, and about 80 percent of the global soybean crop is used to feed livestock.
--- context
The soybean's domestication is one of East Asia's founding contributions to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], developed alongside rice and other regional crops; its adoption of genetically modified varieties from the 1990s parallels the yield-focused breeding of the 20th-century [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]] in other crops. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the soybean has passed through two very different uses over its long relationship with humans: for millennia mainly a food transformed by fermentation and pressing into tofu, sauce and other dishes across East Asia, and in the last century mainly an input, grown at enormous scale chiefly to feed livestock and produce oil, in a system reliant on genetically modified varieties barely three decades old. This shift shows how a crop's tool-lineage of use can change faster than the plant itself. The reading would weaken if the rapid adoption of genetically modified soy proves to have depended more on regulatory approval sequencing than on farmer preference.
--- question 1
How much of the soybean's shift toward livestock feed and oil, rather than direct human food, reflects changing global diets versus agricultural economics?
--- question 2
How did the introduction of genetically modified soybeans affect farming communities differently across producing countries?

=== species/sugarcane
title: Sugarcane
type: species
date: c. 4000 BCE
themes: trade, war
src: c1abdb3b19d6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea and nearby parts of Southeast Asia around 6,000 years ago, spread through Asia and the Mediterranean world over the following millennia, and after 1492 became the crop most closely tied to slave labor in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Sugarcane was domesticated from the wild grass Saccharum robustum in New Guinea around 6,000 years ago; a related domestication of Saccharum sinense by Austronesian peoples in southern China and Taiwan occurred by at least 5,500 years ago.
--- fact 2
Austronesian traders carried sugarcane to China and India by about 3,000 years ago, and Persian and Greek writers encountered it in the 6th to 4th centuries BCE; by the 8th century CE sugar was traded into the Mediterranean world as an expensive luxury spice from India.
--- fact 3
Christopher Columbus brought sugarcane to Hispaniola on his second voyage, and the first sugar harvest in the Americas took place there in 1501; Portuguese colonizers introduced the crop to Brazil, where contemporary observers counted about 60 sugar mills in 1570, 115 in 1583 and 192 in 1612.
--- fact 4
The Atlantic sugar trade formed one side of a triangular trade linking European manufactured goods, enslaved Africans, and New World sugar and other raw materials, and sugar plantations across the Caribbean and Brazil were worked overwhelmingly by enslaved labor; in Louisiana, enslaved workers helped make the state responsible for nearly a quarter of the world's exportable sugar by 1853.
--- fact 5
Global sugarcane production reached about 1.92 billion tonnes in 2022, with Brazil producing about 38 percent and India about 23 percent.
--- context
Sugarcane's post-1492 spread across the Atlantic world is described in more detail under the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and is inseparable from the history of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]; its introduction to Hispaniola is one of the earliest transfers of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that sugarcane's long, slow spread across Asia over thousands of years contrasts sharply with the speed and scale of its expansion in the Americas after 1492, where within a few decades an exchange-web moved the plant, an enslaved workforce and a market for the finished product across an ocean at once, a compression the Observer treats as evidence of how quickly extractive systems can scale once the pieces are in place. The suffering of enslaved workers on sugar plantations is recorded here as central to the crop's modern history, not incidental to it. The reading would weaken if the Louisiana sugar economy's growth is shown to have depended as much on land and capital as on enslaved labor specifically.
--- question 1
How much of sugar's price as a "luxury spice" in medieval Europe reflected genuine scarcity versus deliberately controlled supply along the trade route from India?
--- question 2
How did the scale of the Atlantic sugar plantation system compare with earlier sugar production in the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean worlds?

=== species/tobacco
title: Tobacco
type: species
date: c. 1400 BCE
themes: trade, religion
src: 77ee5648b368
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Tobacco is a plant native to the Americas, cultivated there since at least the 2nd millennium BCE and used ceremonially by many Indigenous peoples, that Spanish colonizers carried to Europe in the 16th century and that became a cash crop underwriting Virginia's colonial economy.
--- fact 1
Cultivation sites in Mexico show tobacco use dating back to between about 1400 and 1000 BCE, and it was used across the Americas both socially and ceremonially, including to seal peace treaties and trade agreements; in many Indigenous traditions tobacco is regarded as a gift used to carry prayers, and it remains part of ceremonial practice today.
--- fact 2
An often-repeated account says that Francisco Hernández de Toledo brought tobacco seeds to Spain in 1559 on the order of King Philip II, but it does not fit his career: he became the king's physician in 1567, and his expedition to New Spain ran from 1570 to 1577. Jean Nicot, the French ambassador in Lisbon, sent tobacco to Paris in 1559, an early step in the plant's introduction to the Old World, and it spread rapidly across Europe, Africa and Asia in the following decades.
--- fact 3
Tobacco became central to the economy of the English colony of Jamestown, used at times as a form of currency and exported as a cash crop that is often credited with saving the struggling colony; its cultivation across the Caribbean and the American South became deeply tied to the history of enslaved labor.
--- fact 4
The World Health Organization has identified tobacco use as the world's single greatest preventable cause of death.
--- fact 5
China is the largest producer of tobacco today, growing about 36 percent of the world total in 2020, followed by India and Brazil.
--- context
Tobacco's cultivation underwrote the early economy of [[place/jamestown|Jamestown]] and, more broadly, its spread to the Old World is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; the labor that grew it across the Americas was increasingly supplied through the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that tobacco carried two very different meanings across the ocean it crossed: a substance woven into ceremony and treaty-making among many Indigenous peoples of the Americas, and a commercial export crop in the colonies that displaced those same peoples, grown increasingly by enslaved labor for a European market that treated it mainly as a source of profit and pleasure. That a plant used to seal peace agreements became bound up with a system built on the ownership of human beings is, in the Observer's reading, a stark case of a substance's meaning-engine changing entirely as it moved between very different societies. The reading would weaken if ceremonial tobacco use is shown to have been far less widespread among Indigenous peoples than the general accounts suggest.
--- question 1
How did the meaning and use of tobacco change among Indigenous communities themselves as European demand for the crop grew?
--- question 2
How much of Virginia's economic survival is fairly credited to tobacco alone, as opposed to other factors in the colony's early development?

=== place/mecca
title: Mecca
type: place
date: c. 500 CE
themes: religion, trade
src: f3aa9f7a9b47
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mecca is a city in the Hejaz region of western Saudi Arabia, about 70 kilometers inland from the Red Sea, the birthplace of Muhammad and the holiest city of Islam, whose sanctuary around the Kaaba is the destination of the annual Hajj pilgrimage.
--- fact 1
The date of Mecca's first settlement is unknown, and the identification of Mecca with the place Macoraba in Ptolemy's 2nd-century geography is disputed. Muslim sources written after Muhammad's time describe a sanctuary town governed by the Quraysh in the century before Islam.
--- fact 2
Islamic tradition places Muhammad's birth in Mecca about 570 CE, his move to Medina in 622, and Mecca's passing under Muslim control in 630 with little fighting.
--- fact 3
The Hajj is one of the Five Pillars of Islam, required once in a lifetime of Muslims able to make the journey; Saudi authorities have reported annual attendance in the low millions in recent decades.
--- fact 4
Historians disagree about Mecca's importance in long-distance trade before Islam; some describe it as a regional sanctuary and market town rather than a hub of the incense and spice trades.
--- fact 5
Mecca was ruled locally by the Sharifs under successive overlords, including the Ottomans from 1517, until the Saudi conquest of 1924 to 1925. Non-Muslims are barred from entering the city.
--- fact 6
In the crush at Mina, near Mecca, during the 2015 Hajj, the Saudi authorities reported 769 deaths, while counts compiled from other governments' figures exceeded 2,000.
--- context
Mecca is the central place of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], the city Muhammad left in the Hijra to [[place/medina|Medina]], and a stop in the peninsula's wider [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]] setting. It came under the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]] and later the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]], and pilgrims came from far away, including the ruler of the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]], Mansa Musa, who made the Hajj in 1324. Mecca sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Mecca is a place where a meaning-engine draws millions of strangers to one point each year, creating a recurring exchange-web of people, goods and news that reached from West Africa to Southeast Asia. Pilgrims wear plain white garments (for men, two unstitched cloths), which reduces visible differences of rank during the rites. Pilgrims themselves describe the journey as an act of obedience and devotion, and this reading of its social effects is meant to sit beside that account, not replace it. Crowd disasters such as Mina in 2015 are a recurring cost of gathering at this scale and are recorded here as suffering. The reading would weaken if pilgrim networks turn out to have carried little trade or news compared with their devotional purpose.
--- question 1
What does archaeology show about pre-Islamic Mecca, given how little excavation has been done in the sacred precinct?
--- question 2
How large was Mecca's role in long-distance trade before Islam?

=== place/nan-madol
title: Nan Madol
type: place
date: c. 900 CE – 1628
themes: kinship, technology
src: 3838d424d21b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nan Madol is a ruined city of artificial stone islets built in a lagoon off Pohnpei, in the Federated States of Micronesia, the ceremonial and political center of the Saudeleur dynasty from about the 12th century until around 1628.
--- fact 1
Nan Madol consists of about 92 artificial islets built from stacked basalt columns and coral fill, linked by tidal canals, over a core area of about 1.5 by 0.5 kilometers.
--- fact 2
Human activity at the site dates from as early as the 1st or 2nd century CE, islet construction began in the 8th or 9th century, and the megalithic stonework that gives the site its present form was built from about 1180 to 1200.
--- fact 3
Tradition holds that the Saudeleur dynasty ruled Pohnpei from Nan Madol, uniting an estimated population of about 25,000 on the island, though the number of people living at Nan Madol itself was probably under 1,000.
--- fact 4
Around 1628 a warrior named Isokelekel is said in tradition to have invaded and ended Saudeleur rule; some scholars also point to population decline as a contributing cause of the site's abandonment.
--- fact 5
UNESCO inscribed Nan Madol as a World Heritage Site in 2016.
--- context
Nan Madol's builders were Pohnpeians, speakers of a Micronesian language, and part of the wider settlement of the Pacific carried by the [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]]; Pohnpei's first settlers probably came from the [[culture/lapita-culture|Lapita Culture]] of the southeast Solomon Islands or Vanuatu, farther south and east. They were not Polynesians: the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] is a separate movement within the same expansion. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Nan Madol turned a lagoon into a claim-line: a ruling lineage built its seat on stone islets separated from the rest of the island by water, a status-signal that set the center of power physically apart from those it ruled. The tradition of Isokelekel's conquest, like many founding and ending stories, is ancestor-weight carried forward by the community rather than a document, and the Observer treats it as a claim to be tested rather than a settled fact. The reading would weaken if archaeology shows the site's decline came mainly from environmental or demographic causes rather than conquest.
--- question 1
How much of the Isokelekel tradition reflects an actual conquest, and how much is later legend explaining Saudeleur rule's end?
--- question 2
How was the labor to move and stack the basalt columns organized, given Pohnpei's small population?
