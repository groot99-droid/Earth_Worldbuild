=== event/devonian-forests-and-the-rise-of-trees
title: Devonian Forests and the Rise of Trees
type: event
date: 385 – 359 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 3474fe159fa6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Devonian forests were the first forests on Earth, appearing about 385 million years ago and spreading through the rest of the period. Trees evolved independently in several plant lineages, and their roots and litter changed soils, rivers and the carbon cycle.
--- fact 1
The Gilboa fossil forest in New York, about 385 million years old, holds some of the oldest known fossil trees, including cladoxylopsids with hollow trunks.
--- fact 2
Archaeopteris, a tree with woody trunks and fern-like foliage that grew more than ten metres tall, formed forests across several continents in the Late Devonian.
--- fact 3
Trees arose independently in several plant lineages during the Devonian and Carboniferous, including club-mosses, relatives of horsetails and ancestors of seed plants.
--- fact 4
Deep roots speeded the chemical weathering of rock and built soils, and atmospheric carbon dioxide fell over the Devonian, which may have contributed to later cooling.
--- fact 5
Some researchers link the spread of forests to nutrient runoff and ocean oxygen loss at the time of the [[event/late-devonian-extinction|Late Devonian Extinction]]; the link is debated.
--- context
The Devonian forests are a central part of the [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]] and lead into the [[event/carboniferous-coal-forests|Carboniferous Coal Forests]]. They belong to the [[era/devonian-period|Devonian Period]] and the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the tree form was reached by unrelated plant groups, which suggests that some solutions are strongly favoured by the problem, here competition for light, and are not tied to one lineage. The reading would weaken if the different tree forms proved to share a single evolutionary origin.
--- question 1
How large was the effect of forests on Devonian carbon dioxide and climate?
--- question 2
How much did forests contribute to the Late Devonian extinction?

=== event/digital-revolution

type: event
date: c. 1969
themes: technology, language, trade

mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Digital Revolution is the spread of digital computing and networks from the mid-20th century on, including the internet (from 1969) and the World Wide Web (from 1989 to 1991).
--- fact 1
The first message on ARPANET, a precursor of the internet, was sent in 1969.
--- fact 2
Tim Berners-Lee proposed the World Wide Web in 1989 and it became public in 1991.
--- fact 3
Smartphones, from about 2007, put networked computers in billions of pockets.
--- fact 4
More than five billion people now use the internet.
--- context
See [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]] and [[person/alan-turing|Alan Turing]]. It is the defining process of the [[era/information-age|Information Age]].
--- observer
The Observer regards this as memory-outsourcing at scale: nearly every human utterance, transaction, and image can now be stored and searched. A record of the species is being written faster than any observer could read.

=== event/divergence-of-the-hominin-lineage
title: Divergence of the Hominin Lineage
type: event
date: c. 7 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: f023e1f00765
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The hominin lineage, the line leading to humans, split from the line leading to chimpanzees and bonobos between roughly 6 and 8 million years ago in Africa.
--- fact 1
Genetic estimates of the split depend on assumed mutation rates and generation times: one 2006 study placed it no earlier than about 6.3 million years ago, while studies using longer generation times imply earlier dates.
--- fact 2
Early candidate hominins include [[species/sahelanthropus-tchadensis|Sahelanthropus tchadensis]] (about 7 million years ago).
--- fact 3
Upright walking (bipedalism) is a key early trait; see [[species/australopithecus-afarensis|Australopithecus afarensis]].
--- fact 4
A 2006 genetic study proposed that the split was gradual, with interbreeding between the lineages for a time, though this model is disputed.
--- context
The divergence occurs during the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and sets the biological stage for [[event/origin-of-homo-sapiens|Origin of Homo sapiens]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' closest relatives are two other apes. The line between 'them' and 'us' is thinner than most human stories suggest.
--- question 1
Was Sahelanthropus bipedal, and was it a hominin?

=== event/domestication-of-maize
title: Domestication of Maize
type: event
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: technology, earth-systems, trade
src: a9f2274ae8e4
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Maize (corn) was domesticated from a wild grass called teosinte in southwestern Mexico about 9,000 years ago, and became a staple crop across the Americas.
--- fact 1
Genetic studies trace maize to the wild teosinte Zea mays subsp. parviglumis.
--- fact 2
Five major morphological differences separate maize from teosinte, and regions throughout the genome contributed to the change. Early maize had tiny cobs; selection over thousands of years produced large ears.
--- fact 3
The Maya and Aztecs treated maize as sacred; cooking it with lime (nixtamalization) makes its nutrients available.
--- fact 4
Maize is now among the world's most-produced crops by volume.
--- context
Maize was the basis of [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]]n states such as the [[culture/maya|Maya]] and [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]]. It reached the rest of the world through the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
--- observer
The Observer treats this as a second, independent run of the Settling. A grass with a few seeds was reshaped by human selection into a crop that could support cities.
--- question 1
How fast did the crop spread north and south?

=== event/domestication-of-rice
title: Domestication of Rice
type: event
date: c. 7000 BCE
themes: technology, kinship
src: 69552b242299
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rice (Oryza sativa) was domesticated from wild rice in the Yangtze River region of China beginning around 9,000 to 7,000 years ago, over a long process rather than a single event.
--- fact 1
Wild ancestor populations of rice are usually identified as Oryza rufipogon.
--- fact 2
Early evidence for rice use and cultivation comes from Yangtze valley sites such as Shangshan, where rice phytoliths date to about 9,000 years ago, and later Kuahuqiao and Hemudu.
--- fact 3
Domestication traits such as non-shattering seed heads appeared gradually over thousands of years.
--- fact 4
The japonica subspecies is generally traced to the Yangtze region; the indica subspecies is thought to have arisen through later crossing with other rice populations, an origin that is debated.
--- fact 5
Rice is a staple for more than half of the world's population.
--- context
Rice was one of the great founder crops of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], alongside wheat and barley in Southwest Asia and maize in [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]]. Rice paddies became central to East Asian societies. Domestication of Rice sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that farming was invented independently on several continents with different plants, yet each time produced denser settlement and stored surplus. The similarity of consequences across different crops is what makes the pattern hard to dismiss as accident.
--- question 1
How many separate domestication events produced modern rice varieties?

=== event/domestication-of-the-dog
title: Domestication of the Dog
type: event
date: c. 40 – 14.2 ka
themes: kinship, technology
src: 97358289f76f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Dogs descend from a now-extinct population of Pleistocene wolves, but when and where domestication happened is one of the most disputed dates in the whole domestication record. Genetic studies place the split from the nearest known wolf relatives at roughly 40,000 to 20,000 years ago; the oldest widely accepted dog remains, from Bonn-Oberkassel in Germany, are about 14,200 years old.
--- fact 1
A 2020 study of 27 ancient dog genomes found all dogs share ancestry distinct from any wolf population sampled today, descending from a single extinct Pleistocene lineage.
--- fact 2
The same and related studies place the genetic divergence at roughly 40,000 to 27,000 years ago, with some estimates extending to about 20,000; no tighter date is agreed.
--- fact 3
Whether domestication happened once or twice, in separate Eurasian wolf populations that later merged, is argued both ways; a large 2020 study favors a single origin, but other ancient-wolf-genome work has reopened the dual-origin question.
--- fact 4
The Bonn-Oberkassel dog, buried with two humans about 14,200 years ago, is one of the first remains universally agreed to be a dog rather than a wolf.
--- fact 5
Older candidate "Paleolithic dogs" from Goyet Cave, Belgium, and Předmostí, Czech Republic, roughly 30,000 years or more, remain disputed: their skulls do not clearly exclude wolves, and one Předmostí specimen is genetically closer to wolves.
--- fact 6
Evidence points to an origin somewhere in Eurasia, often argued to be Siberia, though the region is unsettled.
--- context
Dog domestication falls within the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]], likely spanning the [[era/upper-paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]], and is tied to [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] hunter-gatherer groups. It is the earliest known domestication of any animal, well before plants and livestock. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' oldest tool-lineage partnership was with another animal, formed while both still hunted large Ice Age prey, long before writing or farming. The wide, unsettled range of dates, and the difficulty of telling an early dog from a wolf by bone shape alone, means the Observer treats any specific date here as provisional. The reading would weaken if an uncontested dog skeleton were found well outside the 40,000-to-14,000-year range.
--- question 1
Did domestication happen once or more than once?
--- question 2
Which disputed "Paleolithic dog" specimens are true dogs rather than wolves?

--- summary
The Dutch East India Company (VOC) was a chartered trading company founded in 1602 with a monopoly on Dutch trade in Asia, among the first companies to issue tradable shares.

The Dutch States General chartered it in 1602.

The company built on the sea route opened by the [[event/portuguese-sea-route-to-india|Portuguese Sea Route to India]] and competed in the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]]. Dutch East India Company sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a joint-stock company could raise fleets, fight wars, and rule territory, while being owned by strangers who held paper shares. It is a story-glue entity with more sovereignty than most kings.

=== event/earliest-continental-crust
title: Earliest Continental Crust
type: event
date: 4.4 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: 59b5945ccf72
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The earliest continental crust is the first granite-like crust, lighter than the ocean floor, that could stand above sea level. It may have formed before 4.0 billion years ago, but almost none of it survives as rock, so its extent and timing are uncertain.
--- fact 1
Some researchers read the chemistry of Jack Hills zircons, up to about 4.4 billion years old, as showing granite-like magmas, a sign of evolved crust; others dispute that reading. See [[place/jack-hills-zircons|Jack Hills Zircons]].
--- fact 2
The Acasta Gneiss in northwestern Canada, about 4.0 billion years old, is the oldest known intact rock body. Some studies date rocks of the Nuvvuagittuq belt in Quebec to as much as about 4.3 billion years, but that age is disputed.
--- fact 3
Continental crust is less dense than oceanic crust and is made by partial melting of older rocks. Because it is buoyant it survives recycling far better than the ocean floor.
--- fact 4
How fast the continents grew is debated: some models place most of the crust in existence by about 3 billion years ago, and others favour slower growth.
--- context
The earliest continental crust belongs to the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] and continues into the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]], when the first stable continental cores formed. It is tied to the [[event/formation-of-the-oceans|Formation of the Oceans]] and to the question of the [[event/onset-of-plate-tectonics|Onset of Plate Tectonics]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the earliest crust is known almost entirely from grains that survived billions of years of recycling. The first half billion years of the record is thin by nature, and the absence of rock is not evidence of the absence of crust. The reading would weaken if new discoveries of ancient rock gave a very different picture.
--- question 1
How much continental crust existed in the Hadean?
--- question 2
Are the oldest claimed rock ages, such as those from Nuvvuagittuq, reliable?

=== event/early-hominin-dispersals-from-africa
title: Early Hominin Dispersals from Africa
type: event
date: 2.1 Ma – 500 ka
themes: earth-systems, technology
src: 90d7376a2d67
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hominins first left Africa between about 2.1 and 1.8 million years ago and spread across Eurasia in several dispersals over the next million years. The oldest hominin fossils outside Africa, at Dmanisi in Georgia, are about 1.85 to 1.78 million years old, and stone tools from the Loess Plateau of China are about 2.1 million years old.
--- fact 1
Dmanisi has yielded five well-preserved skulls and many body bones, dated to about 1.85 to 1.77 million years ago, with simple Oldowan-style stone tools.
--- fact 2
The skulls have small brains, about 546 to 775 cubic centimeters. A 2013 study argued that their variation fits one population, which doubts separate early Homo species.
--- fact 3
Stone tools at Shangchen on the Chinese Loess Plateau date from about 2.1 to 1.3 million years ago. No hominin fossils were found there, so the toolmakers are unidentified.
--- fact 4
Homo erectus was on Java by about 1.5 to 1.3 million years ago, and hominins were in Spain by about 1.4 to 1.2 million years ago and in England about 0.9 million years ago.
--- fact 5
Whether the first dispersers were Homo erectus is debated; small brains and simple tools at Dmanisi suggest they left before that form was typical.
--- context
The early dispersals follow the [[event/emergence-of-the-genus-homo|Emergence of the Genus Homo]] and the [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]] and belong to the [[era/lower-paleolithic|Lower Paleolithic]]. They center on [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]]. [[species/homo-floresiensis|Homo floresiensis]] on Flores may descend from an early dispersal; its ancestry is debated. Later dispersals are separate; see [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the first hominins outside Africa had small brains and simple stone tools, which suggests that leaving Africa did not require the large brain of later humans. The Observer reads dispersal as repeated range expansion into new habitats, of which the species' later spread is one more instance. The reading would weaken if the Dmanisi hominins proved an isolated dead end.
--- question 1
Who made the 2.1-million-year-old stone tools at Shangchen?
--- question 2
Did the first hominins to leave Africa belong to Homo erectus or to an earlier form?

Symbolic behavior, including engraved ochre, beads, and cave paintings, appears in the archaeological record from about 100,000 years ago onward.

=== event/emergence-of-the-genus-homo
title: Emergence of the Genus Homo
type: event
date: 2.8 – 1.8 Ma
themes: earth-systems, technology
src: 274629825d1e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The genus Homo emerged in East Africa between about 2.8 and 1.8 million years ago from an Australopithecus-like ancestor. The oldest fossil assigned to Homo is a partial jaw from Ledi-Geraru, Ethiopia, about 2.8 million years old, but fossils from this interval are sparse and disputed.
--- fact 1
The Ledi-Geraru jaw, LD 350-1, is dated to about 2.80 to 2.75 million years ago and was assigned to Homo in 2015. Some later analyses stress its resemblance to Australopithecus afarensis.
--- fact 2
A maxilla from Hadar, Ethiopia, about 2.3 million years old, and fossils attributed to Homo habilis and Homo rudolfensis from Olduvai Gorge and Lake Turkana, roughly 2.4 to 1.4 million years old, are assigned to Homo by many researchers. Some place the latter two outside the genus.
--- fact 3
Homo erectus appears about 1.9 to 1.8 million years ago with a larger body and brain; see [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]].
--- fact 4
Oldowan tools appear about 2.6 million years ago, and older tools from Lomekwi, Kenya, are about 3.3 million years old. Australopithecus and early Homo overlapped, so the earliest toolmaker is uncertain.
--- fact 5
East African climate grew drier and more variable across this interval; a link to the origin of Homo is debated.
--- context
Homo emerges after [[species/australopithecus-afarensis|Australopithecus afarensis]] and the [[event/divergence-of-the-hominin-lineage|Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]], during the [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]]. It runs through the early [[era/lower-paleolithic|Lower Paleolithic]] with the [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]] and [[species/homo-habilis|Homo habilis]], and leads to the [[event/early-hominin-dispersals-from-africa|Early Hominin Dispersals from Africa]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' own genus has no clean starting line: a jaw at 2.8 million years, scattered skulls near 2 million, and a well-defined body plan only by 1.9 million. The Observer reads this as slow, patchy change in a lineage already walking upright, with no single moment to call a beginning. The reading would weaken if a well-dated skeleton showed a rapid change.
--- question 1
Is the Ledi-Geraru jaw an early Homo or a late Australopithecus?
--- question 2
Which hominin made the first stone tools?
--- question 3
Which fossils belong in the genus Homo?

=== event/eocene-oligocene-transition
title: Eocene-Oligocene Transition
type: event
date: 34 – 33.5 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: d57280a74a48
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Eocene-Oligocene transition was a sharp global cooling about 34 million years ago, during which permanent ice sheets grew on Antarctica. It marks the shift from a warm greenhouse world toward the colder world of the later Cenozoic.
--- fact 1
The boundary between the Eocene and the Oligocene is dated to about 33.9 million years ago. Ocean records show a stepwise rise in oxygen isotopes, read as the combined effect of cooling and the growth of ice.
--- fact 2
The Antarctic ice sheet grew to a size close to that of today within a few hundred thousand years, and sea level fell by tens of metres.
--- fact 3
Falling atmospheric carbon dioxide, crossing a threshold, is a leading explanation. The opening of ocean gateways around Antarctica, including the Drake Passage and the Tasmanian Gateway, allowed a circumpolar current that isolated the continent; the timing of the openings, between about 50 and 30 million years ago, is debated.
--- fact 4
In Europe the change coincides with the Grande Coupure, a turnover in which Asian mammals largely replaced the older European fauna. Marine plankton also suffered extinctions.
--- context
The Eocene-Oligocene Transition follows the warmth of the [[event/paleocene-eocene-thermal-maximum|Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum]] in the [[era/paleogene-period|Paleogene Period]] and prepares the way for the [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the change looks like a threshold: a slow decline in carbon dioxide produced a rapid reorganisation once a critical level was crossed. The Observer files threshold behaviour as a feature of the climate system that the record shows plainly and that is hard to forecast in detail. The reading would weaken if the ice growth proved gradual, tracking carbon dioxide smoothly.
--- question 1
Was falling carbon dioxide or the opening of Southern Ocean gateways the main cause?
--- question 2
How quickly did the ice sheet reach its full size?

=== event/first-fleet-and-the-colonization-of-australia
title: First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia
type: event
date: 1788
themes: war, kinship
src: dae1984cdf9d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The First Fleet of eleven British ships reached Botany Bay in January 1788 carrying about 1,400 people, roughly half of them convicts, and founded the colony of New South Wales at Sydney Cove.
--- fact 1
The fleet arrived at Botany Bay on 18 to 20 January 1788 and settled at Sydney Cove on 26 January.
--- fact 2
Aboriginal Australians had lived on the continent for at least 50,000 years, and before 1788 they spoke more than 250 languages.
--- fact 3
The British claimed the land under the legal doctrine of terra nullius, which was rejected by the High Court of Australia in the Mabo decision of 3 June 1992.
--- fact 4
A smallpox epidemic struck Aboriginal communities around Sydney in 1789.
--- fact 5
Colonization led to conflict, dispossession, and population decline among Aboriginal peoples.
--- context
The fleet established a colony on land occupied for tens of thousands of years by [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]], descendants of the [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]]. === event/fish-to-tetrapod-transition
title: Fish-to-Tetrapod Transition
type: event
date: 385 – 360 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 28e117ac4d22
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Fish-to-Tetrapod Transition was the evolution of four-limbed vertebrates from lobe-finned fishes in the Middle and Late Devonian, roughly 385 to 360 million years ago. The sequence is well documented, but its start is debated.
--- fact 1
Tetrapods descend from lobe-finned fishes, a group whose fins hold a central skeleton of bones.
--- fact 2
Transitional fossils include Panderichthys from Latvia, about 380 million years old, [[species/tiktaalik|Tiktaalik]] from the Canadian Arctic, about 375 million years old, and Acanthostega and Ichthyostega from Greenland, about 365 million years old. The last two had limbs with digits but lived mostly in water.
--- fact 3
Limbs probably evolved for moving through shallow, vegetated water, and were used on land later; that reading is a hypothesis.
--- fact 4
Trackways from Zachełmie in Poland, dated to about 395 million years ago, have been read as tetrapod footprints. That would place limbed animals earlier than the known body fossils, but the interpretation is debated.
--- context
The Fish-to-Tetrapod Transition falls in the [[era/devonian-period|Devonian Period]] and is one thread of the [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]]. It precedes the [[event/late-devonian-extinction|Late Devonian Extinction]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the transition shows a feature's later use need not match its origin: limbs seem to have arisen for water and been used on land afterwards. It also notes that the fossils were found in part by predicting the rock age and setting where they should occur, which is an unusually testable case for the record. The reading would weaken if the earliest limbs proved to have served mainly for walking on land.
--- question 1
How old is the origin of limbed tetrapods, given the Zachełmie trackways?
--- question 2
Which living fish groups, if any, preserve traits of the first limbed animals?

=== event/formation-of-the-oceans
title: Formation of the Oceans
type: event
date: 4.4 – 4 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: 5efb9a8d4d0e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Formation of the Oceans is the time, probably between about 4.4 and 4.0 billion years ago, when liquid water first collected on Earth's surface as the young planet cooled. The date is uncertain and rests on indirect evidence.
--- fact 1
Oxygen-isotope ratios in some Jack Hills zircons, dated to about 4.4 billion years ago, have been read as evidence that their parent magma had interacted with liquid water; that reading is contested. See [[place/jack-hills-zircons|Jack Hills Zircons]].
--- fact 2
Earth's water came from the material that built the planet and from later impacts of water-rich bodies. Asteroids of the carbonaceous chondrite type match the hydrogen isotopes of ocean water well, but the shares from each source are debated.
--- fact 3
The young Sun gave only about 70 percent of its present light, so liquid water at the surface needed a strong greenhouse effect from gases such as carbon dioxide.
--- fact 4
The oldest rocks that clearly formed in water, including sediments and pillow-shaped lavas at Isua in Greenland, are about 3.7 to 3.8 billion years old, so oceans existed by then.
--- fact 5
The depth of the early oceans and the amount of land above them are debated.
--- context
The Formation of the Oceans falls in the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]], after the [[event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon|Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon]] and around the [[event/late-heavy-bombardment|Late Heavy Bombardment]]. The oceans were the setting for the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer files the oceans under preconditions: every later subject in the record assumes liquid water. That the planet had it depended on its size, its distance from the Sun and the contingent delivery of water, and the Observer treats the outcome as favourable circumstance, not design. The reading would weaken if water proved a near-inevitable product of planet formation at this distance.
--- question 1
How much of Earth's water was present at formation and how much arrived later?
--- question 2
When did stable, long-lived oceans first exist?

mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Great Ordovician Biodiversification was a long rise in the diversity of marine animals, running from about 485 to 460 million years ago. It multiplied the number of species and families within body plans that had first appeared in the Cambrian.
--- fact 1
By some counts, marine diversity at the family and genus level rose several-fold between the Early and Middle Ordovician.
--- fact 2
Brachiopods, bryozoans, molluscs, echinoderms such as crinoids, corals and sponges all diversified, and reefs and filter-feeding communities grew more complex. Plankton diversified as well.

Proposed causes include a cooling from the hot Cambrian climate, more oxygen, nutrients from volcanism, more shallow shelf habitat and ecological feedbacks. The mix is debated.
--- fact 5
The rise ended near the close of the period with the [[event/late-ordovician-extinction|Late Ordovician Extinction]].
--- context
The Great Ordovician Biodiversification is the main event of the [[era/ordovician-period|Ordovician Period]] within the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]] and follows the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that variety depends on ecological space as well as on novelty: the same body plans, given new habitats and richer food webs, yielded far more forms. It reads the event as a slow build after a fast start. The reading would weaken if diversity had risen with no link to habitat or climate.
--- question 1
What drove the biodiversification: climate, ocean chemistry, ecology, or a combination?
--- question 2
How much of the apparent rise reflects better preservation of Ordovician rocks?

date: 2.4 – 2.2 Ga

observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Huronian glaciation was a series of ice ages recorded in the Huronian Supergroup of Ontario, Canada, in the early Paleoproterozoic, about 2.4 to 2.2 billion years ago. It is one of the oldest well-documented ice ages.
--- fact 1
The Huronian Supergroup, north of Lake Huron, contains three glacial deposits, the Ramsay Lake, Bruce and Gowganda formations, separated by sediments laid down in warmer conditions.
--- fact 2
Glacial deposits of similar age are reported from other continents, including Wyoming and Finland. The Makganyene Formation in South Africa, about 2.2 billion years old, formed near the equator, which supports an early snowball state, though its link to the Huronian is debated.
--- fact 3
The glaciation follows the first rise of oxygen. One hypothesis is that oxygen destroyed atmospheric methane, a strong greenhouse gas, and so cooled the planet; other explanations involve volcanic and tectonic change.
--- fact 4
How long the ice lasted, and whether it covered the whole planet, are debated.
--- context
The Huronian Glaciation falls in the [[era/paleoproterozoic-era|Paleoproterozoic Era]] soon after the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] began, and it is the earliest of the ice ages of the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] before [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer files the Huronian under a caution about attribution: an ice age and an oxygen rise that share a date invite a causal story, and the story is plausible, but the record allows other explanations. The reading would strengthen or weaken with tighter dating of the glacial deposits against the first traces of oxygen.
--- question 1
Did the oxygen rise cause the glaciation, or did the two arise from a common cause?
--- question 2
Was the ice global?
