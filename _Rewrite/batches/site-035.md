=== era/middle-paleolithic
title: Middle Paleolithic
type: era
date: c. 300 – 50 ka
themes: technology, art
src: b10b96f1b208
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Middle Paleolithic is the middle part of the Paleolithic, from about 300,000 to about 50,000 years ago. It is the interval of prepared-core stone tools, of the earliest Homo sapiens, and of several human species living at the same time.
--- fact 1
The Middle Paleolithic runs from about 300,000 to about 50,000 years ago in Eurasia; Africa uses the name Middle Stone Age, and boundary dates vary by region. Toolmakers used prepared-core methods such as the Levallois technique, and the Mousterian industry of Europe and western Asia is linked mainly to Neanderthals.
--- fact 2
Several human species overlapped in time: [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]], [[species/denisovans|Denisovans]], [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]], [[species/homo-naledi|Homo naledi]] and [[species/homo-floresiensis|Homo floresiensis]]. The earliest Homo sapiens fossils are about 315,000 years old; see [[event/origin-of-homo-sapiens|Origin of Homo sapiens]].
--- fact 3
Homo sapiens fossils from Misliya Cave in Israel (about 185,000 years old) and from Skhul and Qafzeh caves (roughly 90,000 to 130,000 years old) show an early presence outside Africa; whether these groups contributed to living non-Africans is uncertain (see [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]]).
--- fact 4
Blombos Cave in South Africa has engraved ochre and ochre-processing tools about 75,000 to 100,000 years old, among the earliest signs of symbolic behaviour; see [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]].
--- fact 5
Neanderthals disappear from the fossil record by about 40,000 years ago. Whether they buried their dead or made art is disputed.
--- context
The Middle Paleolithic follows the [[era/lower-paleolithic|Lower Paleolithic]] and is followed by the [[era/upper-paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]] within the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]]. It belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]]. See the essay [[observer-note/why-the-species-buries-its-dead|Why the Species Buries Its Dead]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Middle Paleolithic is the first period in which several human species lived at the same time and sometimes in the same regions, so the family tree looks more like a bush than a ladder. Claims about their behaviour, especially burial and symbolic art, are disputed, and the Observer applies the same standard of evidence to each species. The reading would weaken if the fossils assigned to different species proved to be variants of one.
--- question 1
Did Neanderthals bury their dead or make art?
--- question 2
How much of the variety in Middle Paleolithic tools reflects different cultures rather than different raw materials?

=== era/upper-paleolithic
title: Upper Paleolithic
type: era
date: c. 50 ka – 10000 BCE
themes: art, technology, kinship
src: 00e31a7a09cc
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Upper Paleolithic is the last part of the Paleolithic, from about 50,000 to about 10,000 BCE. It is the interval of blade tools, bone and antler technology, abundant art and ornament, and the spread of Homo sapiens across the globe.
--- fact 1
The Upper Paleolithic runs from about 50,000 to about 10,000 BCE, ending with the warming at the close of the last ice age (Africa uses the name Later Stone Age). Toolmakers made long blades and worked bone, antler and ivory; eyed bone needles from Denisova Cave in Siberia, about 40,000 to 50,000 years old, suggest sewn clothing.
--- fact 2
Homo sapiens spread across the globe: Sahul by about 50,000 years ago ([[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]]), Europe by about 45,000 years ago, Beringia by about 30,000 years ago ([[place/beringia|Beringia]]), and the Americas by at least about 23,000 years ago ([[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]]). Neanderthals disappear from Europe about 40,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Painted, carved and engraved images are abundant: [[place/chauvet-cave|Chauvet Cave]] in France has paintings about 36,000 years old, and cave paintings in Sulawesi, Indonesia, include a narrative scene about 51,000 years old.
--- fact 4
The [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]], about 26,500 to 19,000 years ago, put ice over much of northern Eurasia and North America. Pottery appears in East Asia by about 20,000 years ago ([[technology/pottery|Pottery]]), and dogs were domesticated from wolves by at least about 15,000 years ago.
--- context
The Upper Paleolithic follows the [[era/middle-paleolithic|Middle Paleolithic]] and is followed by the [[era/epipaleolithic|Epipaleolithic]] in the Near East, within the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]]. It belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]]. See also [[event/quaternary-megafauna-extinctions|Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Upper Paleolithic sites hold the first large body of objects that seem made for display, ritual or play. It treats beads and ornaments as possible status-signals and burials as possible grief-rites, and it keeps in mind that a practice cannot be read from a surviving object alone. The reading would weaken if comparable objects proved common long before this period, which would make it a change in what survives, not in behaviour.
--- question 1
Is the apparent burst of art and ornament real, or a result of better preservation and more sites?
--- question 2
How did the Upper Paleolithic industries relate to specific human populations?

=== species/anomalocaris
title: Anomalocaris
type: species
date: 515 – 505 Ma
themes: earth-systems, science
src: c74bfb426ccc
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anomalocaris was a large swimming Cambrian animal with spiny grasping appendages, a round mouth ringed by plates, and large compound eyes. It lived about 515 to 505 million years ago, is known from Canada and Australia, and is a stem-group relative of arthropods.
--- fact 1
Anomalocaris is securely known from the Burgess Shale of British Columbia (about 505 million years old) and the Emu Bay Shale of South Australia (about 515). Chinese fossils once assigned to it have mostly been moved to other genera.
--- fact 2
Its appendages were described in 1892 as a crustacean tail, its mouth as a jellyfish, and its body as a sea cucumber. Harry Whittington and Derek Briggs showed in 1985 that they belonged to one animal.
--- fact 3
Complete Burgess specimens are about 20 to 25 cm long. Estimates of 35 to 50 cm include appendages and tail, and a claim of about 1 m rests on fragments.
--- fact 4
Compound eyes from the Emu Bay Shale, up to 3 cm long, preserve at least 16,000 lenses, comparable to the sharpest-sighted living arthropods.
--- fact 5
The mouth looks too weak to crush trilobite shells, and studies propose suction feeding or soft prey. Whether it was the top predator of its seas is debated.
--- context
Anomalocaris lived in the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]], a signature animal of the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], sharing seas with [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]], [[species/hallucigenia|Hallucigenia]], and [[species/pikaia|Pikaia]]. Its sites include the Burgess Shale, the Emu Bay Shale, and Chengjiang Fossil Site. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Anomalocaris was first assembled from three fossils believed to be three animals, and that the apex predator of popular pictures depends on how its mouth is read. It is one of many body plans that arose and vanished in the Cambrian, when nothing marked in advance which would leave descendants. The reading would weaken if radiodonts were shown to be ancestral to a living animal group.
--- question 1
What did Anomalocaris eat?
--- question 2
How many valid species does the genus include?

=== species/archaea
title: Archaea
type: species
date: 3.5 Ga
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 9ca892147305
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Archaea are single-celled organisms without a nucleus that form one of the three domains of life, with Bacteria and eukaryotes. Recognized as separate only in 1977, they may date to about 3.5 billion years ago, though no fossil archaeon has been identified with confidence.
--- fact 1
In 1977 Carl Woese and George Fox used ribosomal RNA sequences to show that methane-producing microbes were as distinct from bacteria as from eukaryotes. In 1990 the three domains were named Bacteria, Archaea, and Eucarya.
--- fact 2
Archaea were first known from hot springs, salt lakes, and methane-rich mud, but they also live in soils, deep sediments, and the open ocean, where in one Pacific study they made up about 39 percent of the smallest plankton at depth.
--- fact 3
The earliest evidence is indirect. Methane in Australian rocks about 3.5 billion years old was read in 2006 as microbial, but nonliving processes can give similar isotope values. Molecular clocks range from 3.5 to 4.1 billion years, and are also disputed.
--- fact 4
Asgard archaea, first found in 2015, carry genes for proteins otherwise known only in eukaryotes. Some researchers therefore place eukaryotes inside Archaea, which would leave two primary domains, not three.
--- fact 5
No archaeon is established as a human pathogen.
--- context
Archaea trace to the [[species/last-universal-common-ancestor|Last Universal Common Ancestor]] and the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]], and belong to the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]]. Their relatives gave rise to eukaryotic cells; see [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]]. Compare the bacterial [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a third branch of life sat unrecognized in hot springs and salt lakes until sequence comparison revealed it in 1977, and that the species' own cells possibly descend from a merger involving an archaeon. The Observer takes it that categories are set by the tools available. The reading would weaken if eukaryotes proved not to have archaeal ancestry.
--- question 1
When did archaea first appear?
--- question 2
Which Asgard lineage is closest to eukaryotes?

=== species/barley
title: Barley
type: species
date: c. 9000 BCE
themes: trade, kinship
src: caa00fe59033
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Barley is a cereal grass domesticated in the Fertile Crescent from around 9000 BCE, one of the earliest crops farmed by humans and, in ancient Mesopotamia, a staple used as food, drink and even a standard of value.
--- fact 1
Barley was domesticated from wild barley in the Fertile Crescent, with early domesticated forms recorded in the Jarmo region of modern Iraq from about 9000 to 7000 BCE; grinding stones with starch traces from wild barley at Ohalo II, near the Sea of Galilee, show it was eaten well before domestication, by about 23,000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Domestication produced grain heads that hold together instead of shattering, making the plant far easier to harvest but dependent on deliberate replanting.
--- fact 3
Barley was likely among the first grains brewed into beer, one of the earliest alcoholic drinks made by Neolithic farmers.
--- fact 4
In Mesopotamia, barley served as a de facto currency, with the shekel functioning as a standard unit of its weight, and cuneiform records from around 2350 BCE, under the ruler Urukagina, describe monthly barley rations issued to adults.
--- fact 5
By about 2000 BCE barley had spread across Eurasia, reaching Finland by about 4200 BCE, the Indus civilization region between roughly 5,700 and 3,300 years ago, and the Korean peninsula by about 1500 to 850 BCE.
--- fact 6
Global production reached about 146 million tonnes in 2023, the fourth largest cereal harvest after maize, rice and wheat; about 70 percent goes to animal feed and about 30 percent to malting for beer and whisky.
--- context
Barley was domesticated alongside [[species/wheat|Wheat]] as one of the founding crops of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent, and it underwrote the economies of early Mesopotamian cities such as [[place/ur|Ur]] and, later, [[place/babylon|Babylon]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a grain used as both food and a unit of account collapses two functions that many later societies kept separate, food and money, and that Mesopotamian rationing records turn barley into a record of a kin-lattice's obligations, who was owed what and by whom. Brewing barley into beer is one of the oldest recorded transformations of a raw crop into a socially significant product, tying farming to gathering and celebration. The reading would weaken if barley's early monetary role proves to have been more local and limited than the shekel-based accounts suggest.
--- question 1
How standard was barley's role as a unit of value across different Mesopotamian cities and periods?
--- question 2
How soon after domestication did brewing become widespread, as opposed to barley being eaten mainly as porridge or bread?

=== species/cacao
title: Cacao
type: species
date: c. 3300 BCE
themes: trade, religion
src: a9f39d96f777
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Cacao, the source of chocolate, was first domesticated in the Amazon basin of South America by at least 3300 BCE and later became a ritual drink and currency in Mesoamerica before Spanish traders carried it to Europe in the 16th century.
--- fact 1
Cacao's native range is the Amazon rainforest, and genetic and archaeological evidence places its earliest domestication at the Santa Ana-La Florida site in present-day Ecuador, by the Mayo-Chinchipe culture, at least 5,300 years ago, before it reached Mesoamerica.
--- fact 2
Chemical residue on pottery from Puerto Escondido, Honduras, shows cacao products were being consumed there between about 1500 and 1400 BCE, with the sweet pulp around the beans likely valued before the beans themselves.
--- fact 3
Across Mesoamerica before the Spanish conquest, cacao beans served as a currency for everyday purchases, and Olmec and Maya elites drank cacao in spiritual ceremonies; a Spanish chronicler recorded that the Aztec ruler Moctezuma II drank only chocolate, reportedly consuming dozens of servings a day from a golden goblet.
--- fact 4
Spanish traders introduced chocolate to Europe, and it became a popular drink there by the mid-17th century; the Spanish also spread cacao cultivation to their colonies in the West Indies and the Philippines.
--- fact 5
Ivory Coast and Ghana together grow most of the world's cacao today, and reporting on the industry has documented large-scale child labor: an estimated 2.1 million children were involved in cocoa harvesting in the two countries as of 2017, often exposed to pesticides and hazardous tasks; a 2021 lawsuit against major chocolate companies over forced child labor was dismissed for lack of a traceable link to specific plantations.
--- fact 6
Global cacao production reached about 5.8 million tonnes in 2022, with Ivory Coast alone supplying about 38 percent.
--- context
Cacao was central to the ritual and economic life of the [[culture/olmecs|Olmecs]], the [[culture/maya|Maya]] and the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] before Spanish contact, and its spread to Europe and other continents is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that cacao moved from a South American forest crop to a Mesoamerican meaning-engine, functioning at once as money, medicine and a marker of elite status, and then to a European luxury good stripped of most of that ritual weight and remade as a sweet. Today's cacao economy repeats an old pattern in a new form: a crop grown mainly by smallholders in West Africa supplies a global confectionery industry, and the labor of children within that supply chain is recorded here as harm, whatever the difficulty of tracing it to a specific buyer. The reading would weaken if cacao's currency role in Mesoamerica proves to have been more limited or local than the general accounts suggest.
--- question 1
How did cacao move from its South American origin to becoming culturally central in Mesoamerica, and by what route and over what timescale?
--- question 2
How effective have industry pledges and lawsuits been at reducing child labor in West African cacao production?

=== species/cassava
title: Cassava
type: species
date: c. 4600 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 24b111a07569
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cassava is a starchy root crop domesticated in south-central Brazil, likely within the last 10,000 years, that requires processing to remove natural toxins and that Portuguese and Spanish traders carried to Africa and Asia in the 16th century, where it became a staple for hundreds of millions of people.
--- fact 1
Cassava was domesticated from wild relatives centered in west-central Brazil; the date of domestication is imprecise, likely within the last 10,000 years, and cassava pollen appears in Gulf of Mexico lowland sediment by about 4600 BCE, with the oldest direct evidence of cultivation coming from the Maya site of Joya de Cerén in El Salvador, about 1,400 years old.
--- fact 2
Raw cassava contains cyanogenic compounds, linamarin and lotaustralin, that release toxic hydrogen cyanide; bitter varieties can carry up to 1,000 milligrams of cyanide per kilogram of fresh root, and communities developed soaking and fermentation methods, some reducing cyanide content by 85 to 99 percent, to make the crop safe to eat.
--- fact 3
Portuguese traders introduced cassava from Brazil to West Africa in the 16th century, and Portuguese and Spanish traders also carried it to their colonies in Goa, Malacca, eastern Indonesia, Timor and the Philippines; it reached East Africa by about 1850 through Arab and European traders.
--- fact 4
Cassava is now a primary food staple for an estimated 500 million to 800 million people, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, and it ranks as the tropics' third-largest carbohydrate source after rice and maize, valued for growing on poor soils with little rainfall.
--- fact 5
Global cassava production reached about 330 million tonnes in 2022, with Nigeria the largest producer and Thailand the largest exporter of cassava starch.
--- context
Cassava's domestication belongs to the wider [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Americas, and its 16th-century spread to Africa and Asia is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; in West and Central Africa it became a staple food during the era of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that cassava is unusual among staple crops in requiring a tool-lineage of processing, soaking or fermenting, before it can be safely eaten, so its adoption in Africa and Asia carried with it a body of practical knowledge as well as the plant itself, an example of an exchange-web moving techniques alongside seeds. Its tolerance of poor soil made it valuable precisely where colonial and later national agricultural systems demanded a reliable staple with little investment, which the Observer reads as an example of a crop's usefulness under stress shaping which regions came to depend on it. The reading would weaken if processing knowledge is shown to have traveled independently of the crop rather than together with it.
--- question 1
How did processing knowledge for detoxifying cassava spread alongside the crop itself in Africa and Asia?
--- question 2
How much do modern reliance on a narrow range of cassava varieties leave current staple supplies vulnerable to disease, as happened with the potato in 19th-century Europe?

=== species/cattle
title: Cattle
type: species
date: c. 8500 BCE
themes: trade, religion, earth-systems
src: b2cd201aeb97
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Cattle are large grazing animals domesticated from the wild aurochs in southwestern Asia about 10,500 years ago, and with over 940 million animals today they are among the most numerous and heaviest livestock on Earth.
--- fact 1
Domestic cattle descend from the aurochs (Bos primigenius), a wild ox that once ranged across Europe, North Africa and much of Asia; humans hunted aurochs long before domestication, and that older history is outside this note.
--- fact 2
Taurine cattle were domesticated about 10,500 years ago in a zone spanning central Anatolia, the Levant and western Iran, and mitochondrial DNA suggests the taurine line may descend from as few as about 80 aurochs tamed in upper Mesopotamia.
--- fact 3
A separate domestication on the Indian subcontinent produced the humped zebu cattle.
--- fact 4
Cattle are kept for meat, milk and leather and as draft animals that pull carts and farm implements; taurine cattle spread across Europe and temperate Asia and were later carried by Europeans to the Americas and Australia.
--- fact 5
More than 940 million cattle lived in the world in 2022, led by India (about 307 million), Brazil (about 194 million) and China (about 102 million); by one estimate they have the largest biomass of any animal species, about 650 million tonnes.
--- fact 6
Cattle farming accounts for about 7 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions, mostly methane from digestion, and overgrazing has reduced biodiversity in some rangelands.
--- fact 7
Cattle are sacred in Hinduism, and slaughter of cows is forbidden by law in several Indian states and in Nepal.
--- context
Cattle were brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] alongside [[species/sheep|Sheep]] and [[species/goat|Goat]] as part of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in southwestern Asia, and their carrying to the Americas after 1492 belongs to the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. Cattle sit in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: cattle turn grass that humans cannot digest into milk, meat and pulling power, so a herd works as wealth that walks, and in many pastoral societies its size acts as a status-signal and a record of kin-lattice obligations. The same animal is property in one society and sacred in another, so one species carries opposite meaning-engines without changing at all, and its methane now ties a Neolithic partnership to the planet's climate. The reading would weaken if herd size proves to have signaled status in far fewer societies than this account assumes.
--- question 1
How many separate taming events lie behind taurine cattle, and how did zebu and taurine lines mix as herds moved across Asia and Africa?
--- question 2
How did ploughing with cattle change the size of fields and settlements after domestication?

=== species/chicken
title: Chicken
type: species
date: c. 1250 BCE
themes: trade, technology, earth-systems
src: 2a9705e7c1b5
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The chicken descends from the red junglefowl of Southeast Asia, was carried across the Pacific and the Old World, and is now the most numerous bird on Earth, with more than 26 billion alive in 2023; the timing of its domestication is uncertain.
--- fact 1
Chickens descend from the red junglefowl (Gallus gallus), native to Southeast Asia, and still interbreed with it.
--- fact 2
Genomic studies estimate that chickens were domesticated about 8,000 years ago in Southeast Asia, but the earliest bones judged probably chicken come from Ban Non Wat in central Thailand, about 3,250 years old, and older claims from China are debated; the date_start of -1250 marks that earliest secure evidence, not the beginning of domestication.
--- fact 3
One proposal holds that wild junglefowl were drawn to rice seeds and nested nearby, and another that chickens were kept first for cockfighting and only later for food; researchers dispute both.
--- fact 4
Chickens spread with Austronesian-speaking voyagers across island Southeast Asia, Micronesia, Melanesia, Polynesia and Madagascar, and reached Egypt from the Middle East about 1400 BCE, becoming widely bred there by about 300 BCE.
--- fact 5
Whether chickens reached the Americas before European contact is debated: bones from Chile's Arauco Peninsula were dated as pre-Columbian and linked by DNA to Polynesian birds, but further study cast doubt on the result.
--- fact 6
More than 26.5 billion chickens were alive in 2023 and more than 50 billion were produced annually for food; about 74 percent of the world's poultry meat and 68 percent of eggs come from factory farming.
--- fact 7
A modern broiler may weigh about 1.8 kilograms at 35 days against about 1.05 kilograms for a heritage bird, and the life expectancy of commercial laying hens falls from around seven years to under two.
--- context
The chicken's early history may be tied to the fields of the [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]], it traveled with the [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] and the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]], and it was bred widely by the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]]. It belongs to the wider record of [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]]. The Chicken sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a bird first kept for reasons we cannot yet fix, food, ritual or sport, has become the largest vertebrate population humans have ever raised, and that its industrial form shows a tool-lineage of breeding and housing that reshapes an animal within a few decades. Many people regard the conditions of industrial flocks as suffering and others weigh them against low food prices, and the Observer records that this disagreement is now part of the chicken's history. The reading would weaken if earlier secure chicken remains are found that change the sequence of its domestication.
--- question 1
When and where were chickens first domesticated, and were they kept first for food, sport or ritual?
--- question 2
Did chickens reach the Americas before 1492?

=== species/coffee
title: Coffee
type: species
date: c. 1450
themes: trade, religion
src: 09964d1d975c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Coffee is a shrub whose beans, first documented as a drink among Sufi communities in Yemen by the late 15th century, spread through the Ottoman Empire and Europe and were then grown on slave and colonial plantations across the tropics.
--- fact 1
Coffee's popular origin story, in which a 9th-century Ethiopian goatherd named Kaldi discovers the plant's effects on his flock, does not appear in writing before 1671 and is likely a later legend; there is no confirmed evidence of coffee as a drink before the 15th century, when it was well established among Sufi communities in Yemen.
--- fact 2
The first known coffeehouse opened in Istanbul in 1555, and Pope Clement VIII's declaration of coffee as an acceptable Christian drink in 1600 helped it spread in Catholic Europe; the first European coffeehouse outside Ottoman territory opened in Venice in 1647.
--- fact 3
French naval officer Gabriel de Clieu brought coffee plants to Martinique in the 1720s, and cultivation began in the French colony of Saint-Domingue (Haiti) in 1734; by 1788 Saint-Domingue, worked by enslaved laborers, supplied roughly half the world's coffee, and the harsh conditions on its plantations were a contributing factor in the Haitian Revolution.
--- fact 4
Coffee reached Brazil in 1727, but large-scale cultivation there did not take off until after independence in 1822.
--- fact 5
Coffee cultivation across its colonial-era expansion generally displaced and exploited Indigenous and enslaved populations, a pattern recorded here as sustained harm rather than as an incidental cost of production.
--- fact 6
Global coffee production reached about 11 million tonnes in 2023, with Brazil producing about 31 percent and Vietnam the second-largest share.
--- context
Coffee's early spread from Yemen through the Ottoman Empire followed routes tied to the wider [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]], and its colonial-era plantation economy in the Caribbean is linked to the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and helped provoke the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]; its global spread after the 16th century belongs to the broader [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] of plants and practices between continents. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that coffee began its documented history bound up with a meaning-engine, used by Sufi communities to stay alert for nighttime devotions, before becoming a secular drink of Ottoman and European coffeehouses that themselves became new sites of conversation and commerce, a shift in the substance's story-glue from devotion to sociability. Its later spread onto slave-worked plantations shows the same plant enabling both a set of religious practices and one of the most extractive labor systems in early modern history, and the Observer treats the suffering on those plantations as inseparable from the substance's global rise. The reading would weaken if the popular Kaldi legend proves to rest on an older oral tradition rather than being a 17th-century invention.
--- question 1
How much of coffee's early diffusion outside Yemen followed Sufi religious networks specifically, as opposed to general trade contacts?
--- question 2
How did the labor conditions on 18th-century coffee plantations compare with those on sugar plantations in the same colonies?

=== species/cotton
title: Cotton
type: species
date: c. 3000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: 991ae9f64abd
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cotton was domesticated independently in the Indus Valley of South Asia and in the Americas, and its later mechanized processing in Britain and the American South tied the plant directly to the Industrial Revolution and to the expansion of slavery.
--- fact 1
Cotton textile fragments and spindle whorls from Mohenjo-daro in the Indus Valley date to the 3rd millennium BCE, and the date_start of -3000 marks this firmly dated use; mineralized thread at Mehrgarh, dated by some to the 6th millennium BCE, would be earlier but is less certain.
--- fact 2
Cotton was domesticated separately in the Americas, with Gossypium hirsutum in Mexico dated to about 3400 to 2300 BCE, bolls from a Tehuacán cave dated to as early as 5500 BCE, and a different species in use in Peru by the 7th to 6th millennia BCE.
--- fact 3
Indian cotton textiles, especially from Bengal and Dhaka, were the most important manufactured goods in 18th-century world trade and held about a quarter of the global textile trade, until British colonial tariffs and policy suppressed Indian exports and favored British cloth.
--- fact 4
Processing a bale of cotton by hand took over 600 hours of labor until Eli Whitney's gin, patented in 1793, cut that to about a dozen hours; the gin made large-scale growing far more profitable and drove a rapid expansion of slavery in the American South, where cotton was the backbone of the regional economy by the mid-19th century.
--- fact 5
India is the largest cotton producer today and the United States has long been the largest exporter; world output is roughly 25 million tonnes a year on about 2.5 percent of the world's arable land.
--- fact 6
Cotton needs an estimated 8,000 to 10,000 liters of water per kilogram of fiber worldwide and up to about 22,500 liters in drier regions of India, and conventional growing relies heavily on fertilizers and pesticides, though pest-resistant Bt cotton has cut insecticide use in some regions.
--- context
Cotton's earliest Old World cultivation is tied to the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], and its colonial-era displacement in favor of British manufacturing became central to the economy of the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]]. Its mechanized processing fed the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and the plantation slavery that helped bring on the [[event/american-civil-war|American Civil War]], and the enslaved labor that grew it in the American South is part of the wider [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. Cotton sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that cotton was domesticated independently in at least two hemispheres, so its usefulness was discovered separately and not diffused from one origin, and that a single invention, the gin, turned a labor bottleneck into an incentive to enslave more people, a case where a tool-lineage advance amplified sanctioned harm instead of reducing it. Britain's suppression of Indian manufacturing in favor of its own industry also shows an exchange-web reshaped by state policy to benefit one side. The reading would weaken if the gin's role in expanding slavery proves small relative to rising cotton prices and available land.
--- question 1
How independent were the Old World and American domestications of cotton, and did any early contact or diffusion occur between them?
--- question 2
How much of the decline of Indian cotton manufacturing reflects deliberate colonial policy as opposed to British industrial efficiency alone?

=== species/domestic-cat
title: Domestic Cat
type: species
date: c. 7500 BCE
themes: religion, earth-systems
src: 4bd46517d364
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The domestic cat descends from the African wildcat and was first tamed in the Near East by Neolithic farmers, with the earliest evidence of a tamed wildcat buried near a human grave on Cyprus about 7500 to 7200 BCE.
--- fact 1
Domestic cats descend from the African wildcat (Felis lybica), and the earliest known sign of a tamed wildcat is a burial found close to a human Neolithic grave at Shillourokambos, southern Cyprus, dated to about 7500 to 7200 BCE.
--- fact 2
The leading explanation is that African wildcats were drawn to early farming settlements in the Fertile Crescent by rodents, especially the house mouse, and were tolerated and then tamed, a long relationship that lasted thousands of years.
--- fact 3
Cats were venerated in Egypt from about 3100 BCE, and Egyptian wildcats later contributed to the maternal gene pool of the domestic cat.
--- fact 4
As farming spread, so did cats: they reached Greece about 1200 BCE with traders, southern Europe by the 5th century BCE and northern Germany by the 5th century CE.
--- fact 5
In the United States alone domestic cats are estimated to kill 1.3 to 4.0 billion birds and 6.3 to 22.3 billion mammals a year, and abandoned pets add to a global feral cat population that has driven declines of bird, mammal and reptile species.
--- context
Cats entered human settlements through the storage of [[species/wheat|Wheat]] and [[species/barley|Barley]] in [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], and their veneration by the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] is a well-known case of a domesticated animal treated as sacred. Cats belong to the wider record of [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]]. The Domestic Cat sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: the cat is the strongest case for the idea that an animal may have chosen the arrangement more than humans did, since a hunter of grain-store rodents needed no training and asked only to be left alone, so the phrase "who domesticated whom" fits here better than for most species. The same animal was honored in Egyptian ritual and now, in large numbers outdoors, is a predator of wild birds and small mammals, a reminder that a meaning-engine's favorite creature can also be an ecological actor. The reading would weaken if the Cyprus burial proves to be an isolated taming with no descendants among modern cats.
--- question 1
Did cats spread from a single Near Eastern source, or did more than one wildcat population contribute to the domestic cat?
--- question 2
How did the veneration of cats in Egypt affect the movement of cats across the Mediterranean?

=== species/domestic-pig
title: Domestic Pig
type: species
date: c. 9400 BCE
themes: trade, religion
src: cb819e656b64
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The domestic pig was bred from the wild boar in the Near East, in or around the Tigris basin, by about 9400 BCE, was domesticated again independently in China, and is now tied with chicken as the most commonly eaten meat in the world.
--- fact 1
Domestic pigs descend from the wild boar (Sus scrofa) and were first domesticated in the Near East, in or around the Tigris basin; pigs brought to Cyprus more than 11,400 years ago imply domestication or close management on the mainland by then.
--- fact 2
Pigs were domesticated separately in China starting some 8,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Pigs reached Europe at least 8,500 years ago and over the next 3,000 years interbred with European wild boar until their genomes held less than 5 percent Near Eastern ancestry, a change that amounts to a further, local domestication.
--- fact 4
Austronesian-speaking peoples carried pigs across island Southeast Asia and Oceania beginning about 4,000 years ago, and most pigs from the Philippines to Hawai‘i descend from those introductions.
--- fact 5
Spanish explorers including Hernando de Soto brought pigs to southeastern North America, where escaped animals became feral; feral pigs now damage crops and wildlife in the United States, New Zealand, northern Queensland and South America.
--- fact 6
Eating pork is forbidden in Islam and Judaism, and pigs are sacred in some other religions.
--- fact 7
About 850 million pigs were alive in 2019, with China holding about 36.5 percent, and roughly 1.5 billion are slaughtered each year.
--- context
Pigs were among the animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] as part of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], they crossed the Pacific with the [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] and the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]], and they reached the Americas through the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. The Domestic Pig sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the pig is at once one of the most eaten animals and one of the most avoided, so a single food can work as a claim-line drawn at the table between communities; the origins of the prohibitions are debated, and the Observer does not choose among the explanations. Pigs also travel well, since they eat almost anything and breed quickly, which made them useful cargo for Austronesian voyagers and Spanish ships, an exchange-web carrying a whole food supply across oceans. The reading would weaken if the prohibitions prove to have had little to do with marking group boundaries.
--- question 1
Where and when were pigs first domesticated in the Near East, given that the Cyprus evidence points to an earlier date than most mainland finds?
--- question 2
How did the replacement of Near Eastern pig lineages in Europe unfold, and did local people domesticate wild boar on their own?

=== species/donkey
title: Donkey
type: species
date: c. 4000 BCE
themes: trade, technology
src: dc66894087d7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The donkey was domesticated from the African wild ass in northeastern Africa, probably by pastoralists in Nubia, some 5,000 to 7,000 years ago, and became the main pack animal of early long-distance trade in Egypt and Southwest Asia.
--- fact 1
Donkeys descend from the African wild ass (Equus africanus) and were domesticated in Africa some 5,000 to 7,000 years ago, probably by pastoral people in Nubia; remains from the fourth millennium BCE have been found at Ma'adi in Lower Egypt, and the date_start of -4000 is the midpoint of that range.
--- fact 2
Donkeys were vital to long-distance trade in Egypt, and in the Fourth Dynasty (about 2675 to 2565 BCE) wealthy individuals were known to own more than 1,000 of them, used as pack animals and in farming and for milk and meat.
--- fact 3
By the end of the fourth millennium BCE donkeys had spread to Southwest Asia, the main breeding center had shifted to Mesopotamia by about 1800 BCE, and the Greeks and Romans later spread them through their empires.
--- fact 4
A male donkey crossed with a female horse produces a mule, and the reverse cross produces a hinny; mules are valued as working and riding animals in many countries.
--- fact 5
More than 40 million donkeys were alive in the early 2000s, about 96 percent of them in developing countries, where they are used mainly as pack animals or for draught work.
--- context
Donkeys served the trade networks of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] and were probably first kept by pastoralists in Nubia, the region later home to the [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]], before the [[species/horse|Horse]] and the [[species/dromedary-and-bactrian-camels|Dromedary and Bactrian Camels]] took over much of the long-distance carrying. They are part of [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]]. The Donkey sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the animal which made early caravans possible is seldom the one honored in a society's story-glue, since chariots and cavalry receive the poems and the donkey carried the grain, copper and cloth; the record is therefore weighted toward what humans admired, not what they relied on. Owning a thousand donkeys was a status-signal in the Fourth Dynasty, which suggests that carrying capacity was itself a form of wealth. The reading would weaken if excavated caravan and settlement records show that horses or camels carried more early long-distance cargo than donkeys did.
--- question 1
Was the donkey domesticated once or several times, and where exactly did the process begin?
--- question 2
How large was the donkey trade between Egypt and neighboring regions during the third millennium BCE?

=== species/dromedary-and-bactrian-camels
title: Dromedary and Bactrian Camels
type: species
date: c. 2500 BCE
themes: trade, war
src: 0eccec938e1d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Two camel species were domesticated by about the middle of the third millennium BCE, the one-humped dromedary in southern Arabia or Somalia and the two-humped Bactrian camel in Central Asia, and together they made long-distance caravan travel across deserts possible.
--- fact 1
The dromedary was domesticated in Somalia or southern Arabia during the third millennium BCE, though a 2016 genetic study points to southeastern Arabia.
--- fact 2
The Bactrian camel was domesticated in Central Asia around 2500 BCE, with evidence at Shahr-e Sukhteh in Iran, and one analysis places its domestication east of the Zagros Mountains by the middle of the third millennium BCE.
--- fact 3
The military Arabian saddle developed in the 7th century BCE, and between about 500 and 100 BCE Bactrian camels entered military use with new saddles placed over the humps.
--- fact 4
About 14 million camels were alive in 2010, roughly 90 percent of them dromedaries; the domestic Bactrian numbers about 1.4 million, and the wild Bactrian, a separate species, is reduced to about 1,000 animals and is still declining.
--- fact 5
Frankincense and myrrh moved from southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa to Mediterranean and Mesopotamian markets mostly by camel caravan, for well over a thousand years.
--- fact 6
More than a million dromedaries are estimated to be feral in Australia, a population growing by about 8 percent a year that stood at about 700,000 in 2008.
--- context
Camels made the desert routes of the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]] work, linking the [[culture/kingdom-of-saba|Kingdom of Saba]] and the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]] to Mediterranean and Mesopotamian markets, and Bactrian camels served the overland routes that became the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. Both species are part of [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]]. The camels sit in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that camels changed what counted as an obstacle: a desert that stopped other animals became a road that only certain traders could use, so the people who held wells and caravan stops held a claim-line over the flow of goods, and cities such as those of Saba and the Nabataeans grew on that control. A saddle is a small tool-lineage change, yet the Arabian and Bactrian saddles turned a pack animal into a war animal within a few centuries. The reading would weaken if the routes proved to have been used mainly for local exchange rather than long-distance trade.
--- question 1
Where exactly was the dromedary first domesticated, and how did it reach North Africa?
--- question 2
How much did the saddle innovations change the volume of trade across the deserts?
