=== culture/pagan-kingdom
title: Pagan Kingdom
type: culture
date: 1044 – 1287
themes: religion, art, war
src: 09845cc89556
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Pagan Kingdom united the Irrawaddy valley in what is now Myanmar under King Anawrahta and his successors, made Theravada Buddhism its state religion, and built thousands of temples at Bagan before falling to the Mongols in 1287.
--- fact 1
Anawrahta reigned from 11 August 1044 to 11 April 1077. He founded the Pagan Empire in the 1050s and 1060s, apparently unifying the Irrawaddy valley and its edges for the first time.
--- fact 2
Anawrahta was converted to Theravada Buddhism by a Mon monk, Shin Arahan. The Mon royal family, scholars, and craftsmen were brought to Pagan, and a form of Theravada adopted from Sri Lanka became official around 1056.
--- fact 3
Pagan's rulers built more than 10,000 Buddhist temples in the Bagan Archaeological Zone, of which more than 2,000 survive.
--- fact 4
Mongol armies invaded from 1277 to 1287. Pagan's rule ended in 1287, and Myinsaing took over on 17 December 1287.
--- context
Pagan was a contemporary of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] at [[place/angkor|Angkor]] to the east, and its end came through the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a ruler who used one shared meaning-engine to bind a population, and a building programme so large that the surviving remnant of it still fills a plain. Status-signal was directed upward at the divine, and the result outlasted the state that paid for it.
--- question 1
How much of the Mongol effect on Pagan was military destruction, and how much was the state's own weakness before 1277?
--- question 2
How were more than 10,000 temples financed and maintained?

=== culture/phoenicians
title: Phoenicians
type: culture
date: c. 1200 – 300 BCE
themes: trade, language
src: cff6c161bea8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Phoenicians were seafaring traders from city-states on the Levant coast (such as Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos) who founded colonies across the Mediterranean.
--- fact 1
They founded Carthage, traditionally in 814 BCE.
--- fact 2
They spread a simple alphabet of about 22 letters.
--- fact 3
They were known for purple dye and for shipbuilding.
--- fact 4
Few Phoenician writings survive; most of what is known comes from others.
--- context
The Phoenicians linked the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] by sea and spread the [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]] after the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a network of commercial city-states rather than a territorial empire, an example of an exchange-web that carries a technology further than any army.
--- question 1
How did Phoenician colonies relate to the home cities?

=== culture/polynesians
title: Polynesians
type: culture
date: c. 1300 BCE
themes: technology, science, kinship
src: 07b3f7482d19
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Polynesians are the Austronesian-speaking peoples of the central and eastern Pacific, who developed long-range voyaging and settled the islands from Hawai'i to Aotearoa and Rapa Nui.
--- fact 1
Ancestors of Polynesians, the Lapita people, spread through Near Oceania and reached Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa between about 1300 and 900 BCE.
--- fact 2
Voyagers used double-hulled canoes and non-instrument navigation.
--- fact 3
Societies ranged from chiefdoms to kingdoms, such as in Hawai'i and Tonga.
--- fact 4
Rapa Nui (Easter Island) is known for its large stone statues (moai).
--- context
See [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] and [[region/oceania|Oceania]].
--- observer
The Observer regards Polynesian wayfinding as an extraordinary case of embodied science: a full body of predictive knowledge held in memory and song.
--- question 1
How were they able to find and return to small islands?

=== culture/qing-dynasty

type: culture
date: 1644 – 1912
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: b7889de2f54b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Qing dynasty (1644 to 1912) was the last imperial dynasty of China, founded by the Manchus after they overthrew the Ming, and it expanded China's borders to their largest extent.
--- fact 1
The Qing extended control over Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang.
--- fact 2
Population and the economy grew strongly in the 18th century.
--- fact 3
The dynasty faced the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]], the [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]], and the [[event/boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]] in the 19th century.
--- fact 4
The last emperor, Puyi, abdicated in February 1912.
--- context
The Qing followed the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] and ended with the 1911 revolution. See [[region/east-asia|East Asia]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a dynasty of outsiders who ruled by adopting the forms of the ruled: Manchu rulers governed through Confucian institutions. Ruling a large population often means adopting its ways.
--- question 1
How Chinese, and how Manchu, was Qing rule?

Isfahan became known for its mosques, squares, and gardens.
--- fact 5
The Safavid state collapsed after an Afghan invasion in 1722 and ended in 1736.

The Safavids re-established a strong Persian state and rivaled the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] and the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]]. --- observer
The Observer notes that Iran's largely Shia identity today can be traced to the deliberate policy of one dynasty. A meaning-engine can be adopted by state decision within a few decades and then treated as ancient.

=== culture/scythians
title: Scythians
type: culture
date: c. 900 – 200 BCE
themes: war, art, trade
src: 5e8191fbd0a2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
'Scythian' is a general name for the horse-riding nomads who lived across the Eurasian steppe in the Iron Age, from about the 9th to the 3rd century BCE, known from Greek writers and rich burial mounds.
--- fact 1
Herodotus (5th century BCE) described Scythian customs, including burial rites and warfare.
--- fact 2
The royal mound Arzhan 1 in Tuva dates to the late 9th century BCE; Arzhan 2 held about 5,600 gold objects, including a cloak studded with some 2,500 gold panthers.
--- fact 3
Frozen tombs at Pazyryk in the Altai, of the 4th to 3rd centuries BCE, preserved textiles, tattoos, and horse gear.
--- fact 4
The Scythians lived by herding horses, sheep, and cattle, moving with the seasons.
--- context
The Scythians interacted with the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] and the Greeks. See [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]] and [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a people who left almost no writing of their own, yet whose art in gold and felt survives at extraordinary quality. Their story reaches us largely through outsiders' texts and their own graves.
--- question 1
How connected were the far-flung 'Scythian' groups?

=== culture/shinto
title: Shinto
type: culture
date: c. 300 BCE
themes: religion
src: 3c5bfad5a45b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Shinto is the indigenous religious tradition of Japan, centered on kami, a broad category of spirits, deified ancestors and natural forces, with no single founder or scripture and a long history of local shrine practice.
--- fact 1
Its practices trace to communal rites of purification, offering and harmony with nature among agricultural communities of the Yayoi period, roughly 300 BCE to 300 CE, with no founder or original canonical text.
--- fact 2
Kami covers gods, spirits, deified people, ancestors and natural phenomena, worshipped through offerings and ritual at shrines.
--- fact 3
By 1945 Japan had 218 national shrines and about 110,000 local ones.
--- fact 4
From 1882 State Shinto held the emperor to be descended from the sun goddess Amaterasu and demanded loyalty on that basis, until the Allied occupation's 1945 Shinto Directive ended state sponsorship and the emperor renounced claims to divinity.
--- fact 5
Only 3 to 4 million people worldwide formally identify as Shinto, though many in Japan take part in its rites alongside Buddhism.
--- context
Shinto developed in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] in Japan during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] with no single founding date. It was mobilized as State Shinto around the reign of [[person/emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]] and lost state sponsorship after [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a diffuse tradition of local shrine practice was gathered in the late nineteenth century and pointed at one claim, the emperor's divine descent, turning scattered story-glue into a tool of the state that occupying powers dismantled in a single directive. Few people formally name Shinto their religion, yet many join its shrine rites, so identification counts little of the practice. A specialist could argue that participation, not adherent counts, measures the tradition, which would weaken any reading of its reach from identification numbers alone.
--- question 1
How much does formal identification undercount actual participation in Shinto practice?
--- question 2
How much of today's shrine practice descends from before the State Shinto period?

=== culture/sikhism
title: Sikhism
type: culture
date: 1469
themes: religion
src: 93867f15c5ee
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sikhism began in the Punjab region of South Asia with the teaching of Guru Nanak, born in 1469, and developed through a line of ten human teachers into a monotheistic tradition with its own scripture and, since 1699, a formal order of initiated members called the Khalsa.
--- fact 1
Guru Nanak (1469 to 1539) taught belief in one God, social equality across caste lines, and rejection of ritualism, gathering the first Sikh community in Punjab.
--- fact 2
Nine more teachers followed him, together the Ten Gurus, regarded by Sikhs as sharing one continuous spirit of guidance.
--- fact 3
In 1699 the tenth guru, Gobind Singh, founded the Khalsa, a discipline of initiated Sikhs marked by the Five Ks, articles of faith including uncut hair, organized in part to defend the faith.
--- fact 4
Before his death, Gobind Singh declared that Sikhs should treat their scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib, a compilation of hymns by the gurus and other spiritual figures, as their living guru, ending the line of human gurus.
--- fact 5
About 25 to 30 million Sikhs live today, mostly in Punjab, India, where they form a majority of the region's population, with further communities across the global Sikh diaspora.
--- context
Sikhism arose in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], within the Punjab region later ruled in part by the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that when the line of living teachers ended in 1699, the community shifted memory-outsourcing onto a book, declaring a text itself a guru, an unusual solution other traditions solve through institutions or bloodlines. The Khalsa also shows a meaning-engine adopting sanctioned self-defense as part of its identity in response to persecution. A specialist could argue that ending the line of human gurus reflected particular circumstances of Gobind Singh's own time as much as settled theology, which would weaken a reading of it as inevitable.
--- question 1
How did the decision to end the line of human gurus shape the community's later development?
--- question 2
How much of the Khalsa's military character reflects a response to specific persecution versus an intended permanent feature of the faith?

=== culture/sintashta-culture
title: Sintashta Culture
type: culture
date: c. 2100 – 1800 BCE
themes: war, technology, kinship
src: 1637ed28afb4
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Sintashta culture was a Bronze Age society of the southern Urals steppe, from about 2100 to 1800 BCE, known for fortified settlements and the earliest known spoke-wheeled chariots.
--- fact 1
About 20 or more fortified settlements are known, including Sintashta and Arkaim, with concentric walls, ditches, and towers.
--- fact 2
Elite graves contain horses, weapons, and chariots with spoked wheels dated to about 2000 BCE.
--- fact 3
The culture is often linked, though not conclusively, to speakers of early Indo-Iranian languages.
--- fact 4
Chariot warfare later spread across Eurasia.
--- context
See [[technology/the-wheel|The Wheel]] and [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. The Sintashta may have contributed to chariot use in [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]] and Near Eastern warfare.
--- observer
The Observer notes a culture of walled villages and elite horse burials, a sign that violence and prestige were entangled with a new technology.
--- question 1
What was the Sintashta's relationship to later Indo-Iranian peoples?

=== culture/sogdians
title: Sogdians
type: culture
date: c. 300 CE
themes: trade, language, religion
src: 25f374260466
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Sogdians were an Iranian-speaking people of the Central Asian oases (Samarkand, Bukhara, Panjikent) who became the leading merchants of the Silk Roads from about the 4th century CE.
--- fact 1
They built trading colonies from Central Asia to China, acting as caravan leaders, interpreters, and bankers.
--- fact 2
Their language served as a lingua franca along the Silk Roads and as a court language of the First Turkic Khaganate.
--- fact 3
The 'Ancient Letters' of about 313 CE, found in a Great Wall tower, show Sogdian colonies writing to the home base in Samarkand.
--- fact 4
Sogdians helped spread Buddhism, Manichaeism, and later other religions across Asia.
--- context
The Sogdians carried the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]] trade through [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]] and served [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]] China and the Turkic khaganates.
--- observer
The Observer notes that a people who never built a large empire shaped the economy of Eurasia. Merchants who speak many languages can be more influential than rulers who speak one.
--- question 1
How did Sogdian merchant communities organize credit and trust?

=== culture/xiongnu
title: Xiongnu
type: culture
date: c. 209 BCE – 93 CE
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 8e13296bfb39
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Xiongnu were a confederation of steppe peoples united under Modu Shanyu in 209 BCE, which became the first great steppe empire and the main rival of Han China.
--- fact 1
Chinese sources report that in 200 BCE Modu surrounded the Han emperor Gaozu at Baideng.
--- fact 2
The Han then adopted the 'heqin' policy: marrying a princess to the ruler and sending annual gifts of silk, grain, and other goods.
--- fact 3
The treaty was renewed many times until about 135 BCE.
--- fact 4
The confederation split around 51 BCE, and the northern Xiongnu were defeated by Han and allied forces around 89 to 93 CE.
--- context
The Xiongnu shaped the frontier policy of the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], following the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]]. They are the first in a line of steppe powers that culminated in the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how a mobile people without cities forced a large agrarian state to pay them for peace. Wealth flowed from the settled to the mobile, and that shaped both.
--- question 1
How centralized was the Xiongnu confederation?

=== culture/zagwe-dynasty
title: Zagwe Dynasty
type: culture
date: c. 1137 – 1270
themes: religion, art
src: 2485fc9c1792
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Zagwe dynasty ruled much of the Ethiopian highlands from about 1137 to 1270. Its best-known king, Lalibela, is remembered for a set of rock-hewn churches carved from living stone.
--- fact 1
The Zagwe dynasty ruled large parts of the Ethiopian highlands from about 1137 until 1270, when the last Zagwe king, Za-Ilmaknun, was killed in battle by the forces of the Amhara ruler Yekuno Amlak.
--- fact 2
King Lalibela reigned about 1185 to 1225 and directed the carving of eleven monolithic churches from living rock at the Zagwe capital, which was later renamed for him. The churches are cut down into the rock below ground level and are a UNESCO World Heritage site.
--- fact 3
In 1270 Yekuno Amlak founded a new dynasty, often called Solomonic, which ruled until 1974. Its rulers traced their descent to the biblical King Solomon through the royal house of Aksum, and dismissed the Zagwe as usurpers, describing their own rule as a restoration.
--- context
The Zagwe followed the decline of the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] and preceded the long Solomonic line whose later rulers faced Italy at the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the dynasty that replaced the Zagwe wrote them down as usurpers and claimed an unbroken older lineage, so the account of the Zagwe survives largely through the story of their successors. The churches, by contrast, are a record the successors could not rewrite; see [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]].
--- question 1
How did the Zagwe understand their own legitimacy, given that the surviving account comes from their successors?
--- question 2
How were the Lalibela churches designed and built, and over how many years?

=== culture/zoroastrianism
title: Zoroastrianism
type: culture
date: c. 1000 BCE
themes: religion
src: 8ab415105d5b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Zoroastrianism is an Iranian religion based on the teachings of Zarathustra, centered on the god Ahura Mazda and a moral struggle between truth and falsehood.
--- fact 1
Its oldest texts, the Gathas, are hymns attributed to Zarathustra in an archaic form of Iranian.
--- fact 2
The date of Zarathustra is uncertain; scholars have proposed dates from about 1500 to about 600 BCE.
--- fact 3
Its sacred collection is the Avesta.
--- fact 4
The religion became the state religion of the Sasanian Empire (224 to 651 CE).
--- fact 5
Zoroastrian communities persist in Iran and India, where they are called Parsis.
--- context
Zoroastrianism shaped the religious world of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], and is often discussed in connection with the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]]. After the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] it became a minority faith. === event/african-humid-period
title: African Humid Period
type: event
date: c. 14.8 ka – 5500 BCE
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: 304682d079a8
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The African Humid Period, or Green Sahara, ran from about 14,800 to between 5,500 and 5,000 years ago, when the Sahara held lakes, rivers, and savanna instead of desert, driven by a stronger monsoon. Its end is debated: marine records show an abrupt collapse, archaeological records a slower, staggered desiccation.
--- fact 1
A marine sediment core off Mauritania shows a low-dust interval from about 14,800 to 5,500 years ago, read as an abrupt onset and end.
--- fact 2
Radiocarbon dating of roughly 150 sites across the eastern Sahara instead shows humid conditions beginning suddenly around 8,500 BCE, then drying out gradually, region by region, over thousands of years.
--- fact 3
A later synthesis proposed the end was locally abrupt but happened progressively later toward lower latitudes, reconciling the two pictures.
--- fact 4
Onset and end occurred at similar levels of Northern Hemisphere summer sunlight, consistent with a threshold set by the precession cycle, one of the Milankovitch cycles, amplified by vegetation and soil-moisture feedbacks.
--- fact 5
Evidence for the wetter Sahara includes rock art at Tassili n'Ajjer showing hippopotamuses and giraffes, lakeside cemeteries at Gobero, Niger, and fossil river networks visible only under satellite radar.
--- fact 6
Similar episodes recurred over at least 800,000 years. A 2025 ancient-DNA study at Takarkori, Libya, found a largely isolated North African ancestry, complicating the idea that the Green Sahara was always an open migration corridor.
--- context
The period falls within the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and continues into the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]], driven by the forcing in [[event/milankovitch-cycles-and-the-ice-ages|Milankovitch Cycles and the Ice Ages]]. Its end overlaps the spread of farming in [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that today's Sahara is not its default state but one phase of a cycle repeated for hundreds of thousands of years, read differently depending on whether the evidence comes from the sea floor or the land people once lived on. The disagreement is itself a caution against expecting one clean date for uneven desertification. The reading would weaken if further dating showed the two records were measuring the same thing after all.
--- question 1
Was the end a single threshold-crossing event or a gradual, region-by-region process?
--- question 2
Did the Green Sahara function as a migration corridor throughout its history, or only in some periods?

mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Age of Revolutions is a period of political upheaval around the Atlantic world, beginning in the 1770s and running to the revolutions of 1848. Its dates are a matter of historians' choice, and this note uses 1775 to 1848.
--- fact 1
Historians periodize the era differently. Eric Hobsbawm's *The Age of Revolution* covers 1789 to 1848; Robert Palmer's *The Age of the Democratic Revolution* starts from about 1760; some accounts begin as early as 1765.
--- fact 2
Major events: the American Revolution (to 1783), the French Revolution (1789 to 1799), the Haitian Revolution (1791 to 1804), the Spanish American wars of independence (beginning about 1808 to 1810), and the revolutions of 1848.

The Haitian Revolution began with a mass uprising of enslaved people in northern Saint-Domingue on 22 August 1791. Independence was declared on 1 January 1804, and Haiti is widely described as the first state founded by formerly enslaved people. It was the second independent country in the Americas, after the United States.
--- fact 4
Ideas of popular sovereignty, citizenship, and nationalism spread widely.
--- fact 5
Slavery and the rights of women were contested and largely excluded at first.
--- context
The revolutions overlap the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and reshape claim-lines and legitimacy; see [[observer-note/the-fiction-of-claim-lines|The Fiction of Claim-Lines]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a change in the story-glue of legitimacy: authority increasingly claimed to come from 'the people' rather than from tradition or the divine.
--- question 1
How connected were the revolutions to each other? Older histories treated them as separate episodes; recent scholarship increasingly treats them as a linked chain, and the question of causation is still argued.

src: 518efe246c14

observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Amazonian urbanism is the growing evidence that parts of the Amazon basin supported large, planned settlements linked by roads and earthworks, from about 500 BCE to the 1500s CE, and that Indigenous peoples enriched the forest soils and landscape.
--- fact 1
Aerial laser survey (lidar) has revealed settlement networks at [[place/upano-valley-settlements|Upano Valley Settlements]] in Ecuador, [[culture/casarabe-culture|Casarabe Culture]] sites in Bolivia, and [[place/xingu-garden-cities|Xingu Garden Cities]] in Brazil.

=== event/american-civil-war
title: American Civil War
type: event
date: 1861 – 1865
themes: war, kinship, technology
src: ee96cbd5bb42
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The American Civil War (1861 to 1865) was fought between the United States and eleven Southern states that seceded and formed the Confederacy. Historians overwhelmingly agree that slavery was central to secession. The war ended with the Confederate defeat, and the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery later in 1865.
--- fact 1
The war began on 12 April 1861 with the Confederate bombardment of Fort Sumter. General Robert E. Lee surrendered the main Confederate army at Appomattox on 9 April 1865; some fighting and surrenders continued into the following weeks.
--- fact 2
Eleven states formed the Confederacy: seven seceded first, and four joined after the war began.
--- fact 3
Estimates of the dead vary by method. The traditional figure is about 620,000. A 2011 census-based study by J. David Hacker put it at about 750,000, with a range of 650,000 to 850,000. A 2024 study using full-count census records estimated about 698,000 deaths, with a bounding interval of roughly 50,000 either way. It found a much higher death rate in the Confederate states than in the Union: about 13 percent of military-age native-born white men in the core Confederate states against about 5 percent in the core Union states, although the Union lost more men in absolute numbers (more than 400,000 against about 300,000). These are estimates of excess mortality, and no side kept standardized records.
--- fact 4
The Emancipation Proclamation took effect on 1 January 1863. It was a wartime measure that freed people held in states in rebellion. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified on 6 December 1865, abolished slavery throughout the United States.
--- fact 5
Railways, telegraphs, and industrial production shaped the conduct of the war.
--- fact 6
It was the deadliest conflict in American history.
--- context
The war is central to the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]] and shows the effects of the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] on warfare. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a modern state fought its own citizens over the ownership of other human beings. The war also displays a technology-driven scale of killing that presaged [[event/world-war-i|World War I]].
--- question 1
How reliable are the casualty estimates? The figures range from about 620,000 to 750,000 or more depending on method, and civilian deaths are counted inconsistently.

The Assembly of Pangaea was the joining of nearly all of Earth's continents into one supercontinent, mostly in the Carboniferous, about 335 to 300 million years ago. It stayed together until it began to split about 200 million years ago.

The evidence includes matching mountain belts, rock types and fossils across ocean basins, such as the plant Glossopteris and the reptile Lystrosaurus, and magnetic data from ancient rocks.
--- fact 4
The supercontinent had a vast, dry, strongly seasonal interior and less shallow-sea shelf area. The latter is one proposed contributor to the marine losses of the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]].
--- fact 5
Its break-up is described in [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]].
--- context
The Assembly of Pangaea runs through the [[era/carboniferous-period|Carboniferous Period]] and into the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]], and it precedes the [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]] in the Mesozoic. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the case for a single landmass was assembled from fossils and rock types decades before a mechanism was known, and was rejected for decades because it lacked one. It reads this as an example of how a well-supported pattern can wait for its explanation. The reading would weaken if the pattern had been dropped once the mechanism was studied.

The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a planet's early maps are reconstructed as much as observed: Rodinia is known from mountain roots and magnetic minerals, not from coastlines. The Observer holds the detailed arrangement loosely and treats the broad claim, that the continents joined and split repeatedly, as firmer. The reading would weaken if the magnetic data proved too sparse to distinguish the competing layouts.
