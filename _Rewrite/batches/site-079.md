=== place/goree-island
title: Gorée Island
type: place
date: c. 1444
themes: trade, kinship, war
src: 53ca11d8501d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Gorée Island is a small island off Dakar, Senegal, held in turn by the Portuguese, Dutch, British and French from the 15th century, and a symbol of the Atlantic slave trade, though historians debate how many enslaved people left from it.
--- fact 1
Gorée Island lies about 2 kilometers off Dakar; the Portuguese landed in 1444, the Dutch held the island by 1628 (exactly when and how they replaced the Portuguese is unknown, since the early Dutch West India Company archive was largely lost), and the French captured it from the Dutch in 1677, after which control passed between Britain and France several times over the following century.
--- fact 2
Gorée was a trading post for goods such as gum and hides and for enslaved people; a community of Afro-European merchant women, called signares, ran trade and property on the island, and some owned enslaved people.
--- fact 3
The House of Slaves, built by Nicolas Pépin at a date given variously as about 1776 or 1780 to 1784, and having a doorway to the sea called the "Door of No Return", is a pilgrimage site for visitors.
--- fact 4
Many historians argue that Gorée was a modest embarkation point compared with larger ports on the Atlantic coast and that the House of Slaves did not hold the numbers often claimed, while others stress its symbolic importance; the debate is unresolved.
--- fact 5
African merchants and rulers took part in the trade alongside European ones.
--- fact 6
Visitors to Gorée have reportedly included Nelson Mandela and Pope John Paul II, and, most firmly documented, US President Barack Obama in 2013.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Island of Gorée in 1978.
--- context
Gorée Island was a small port within the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]], which ended in its legal form with the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] (its first European contact) and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Gorée is a case where a place's symbolic weight and its documented role differ: as a memorial it commemorates the whole of the Atlantic slave trade, while the records suggest that a smaller share of captives left from it than from larger ports. Both can be held at once, since a memorial's function as a grief-rite does not depend on the count, and the Observer records the suffering of the enslaved without ranking sites by numbers. The signares show a kin-lattice and property system in which African women held power within the trade, a reminder that the trade was embedded in local societies. The reading would weaken if new archival counts show that Gorée handled a much larger share of the trade than the current estimates suggest.
--- question 1
How many enslaved people left from Gorée, and in what years?
--- question 2
How should memorial sites present both their documented role and their symbolic one?

=== place/harappa
title: Harappa
type: place
date: c. 3300 – 1900 BCE
themes: trade, technology, language
src: 225705f7bdc2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Harappa is an archaeological site in Punjab, Pakistan, on a dry bed of the Ravi River, occupied from about 3300 BCE and a major city of the Indus Valley Civilization until its urban phase declined after about 1900 BCE.
--- fact 1
Harappa was occupied from about 3300 BCE, and its urban "Mature Harappan" phase ran from about 2600 to 1900 BCE, with population estimates ranging from as many as 23,500 in site-specific studies to a wider 30,000 to 60,000 suggested for the largest Indus cities generally.
--- fact 2
Harappa had planned streets, brick drains and standardized weights, and its bricks followed a common size ratio used at other Indus sites.
--- fact 3
Harappa's seals, carved with animals and signs in a script that has not been deciphered, are found in many Indus cities, and Indus objects such as seals and carnelian beads have been found in Mesopotamian cities.
--- fact 4
Daya Ram Sahni began excavations at Harappa in 1921, after British engineers had taken bricks from the site in the 1850s for railway ballast.
--- fact 5
The city's urban phase declined after about 1900 BCE, and occupation continued at a smaller scale; proposed causes include changes in monsoon rainfall and river courses, and the causes are debated.
--- context
Harappa gave its name to the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], whose other great city was [[place/mohenjo-daro|Mohenjo-daro]], and its planned streets belong to the story of [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Harappa left thousands of inscribed seals that cannot yet be read, so its people's own account of themselves is missing, and what is known of its order comes from bricks, weights and drains that suggest a shared administrative logic. The lack of obvious palaces or royal tombs has led some scholars to propose that its rule differed from that of contemporary Mesopotamia and Egypt, though the proposal is debated. The reading would weaken if the script is deciphered and shows a more conventional royal order.
--- question 1
What do the signs on the Harappan seals say?
--- question 2
Why did the urban phase of Harappa decline after about 1900 BCE?

=== place/hispaniola
title: Hispaniola
type: place
date: c. 4000 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade, war
src: 402f2b477a9e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hispaniola is the second-largest island of the Caribbean, in the Greater Antilles, divided between Haiti in the west and the Dominican Republic in the east, and the site of the first lasting European settlement in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Hispaniola covers about 76,000 square kilometers; its features include Pico Duarte, reported at about 3,101 meters, the highest peak in the Caribbean, and Lake Enriquillo, a saline lake whose depth below sea level is not firmly established by the sources consulted.
--- fact 2
The earliest evidence of human presence on Hispaniola is dated by many archaeologists to about 4000 BCE, though estimates vary; by 1492 the island was home to Taíno societies organized into several chiefdoms, and Spanish chroniclers report the names Ayiti, Quisqueya and Bohío for it.
--- fact 3
Columbus reached Hispaniola in December 1492, and the Spanish founded La Navidad (destroyed in 1493), La Isabela (1493) and Santo Domingo (1498).
--- fact 4
The Taíno population of Hispaniola declined sharply after 1492 under forced labor, violence and disease.
--- fact 5
In 1697 Spain ceded the western third of Hispaniola to France, which developed the sugar and slave colony of Saint-Domingue; the Haitian Revolution (1791 to 1804) ended slavery and French rule there.
--- fact 6
The Dominican Republic became independent in 1844, after Haitian rule over the whole island from 1822.
--- context
Hispaniola links the [[culture/taino|Taino]], the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], the city of [[place/santo-domingo|Santo Domingo]], the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (its earliest human presence) and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one island now holds two states, two languages and two colonial histories because a treaty between distant European powers drew a claim-line through it in 1697, with no reference to anything on the island. Hispaniola compresses the Atlantic system into one place, with an indigenous society, a first European base, a plantation colony built on enslaved labor and a successful revolution of enslaved people that founded a state, all within about three centuries. The pattern is not unique, since other Caribbean islands show parts of it, and a specialist could weaken the reading by showing that Hispaniola was more exceptional or more ordinary than it suggests.
--- question 1
When did people first reach Hispaniola, and from where?
--- question 2
How large was the Taíno population of the island in 1492?

=== place/issyk-kul
title: Issyk-Kul
type: place
date: c. 800 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade, science
src: 27e6ed4723b4
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Issyk-Kul is a large mountain lake in the northern Tian Shan of Kyrgyzstan, at about 1,600 meters above sea level, on the routes between the steppe and the oases and near cemeteries whose graves have been linked to an early stage of the Black Death.
--- fact 1
Issyk-Kul has an area of about 6,200 square kilometers, a maximum depth of about 668 meters and no outlet; the lake is slightly saline and rarely freezes despite its elevation and cold winters, and its name is usually rendered "warm lake" in Kyrgyz, though the literal meaning is closer to "hot lake."
--- fact 2
Saka burial mounds around the lake date from the 1st millennium BCE; the Saka were Iranian-speaking pastoralists related to the Scythians.
--- fact 3
Underwater surveys have reported settlement remains and objects from the shallows, dated variously from the Bronze Age to the Middle Ages; interpretations vary.
--- fact 4
Routes across the Tian Shan connecting the steppe with the oases of Central Asia passed near the lake.
--- fact 5
Gravestones in cemeteries at Kara-Djigach and Burana, in the region of Issyk-Kul, record deaths from pestilence in 1338 to 1339.
--- fact 6
A 2022 study reported Yersinia pestis DNA from graves at Kara-Djigach and concluded that the strain was close to the ancestor of the lineage that caused the Black Death; some researchers question whether this locates the origin of the pandemic.
--- context
Issyk-Kul was used by Saka groups related to the [[culture/scythians|Scythians]] and lay near routes of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. Its region is one candidate for the beginning of the [[event/black-death|Black Death]]. It sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a community's grief-rites left the evidence that later science used: gravestones that named the cause of death, read six centuries afterward through ancient DNA, an unplanned act of memory-outsourcing that dated the disease's path. A warm, ice-free mountain lake on routes between pastoral and farming societies drew successive peoples, and the same routes carried the pathogen. The origin of the Black Death is still debated, and the reading would weaken if later studies place the pandemic's origin elsewhere.
--- question 1
Did the Black Death begin near Issyk-Kul, or did a plague lineage already circulate more widely?
--- question 2
What do the submerged sites on the lake bed show about earlier settlement?

=== place/jerusalem
title: Jerusalem
type: place
date: c. 3000 BCE
themes: religion, war
src: de6990d534bf
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jerusalem is a city in the Judean hills of the southern Levant, settled since about the 4th millennium BCE and holy to Jews, Christians and Muslims, whose political status is disputed today.
--- fact 1
Settlement on the spur above the Gihon spring dates from about the 4th millennium BCE, and Egyptian Execration Texts of the 19th century BCE and the Amarna letters of the 14th century BCE may contain the earliest mentions of the city, though this is debated.
--- fact 2
According to the Hebrew Bible, King David captured the city about 1000 BCE and his son Solomon built the First Temple; archaeologists debate the size of Jerusalem in the 10th century BCE, and the city grew markedly by the 8th century BCE.
--- fact 3
Babylonian forces destroyed Jerusalem and the First Temple in 586 BCE and deported part of its population; the Second Temple was dedicated in 516 BCE.
--- fact 4
Roman forces destroyed the city and the Second Temple in 70 CE; after the Bar Kokhba revolt (132 to 135 or 136 CE, sources vary on the end year) the Romans refounded it as Aelia Capitolina and barred Jews from entering.
--- fact 5
The Church of the Holy Sepulchre was built in the 4th century under Constantine; Muslim forces took the city in 638 (some sources give 637), and the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik completed the Dome of the Rock in 691 to 692.
--- fact 6
Crusaders captured Jerusalem in 1099 and killed many of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, with estimates of the dead varying widely; Saladin retook it in 1187, and it was under Ottoman rule from 1517 to 1917.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Old City of Jerusalem and its walls in 1981. The city was divided between Israel and Jordan in 1948 and has been under Israeli control since 1967, and its status remains disputed between Israelis and Palestinians.
--- context
Jerusalem's history is tied to [[place/babylon|Babylon]] through the destruction of 586 BCE, and to the [[culture/romans|Romans]], the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], the [[event/crusades|Crusades]] and the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] through later conquests. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] (its earliest settlement) and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Jerusalem is a place where three meaning-engines, Jewish, Christian and Muslim, locate some of their most significant sites within a few hundred meters of one another, so one spot can hold the claim-lines of several communities, and builders of each period have reused or replaced earlier structures. The city's long record of sieges, destructions and massacres is recorded here as suffering, whichever community was its victim at the time. The reading would weaken if the sanctity of particular sites proves to have been less continuous over time than each tradition holds.
--- question 1
How large was Jerusalem in the 10th century BCE?
--- question 2
How did the population of Jerusalem change under successive rulers?

=== place/kilwa-kisiwani
title: Kilwa Kisiwani
type: place
date: c. 800 CE
themes: trade, religion, art
src: a97cd5b7ac32
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kilwa Kisiwani is an island town off the coast of southern Tanzania that was a leading Swahili port from about the 12th to the 15th century, trading gold from the interior for goods carried across the Indian Ocean.
--- fact 1
Kilwa Kisiwani was occupied from about the 9th century CE, and its Great Mosque has an earliest section dated to between 1131 and 1170 (12th century, though local oral tradition places the founding earlier), enlarged in the early 14th century.
--- fact 2
The Kilwa Chronicle, written in the 16th century, traces the ruling dynasty to a founder from Shiraz in Persia; archaeological and linguistic evidence indicates that Kilwa's society was rooted in African coastal communities that took part in Indian Ocean trade.
--- fact 3
From about the 13th century Kilwa controlled the gold trade from Sofala, in the south, whose gold came from the interior of Zimbabwe, and the town minted its own coins, among the earliest in sub-Saharan Africa.
--- fact 4
Sultan al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman built the palace and market complex of Husuni Kubwa in the early 14th century.
--- fact 5
The traveler Ibn Battuta visited Kilwa in about 1331 and described it as one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns.
--- fact 6
Portuguese forces attacked Kilwa in 1505 and built a fort; the town later came under Omani influence and was involved in the trade in enslaved people in the 18th and 19th centuries.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara in 1981.
--- context
Kilwa Kisiwani was one of the [[culture/swahili-coast-city-states|Swahili Coast City-States]] and a hub of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]]; its gold came through the trade that also supplied [[place/great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]]'s region, and the [[event/portuguese-sea-route-to-india|Portuguese Sea Route to India]] led to its attack in 1505. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kilwa's wealth came from standing between two exchange-webs, the gold of the interior and the ships of the Indian Ocean: the town made none of its main goods but profited from the position between them. The founding story of a Persian origin may have served as a status-signal within a Muslim trading world, while the archaeological evidence shows local African roots, so the story and the record point in different directions. The reading would weaken if excavation shows that Kilwa's wealth depended more on local production than on trade in transit.
--- question 1
How much did gold from Sofala contribute to Kilwa's wealth, and how did that trade decline?
--- question 2
How large was Kilwa Kisiwani's population at its height?

=== place/kolyma
title: Kolyma
type: place
date: c. 1932 – 1957
themes: war, trade
src: 6caecfc53fb5
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kolyma is a remote region of northeastern Siberia, Russia, that from 1932 until the 1950s held the Soviet Union's largest system of forced-labor camps, where prisoners mined gold and other metals in extreme cold and many died.
--- fact 1
Kolyma lies along the Kolyma River in the far northeast of Siberia, where average winter temperatures run from about minus 19 to minus 38 degrees Celsius, colder still in the interior.
--- fact 2
Gold was found on the Kolyma River in the early 20th century, and in 1931 the Soviet state created Dalstroy, a state trust that developed the region with prisoners from the Gulag system, the first of whom arrived in 1932.
--- fact 3
Prisoners included people convicted of ordinary crimes and of political offenses, and many were sent during the Great Terror of 1937 to 1938; secret-police records cited by historians put about two million people shipped to the Kolyma camps between 1930 and 1955, with a recorded peak camp population of about 200,000 in 1952.
--- fact 4
Estimates of deaths in the Kolyma camps range from about 250,000 to over a million, from hunger, cold, disease, overwork and executions; the full toll is uncertain.
--- fact 5
Prisoners built the Kolyma Highway between Magadan and Yakutsk, known as the "Road of Bones" because of the deaths among its builders.
--- fact 6
Varlam Shalamov, who was imprisoned in the Kolyma camps, wrote the short stories known as the Kolyma Tales about them.
--- fact 7
Dalstroy was dissolved in 1957, and a memorial, the Mask of Sorrow, was unveiled in Magadan in 1996.
--- context
Kolyma was a major site of the Gulag under the Soviet state that emerged from the [[event/russian-revolution|Russian Revolution]], in a region that Russia had entered through the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]]. Much of what is known about the camps comes from survivors' writing and from archives opened after the [[event/collapse-of-the-soviet-union|Collapse of the Soviet Union]]. It sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kolyma is a case of sanctioned harm carried out through economic administration: prisoners were counted as labor for gold production, and their deaths were entered in ledgers, which the Observer records as suffering. The record of the place comes from two sources that do not always agree, survivors' writing and, after 1991, opened archives, which is why the death toll is given here as a range. The reading would weaken if fuller archival access shows that the camps served aims other than production more than this account suggests.
--- question 1
How many people died in the Kolyma camps, and how many survived?
--- question 2
How is Kolyma remembered inside Russia today?

=== place/lake-baikal
title: Lake Baikal
type: place
date: c. 5000 BCE
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 6c495fd7fe25
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Lake Baikal is a rift lake in southern Siberia, Russia, the deepest and oldest lake on Earth and the largest by volume of fresh water, with people living around it for many thousands of years and a UNESCO listing since 1996.
--- fact 1
Lake Baikal lies in a rift valley, is about 636 kilometers long, has a maximum depth of 1,642 meters, and holds about 20 percent of the world's unfrozen surface fresh water.
--- fact 2
The Baikal rift basin is estimated to be 25 to 30 million years old, which makes the lake the oldest on Earth; this geologic age is far older than any human presence.
--- fact 3
More than 1,000 species of plants and 2,500 species of animals have been recorded in and around Lake Baikal, with the true totals thought to be higher; many are found nowhere else, including the Baikal seal, a freshwater seal.
--- fact 4
Hunter-fisher-gatherers of the Kitoi culture buried their dead around the lake from about the 5th millennium BCE, and much older Paleolithic sites, such as Mal'ta, lie nearby.
--- fact 5
Russian explorers reached Lake Baikal in the 1640s, beginning Russian settlement among the Buryat and Evenki peoples.
--- fact 6
A pulp and paper mill on the southern shore discharged waste into the lake from 1966, with production interrupted between 2008 and 2010 before a final closure in 2013, and it was one of the earliest targets of organized environmental protest in the Soviet Union.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Lake Baikal in 1996.
--- context
Lake Baikal lies near the Paleolithic site linked to [[culture/mal-ta-and-the-ancient-north-eurasians|Mal'ta and the Ancient North Eurasians]], and its shores were reached by the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] in the 17th century. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (its earliest Holocene burials) and the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Lake Baikal is valued both as a store of an unusual biota and as a resource, that both valuations have shaped policy and the state of the lake, and that the balance between them has shifted with the political era. A lake that took millions of years to form and holds a fifth of the planet's unfrozen fresh water could be altered by one industrial plant within a few decades, which shows the mismatch between geological and human timescales. The reading would weaken if measurements show that the mill's effect on the lake as a whole was small compared with natural variation.
--- question 1
How much lasting change did the pulp mill cause in the lake's ecosystem?
--- question 2
How did people live around Lake Baikal in the Neolithic, and how did they treat their dead?

=== place/london
title: London
type: place
date: c. 47 CE
themes: trade, science, war
src: 118936664ac6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
London is the capital of the United Kingdom, on the Thames River, founded as the Roman town of Londinium in about 47 CE and grown into a leading center of trade, finance and empire and, in the 19th century, the largest city in the world.
--- fact 1
Londinium was founded by the Romans in about 47 CE near a crossing of the Thames; Boudica's revolt destroyed it in 60 or 61, and it grew into the largest town in Roman Britain, though clear evidence that it was ever formally the province's capital is lacking, until Roman rule ended around 410.
--- fact 2
William I was crowned at Westminster in 1066 and began the Tower of London in about 1078.
--- fact 3
The Black Death of 1348 to 1349 killed a large share of London's people, and the Great Plague of 1665 killed tens of thousands; the Great Fire of 1666 destroyed about 13,000 houses and 87 parish churches.
--- fact 4
London was probably the largest city in the world from the 1830s to the 1920s, and the world's first underground railway opened there in 1863.
--- fact 5
During the 1854 cholera outbreak in Soho, John Snow mapped deaths by address and linked them to a water pump; the "Great Stink" of 1858 led to the building of a sewer system by Joseph Bazalgette.
--- fact 6
London was a major center of shipping, insurance and finance for the Atlantic slave trade and for British colonial trade, and the capital of the British Empire.
--- fact 7
The German air raids of 1940 to 1941 killed about 20,000 Londoners or more.
--- context
London began as a town of the [[culture/romans|Romans]], lost people to the [[event/black-death|Black Death]], grew with the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], served as the imperial center of the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]] and other territories, and was bombed in [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that London was founded as a bridge town for a foreign state's administration and grew into a hub of an exchange-web that spanned oceans, and that its wealth included profits from the sale and transport of enslaved people, which the Observer records as suffering. The Snow map of 1854 shows a city becoming an object of pattern-taming: tabulating deaths by address led to the identification of a source of disease, a method later used across public health. The reading would weaken if the record shows that London's global role rested more on later trade than on the earlier ones it emphasizes.
--- question 1
How large was Roman Londinium, and why was it abandoned in the 5th century?
--- question 2
How much of London's wealth in the 17th and 18th centuries came from the Atlantic slave trade and related finance?

=== place/mekong-river
title: Mekong River
type: place
date: c. 210 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade, technology
src: 46db8f69dcc7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mekong River is a river of Asia with an estimated length of about 4,900 kilometers, rising on the Tibetan Plateau and flowing through China, Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia and Vietnam, whose annual flood supports rice farming and one of the world's largest inland fisheries.
--- fact 1
The Mekong rises on the Tibetan Plateau and flows through China, along the borders of Myanmar, Laos and Thailand, and through Cambodia and Vietnam into the South China Sea, where it forms a large delta.
--- fact 2
Settlement along the Mekong is documented from at least 210 BCE, and the early port of Oc Eo in the delta, part of the state called Funan, traded from about the 1st century CE; the region's much earlier prehistory is not well fixed by the sources consulted here.
--- fact 3
The Tonlé Sap, a lake in Cambodia joined to the Mekong, changes its direction of flow with the season: floodwater flows into it in the wet season and back out in the dry season, supporting a major fishery.
--- fact 4
The lower basin has about 60 million people, and the river is among the world's most productive inland fisheries.
--- fact 5
A French exploration commission in 1866 to 1868 found that rapids made the Mekong unnavigable as a route to China, and the Mekong Delta was a major area of fighting in the Vietnam War.
--- fact 6
China has built a cascade of dams on the upper Mekong since the 1990s, and Laos began operating the Xayaburi dam on the lower river in 2019; the Mekong River Commission, formed in 1995, coordinates the lower-basin countries, and the effects of the dams on fish, sediment and flood timing are debated.
--- context
The Mekong River was the corridor of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] and its capital [[place/angkor|Angkor]], and its delta was a theater of the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] (its earliest documented settlement) and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Mekong is a river whose annual pulse feeds fish and rice for tens of millions, and that dams upstream change that pulse for people downstream, a relation in which those who control the flow can alter conditions for others across claim-lines that the river itself ignores. The reversing flow of the Tonlé Sap shows a natural rhythm that a whole society organized its seasons around. The reading would weaken if measurements show that the dams' net effect on downstream fish and sediment is smaller than the models suggest.
--- question 1
How much have upstream dams changed the flood pulse, fish migration and delta sediment?
--- question 2
How did early states such as Funan use the river for trade?

=== place/merv
title: Merv
type: place
date: c. 500 BCE – 1221
themes: trade, war, science
src: df3061c0573a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Merv was a major oasis city on the Silk Roads in what is now Turkmenistan, a city of the Achaemenid Persians, a capital of the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun and of the Seljuk sultans, and sacked by the Mongols in 1221.
--- fact 1
Merv lies in the Murghab delta oasis near the modern city of Mary; Bronze Age towns of the region, such as Gonur Depe, date to about 2000 BCE, and the walled city of Erk Kala dates from the Achaemenid period, from about the 7th century BCE.
--- fact 2
Arab forces took Merv in 651, after the last Sasanian king, Yazdegerd III, was killed near it.
--- fact 3
Abu Muslim launched the Abbasid Revolution from Merv in 747 and declared the new Abbasid dynasty there in February 748; the caliph al-Ma'mun ruled from Merv from about 813 to 818 before moving to Baghdad.
--- fact 4
Merv was a Seljuk capital, notably under Sultan Sanjar (reigned 1118 to 1157), whose mausoleum survives, and some estimates suggest it was among the largest cities in the world in the 12th century.
--- fact 5
The geographer Yaqut, who studied in Merv's libraries in the early 13th century, described them.
--- fact 6
The Mongol army took Merv in 1221; Persian chronicles report hundreds of thousands to over a million killed, figures that modern historians regard as greatly exaggerated but that reflect a severe massacre and destruction.
--- fact 7
Merv was rebuilt on a smaller scale in the 15th century and was finally abandoned in the late 18th century.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed Ancient Merv in 1999.
--- context
Merv was a satrapy center of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], the political base of the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] at its start, and a victim of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]]. It lay on the routes of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. Merv sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Merv's fortunes followed the claim-lines drawn by others: an irrigated oasis city depends on which empire's exchange-web runs through it, and Achaemenids, Seleucids, Arabs, Seljuks and Mongols each held it. The events of 1221 show that a city, unlike a mobile camp, cannot leave, and the reported loss of its libraries suggests that a city's stored writing is as exposed as its people, though those reports are hard to verify. The reading would weaken if the true death toll and the extent of the library losses prove much smaller than the chronicles say.

--- question 2

=== place/mogao-caves
title: Mogao Caves
type: place
date: c. 366 CE
themes: religion, art, language
src: e2743ea6a53a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Mogao Caves are a complex of Buddhist cave temples cut into a cliff near Dunhuang, in Gansu, China, begun by tradition in 366 CE and decorated with murals and sculptures over about a thousand years at a crossroads of the Silk Roads.
--- fact 1
The Mogao Caves lie near the oasis of Dunhuang at the edge of the desert, where Silk Road routes met; Han China had established a commandery at Dunhuang in the late 2nd century BCE.
--- fact 2
By tradition the first cave was dug in 366 CE by the monk Yuezun, and work continued into the 14th century; the site has roughly 735 caves (487 known since the early 1900s plus 248 more found between 1988 and 1995), several hundred of which hold murals or painted sculptures, with murals covering about 46,000 square meters.
--- fact 3
The art shows Indian, Central Asian and Chinese influences, and donors, including officials, merchants and local rulers, are painted into many caves.
--- fact 4
Dunhuang was under Tibetan rule from about 787 to 848, was governed by a local regime from 848, and passed to the Tangut state of Western Xia in about 1036.
--- fact 5
The "Library Cave" (Cave 17), sealed around the early 11th century, was opened in 1900 by Wang Yuanlu, a Daoist priest who guarded the caves; it held tens of thousands of manuscripts in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Sogdian, Khotanese and other languages, including a printed Diamond Sutra dated 868.
--- fact 6
Aurel Stein in 1907, Paul Pelliot in 1908 and others obtained large numbers of these manuscripts, now held in London, Paris and elsewhere; the removals are described in China as theft and by the acquiring institutions as purchases.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Mogao Caves in 1987, and the Dunhuang Academy has conserved them since 1944.
--- context
The Mogao Caves grew with the trade of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], and held documents written by [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] and others. They sit in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the record of a caravan crossroads survived because a room was sealed: contracts, letters, school exercises and Buddhist texts in many scripts, kept by dry air, a rare case of memory-outsourcing that preserved ordinary life and not only palace records. Donors painted into the murals beside sacred figures joined status-signal and devotion in one image. The scattering of the manuscripts across several countries shows how the record of one place can become the property of many claim-lines. The reason the room was sealed is debated, and the reading would weaken if it proves to have been routine disposal of worn-out texts and not a deliberate act of preservation.
--- question 1
Why was the Library Cave sealed, and by whom?
--- question 2
How should the manuscripts taken from the Mogao Caves be held and shared today?

=== place/newgrange
title: Newgrange
type: place
date: c. 3200 BCE
themes: religion, art, science
src: 93d0306107de
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Newgrange is a large passage tomb in the Boyne Valley of County Meath, Ireland, built about 3200 to 3100 BCE by Neolithic farmers and aligned so that the winter solstice sunrise lights its inner chamber.
--- fact 1
Newgrange is a mound about 12 meters high and about 85 meters across at its widest, with a passage about 19 meters long leading to a cross-shaped chamber, and radiocarbon dates place its construction at about 3200 to 3100 BCE, older than the Great Pyramid of Giza.
--- fact 2
A "roof box" above the entrance admits sunlight along the passage for about 17 minutes around sunrise at the winter solstice.
--- fact 3
Kerbstones around the base carry carved designs, including spirals, and cremated remains of a small number of people were found in the chamber.
--- fact 4
The white quartz wall at the front was reconstructed in the 1970s, and whether it originally looked like that is debated.
--- fact 5
Newgrange was used again in later periods, and Roman coins and other offerings have been found there; later Irish tradition associated the Boyne complex with legendary figures.
--- fact 6
Newgrange lies with the nearby passage tombs at Knowth and Dowth, and UNESCO inscribed the Brú na Bóinne complex in 1993.
--- context
Newgrange belongs to the farming societies that followed [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in Europe, and it stands in the same broad tradition of monuments as [[place/stonehenge|Stonehenge]]; it predates the [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Newgrange has a chamber built so that sunrise light at the winter solstice enters for a short time, which suggests careful, sustained observation of the sky by a farming society that had no mark-code to record what the alignment meant. The meaning is inferred: a calendar, a grief-rite for the dead, or both, and the evidence supports the alignment more strongly than any one explanation of its purpose. The reading would weaken if the roof box is shown to be a later addition and not part of the original design.
--- question 1
What did the winter solstice alignment mean to the builders?
--- question 2
How many people built Newgrange, and how long did it take?

=== place/nile-river
title: Nile River
type: place
date: c. 5500 BCE
themes: earth-systems, trade, technology
src: 2af52a055700
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Nile River is a river of northeastern Africa, commonly cited as about 6,650 kilometers long though estimates vary by measurement method, flowing north to the Mediterranean, whose annual flood supported farming and the states of Egypt and Nubia from about the 6th millennium BCE.
--- fact 1
The Nile flows north from the highlands of east and central Africa to the Mediterranean; its two main branches, the White Nile from the Lake Victoria region and the Blue Nile from Lake Tana in Ethiopia, meet at Khartoum, and the Ethiopian highlands supply most of the seasonal flood.
--- fact 2
Farming communities are documented in the Nile Valley and the Fayum from about the 6th millennium BCE, and the summer flood deposited silt that kept the valley fertile.
--- fact 3
Egyptian officials measured the flood on nilometers, and in later periods taxation was tied to its height.
--- fact 4
The states of Egypt, after the Unification of Egypt, and of Kush, upstream in Nubia, grew along the river.
--- fact 5
The Aswan High Dam, completed in 1970, ended the annual flood in Egypt and created Lake Nasser; about 100,000 Nubians were relocated, and monuments such as Abu Simbel were moved.
--- fact 6
Eleven countries share the Nile basin. A 1959 agreement between Egypt and Sudan allocated most of the river's measured flow to those two states, and Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, begun in 2011 and filled in stages from 2020, is a subject of dispute over water with Egypt and Sudan.
--- context
The Nile River is the axis of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] and the state formed in the [[event/unification-of-egypt|Unification of Egypt]], and it linked Egypt with the upstream [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] (its earliest farming communities) and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Nile turned a predictable flood into a state: a river whose height could be measured, forecast and taxed rewarded record-keeping, an early case of pattern-taming tied to administration. The river's modern history shows that claim-lines drawn across water are harder to keep than lines on land, since a 1959 allocation between two states now faces the claims of upstream countries. The reading would weaken if the flood proves to have been too variable to forecast well enough to shape administration.
--- question 1
How much did variation in the Nile flood affect Egyptian politics and famine?
--- question 2
How will the water of the Nile be shared among the basin countries?
