=== technology/eyeglasses
title: Eyeglasses
type: technology
date: c. 1286
themes: technology, science
src: 7583e2b67177
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Eyeglasses are corrective lenses mounted in a frame worn over the eyes. They first appear in northern Italy in the late 13th century and spread across Europe within decades, extending reading and close work for people whose eyesight weakened with age.
--- fact 1
Framed convex lenses correcting farsightedness appear in the Pisa-Florence area of northern Italy in the late 13th century, commonly dated about 1286-1290.
--- fact 2
The earliest firm evidence is a 23 February 1306 Florence sermon by the friar Giordano da Pisa, calling eyeglasses less than twenty years old and citing an unnamed maker.
--- fact 3
No inventor is confirmed; a claim naming "Salvino D'Armati" traces only to an unsupported 1684 book, debunked in 1920.
--- fact 4
Historians debate whether the Pisan friar Alessandro della Spina, who died in 1313 and is credited with independently learning and spreading the technique, was an inventor or an early popularizer.
--- fact 5
Early lenses were convex, for farsightedness; concave lenses for nearsightedness are not documented until the mid-15th century.
--- context
Eyeglasses emerged in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], out of Italian craft glassmaking, spreading with growing literacy among clergy, merchants, and scholars, into the era of the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]]. As a device built from glass-working skill, it sits at the boundary of [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]], appearing centuries before optical theory explained why curved glass corrects vision.
--- observer
The Observer reads eyeglasses as tool-lineage plainly at work: a working solution assembled from inherited glass-craft skill, centuries before any pattern-taming account of why curved glass bends light. The reading weakens if the 1306 sermon describes something already established rather than a recent novelty. An alternative reading treats the invention as near-simultaneous diffusion among several Italian glassworking towns rather than one breakthrough.
--- question 1
Who first made the earliest pair, and did Alessandro della Spina invent the technique or only spread it?

=== technology/gutenberg-bible
title: Gutenberg Bible
type: technology
date: c. 1455
themes: language, religion, art
src: 2d04f1101a9a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Gutenberg Bible is a Latin Vulgate Bible printed by [[person/johannes-gutenberg|Johannes Gutenberg]] in Mainz around 1455 using his movable metal-type press. It is regarded as the first substantial European book printed with movable type, most copies set in 42 lines per column.
--- fact 1
Printed by Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz, Germany, using the movable metal-type press he developed.
--- fact 2
Most surviving copies are set in 42 lines per column, giving the book its common name; a few use fewer lines.
--- fact 3
A March 1455 letter by the future Pope Pius II describes printed quires of the Bible seen at the Frankfurt fair in October 1454, the earliest known reference to European printing.
--- fact 4
Exact completion is usually placed at about 1454-1455; scholars debate the precise finishing date.
--- fact 5
Estimates of the original print run range from about 160 to 185 copies, often cited as roughly 180, on paper and vellum.
--- fact 6
About 49 copies survive today, complete or in part, with roughly 21 considered complete; exact counts vary by source.
--- context
It was made with Gutenberg's [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]], printed mostly on [[technology/paper|Paper]] with some vellum copies, in [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] [[region/europe|Europe]]. Its Latin Vulgate text ties it to [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]]; its hand-added decoration connects it to [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] manuscript traditions.
--- observer
The Observer reads the Bible as a step in the tool-lineage: a mechanical way to multiply mark-code that once needed a scribe's hand per copy. It carried a meaning-engine's central text, letting existing story-glue reach more households without matching copying labor. The reading would weaken if the object were mainly a luxury commission rather than a mass-production prototype, or if earlier movable-type printing in East Asia were treated as more decisive.
--- question 1
Was completion in 1454 or 1455?
--- question 2
What are the precise print-run and surviving-copy counts?

=== technology/haber-bosch-process
title: Haber-Bosch Process
type: technology
date: 1909
themes: technology, earth-systems
src: a934d3af564f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Haber-Bosch process combines atmospheric nitrogen and hydrogen under high pressure over a catalyst to make ammonia. Developed in Germany between 1908 and 1913, it underpins synthetic nitrogen fertilizer.
--- fact 1
Fritz Haber patented ammonia synthesis in October 1908 and demonstrated a working laboratory apparatus at Karlsruhe in 1909.
--- fact 2
Carl Bosch and BASF engineers scaled it up; the first industrial plant, at Oppau, began operating in 1913.
--- fact 3
Haber received the Chemistry Nobel for 1918 (awarded 1919); Bosch shared the 1931 prize with Friedrich Bergius.
--- fact 4
The Allied blockade cut off Chilean nitrate in World War I; synthetic ammonia supplied nitrogen for German explosives.
--- fact 5
Haber organized the first large-scale German chlorine gas release, at Ypres on 22 April 1915.
--- fact 6
Estimates of humanity fed by synthetic nitrogen range from about one third to one half, depending on assumptions.
--- context
Synthetic nitrogen supported the fertilizer growth of the [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]] and the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]], and altered the nitrogen cycle at planetary scale. Its explosives role ties it to [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. It arose in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
Here the species turned air into a tool-lineage input: one reaction supplies the Settling's harvests and also sanctioned harm. Read this way, part of the species' numbers now rests on a factory chain. The reading weakens if dependence estimates prove inflated by diet assumptions, or if manure and legumes could have sufficed. An alternative view stresses contingency: rival fixation routes existed, so a single-inventor story is too neat.
--- question 1
How much of the fertilizer-linked population gain would have occurred through other routes?
--- question 2
How quickly can low-carbon ammonia replace fossil-derived hydrogen?

=== technology/insulin
title: Insulin
type: technology
date: 1921
themes: science
src: 5a1a071147de
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Insulin is a pancreatic hormone that regulates blood sugar. Before a Toronto team purified it in 1921 and 1922, type 1 diabetes was fatal, often within a year or two of diagnosis.
--- fact 1
In summer 1921, Frederick Banting and Charles Best, working in J. J. R. Macleod's Toronto laboratory, obtained a glucose-lowering pancreatic extract from dogs.
--- fact 2
Leonard Thompson, aged 14, received the first human injection at Toronto General Hospital on 11 January 1922; James Collip's purer extract succeeded on 23 January.
--- fact 3
Banting and Macleod won the 1923 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine; Banting shared his prize money with Best, and Macleod with Collip.
--- fact 4
Banting, Best, and Collip sold their patents to the University of Toronto for one dollar each in 1923; Eli Lilly began shipping commercial supply that October.
--- fact 5
Nicolae Paulescu in Romania published pancreatic extract work in 1921 and patented it in 1922; priority is contested, and the Nobel committee did not recognize his claim.
--- context
Insulin belongs with [[technology/antibiotics|Antibiotics]], [[technology/vaccination|Vaccination]], and [[technology/anesthesia|Anesthesia]] among medical technologies that changed survival or care. It grew from laboratory [[theme/science|Science]] in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. It manages type 1 diabetes but does not cure it.
--- observer
Within the species' tool-lineage, insulin shows pattern-taming (animal experiments, chemical purification) yielding a molecule whose patents were handed to a university. That may work as a status-signal of public duty as much as a commercial choice; an alternative reading sees plain institutional pragmatism. Licensing records showing that royalty terms mattered more than the gesture would weaken this.
--- question 1
How should credit be divided among Banting, Best, Macleod, Collip, and Paulescu?
--- question 2
How much did the one-dollar patents shape early access to insulin?

=== technology/internal-combustion-engine
title: Internal Combustion Engine
type: technology
date: 1860
themes: technology
src: 1c3f427f57f9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
An internal combustion engine burns fuel inside its own cylinder, so expanding gas drives the piston directly, without the external boiler of a steam engine.
--- fact 1
Etienne Lenoir, a Belgian-born engineer in France, patented a noisy, inefficient coal-gas engine on 24 January 1860; hundreds were sold in the 1860s.
--- fact 2
Alphonse Beau de Rochas patented the four-stroke principle in 1862 but built no engine.
--- fact 3
Nikolaus Otto built a practical four-stroke engine in Germany in 1876; his patent was revoked in 1886 after Beau de Rochas's earlier patent surfaced, though he apparently worked independently.
--- fact 4
Carl Benz applied for a patent on his motor car on 29 January 1886; Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach built a rival high-speed engine vehicle in the same years.
--- fact 5
Rudolf Diesel filed a German patent application in 1892; prototypes ran from 1893 and an efficient engine ran at Augsburg in 1897.
--- context
The engine displaced steam in road transport and, in lighter form, powered the [[technology/airplane|Airplane]]. Diesel engines later replaced steam on many [[technology/railways|Railways]], and motor vehicles saw mass use in [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. It built on the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]] and the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] in [[region/europe|Europe]]. Thread: [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
As tool-lineage, the engine has no single inventor: Lenoir, Beau de Rochas, Otto, Benz, Daimler, and Diesel each inherited and modified earlier partial solutions, and patent disputes show how credit is assigned after the fact. This reading would weaken if records showed one design copied unchanged by the rest. An alternative reading stresses cheap petroleum moving through the exchange-web, and machining capacity, more than inventors.
--- question 1
How much did Lenoir's engine shape Otto's work?
--- question 2
When did engines overtake steam in global freight?

=== technology/iron-smelting
title: Iron Smelting
type: technology
date: c. 1200 BCE
themes: technology, war, trade
src: 43247defe9e4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Iron smelting, extracting iron from ore, began on a limited scale in Anatolia in the early 2nd millennium BCE, possibly earlier, and became widespread across the Near East from the late 2nd millennium BCE, about 1200 BCE onward. Ironworking also developed in sub-Saharan Africa, and whether it did so independently is debated.
--- fact 1
Extractive iron metallurgy is attributed to Anatolia on a limited scale in the early 2nd millennium BCE, with the first major expansion in the late 2nd and early 1st millennium BCE. A small iron lump from Kaman-Kalehoyuk in Turkey, from a layer dated to about 2500 to 2250 BCE, has been argued to be earlier smelted iron, possibly made from ore from elsewhere; this is a single find and remains under study.
--- fact 2
Iron ore is much more common than copper and tin.
--- fact 3
Smelting requires higher temperatures and produced a tougher metal once carbon-steel techniques improved.
--- fact 4
The earliest reported dates for iron smelting in sub-Saharan Africa (about 2000 BCE at Lejja in Nigeria, Oboui in the Central African Republic, and Gbatoro in Cameroon) are contested, because it is unclear that the dated charcoal is securely tied to iron-working remains and because of old-carbon contamination. Iron working in the Nok culture of Nigeria dates between about the 9th century BCE and 550 BCE.
--- fact 5
Scholars are divided on whether African ironworking was independent of the Near East. Older theories of diffusion from Carthage or Meroe have been challenged, and current evidence leans toward independent African development.
--- fact 6
Iron tools made it easier to clear forests and to arm large populations.
--- context
Iron smelting defines the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and followed [[technology/bronze-metallurgy|Bronze Metallurgy]]. In Africa, the [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]] spread ironworking across eastern and southern regions.
--- observer
The Observer notes that a common metal changed who could afford tools and weapons. Cheaper materials often lead to broader participation, in production and in war.
--- question 1
Did iron working arise independently in sub-Saharan Africa? Evidence leans that way, but the very oldest dates are disputed.
--- question 2
How early did smelting begin in Anatolia, and did the 2500 to 2250 BCE lump come from a working smelting tradition or an isolated experiment?

=== technology/irrigation-canals
title: Irrigation Canals
type: technology
date: c. 5500 BCE
themes: technology, earth-systems
src: da79c023a99e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Irrigation canals are channels that carry river water to fields. The earliest known examples are at Choga Mami in Iraq, dated to about the 6th millennium BCE, and canal systems later supported southern Mesopotamian cities and states in China.
--- fact 1
Choga Mami in eastern Iraq, a Samarran settlement occupied about 5600 to 4800 BCE, yielded canals identified by the excavators David and Joan Oates. Their date and irrigation role remain uncertain.
--- fact 2
Southern Mesopotamia has too little rain for grain, so cities such as [[place/uruk|Uruk]] relied on river canals by the 4th millennium BCE.
--- fact 3
The [[technology/code-of-hammurabi|Code of Hammurabi]], about 1754 BCE, makes a landholder pay for grain lost when a neglected dike or careless irrigation floods a neighbor's field.
--- fact 4
The Dujiangyan system in Sichuan, built by the state of Qin in 256 BCE, is still in use and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2000).
--- fact 5
Some scholars link salt buildup to declining yields in southern Mesopotamia in the late 3rd millennium BCE. The size of the effect is debated.
--- context
Irrigation canals extended the farming of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] onto dry river plains in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]]. See [[person/hammurabi|Hammurabi]] and the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]].
--- observer
The Observer regards irrigation canals as a running cost of the Settling: whoever depends on a canal must dig, clear, and police it, so shared rules become part of the tool. The Hammurabi liability clause is story-glue in the form of law holding infrastructure together. The reading would weaken if the earliest canals proved to be small local works kept up without wider authority.
--- question 1
Were the Choga Mami channels irrigation canals, and how old are they?
--- question 2
How much did salt buildup, rather than politics or climate, weaken southern Mesopotamian farming?

=== technology/microscope
title: Microscope
type: technology
date: c. 1620
themes: science, technology
src: a6e5465d9e6a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The microscope is an optical instrument that magnifies objects too small to see unaided. Compound microscopes appeared in the Netherlands around 1620, and single-lens designs later reached even higher magnification.
--- fact 1
Compound microscopes appeared in the Netherlands around 1620; the attribution to Zacharias Janssen, often dated to the 1590s, is now doubted, and priority is contested.
--- fact 2
Cornelis Drebbel demonstrated a compound microscope in London around 1621.
--- fact 3
[[person/galileo-galilei|Galileo Galilei]] adapted an existing design into his own instrument, presented in 1624 and called the occhiolino.
--- fact 4
Robert Hooke's Micrographia (1665) used compound-microscope observations of cork to coin the term "cell."
--- fact 5
Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using single-lens microscopes of his own making, was first to observe microorganisms in the 1670s; the Royal Society elected him a fellow in 1680.
--- context
The microscope emerged in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], alongside the telescope, part of a broader turn toward instrumented observation later systematized as [[theme/science|Science]]. Its early history involves competing claims among Dutch lensmakers, not one clean inventor.
--- observer
The microscope reads as part of a pattern-taming project, a tool built to push observation past an inherited sensory limit. In several surveyed cases its earliest credit is unstable, fitting a tool-lineage pattern where a tidy single-inventor story replaces a messier network of lensmakers refining each other's work. This weakens if the disputed 1590s Janssen claim is ever confirmed. An alternative reading treats the instrument as a byproduct of an existing lens trade rather than a triumph of curiosity.
--- question 1
Could any surviving instrument settle priority among the early Dutch claimants?
--- question 2
How much informal microscope use predated Hooke's and Leeuwenhoek's published findings?

=== technology/outrigger-canoe
title: Outrigger Canoe
type: technology
date: c. 1600 BCE
themes: technology, trade
src: 431e098bb28e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
An outrigger canoe is a narrow hull steadied by a float attached to one or both sides. It was developed by Austronesian-speaking seafarers, and its early use is dated here to the Lapita expansion of about 1600 BCE, mainly from indirect evidence.
--- fact 1
The float lets a slender hull carry a sail and cargo without capsizing.
--- fact 2
Reconstructed Proto-Oceanic vocabulary includes terms for canoe parts and seafaring, which indicates outrigger canoes in the Lapita period. Wooden hulls rarely survive, so the date rests on the Lapita expansion, not on a preserved canoe.
--- fact 3
The [[culture/lapita-culture|Lapita Culture]] spread from the Bismarck Archipelago to Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa by roughly 1000 to 800 BCE.
--- fact 4
The [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] carried outrigger canoes from Taiwan through the Philippines and Indonesia into the Pacific, and to Madagascar in the first millennium CE.
--- fact 5
Reliefs at [[place/borobudur|Borobudur]] in Java, about 800 CE, show ships with outriggers.
--- context
The outrigger canoe links the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] voyages of [[region/oceania|Oceania]] to the later [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] and to the trade of maritime [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the outrigger as a tool-lineage solution simple enough to rebuild from memory on every island: a hull, a float, and a sail. The knowledge traveled without writing, which is why the early record is linguistic and archaeological. An alternative reading gives the double-hulled canoe, which the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]] also used, the central role in the far Pacific, and the evidence cannot yet rank the two designs.
--- question 1
When and where did the outrigger first appear, and could it be older than the Lapita expansion?
--- question 2
How were outrigger and double-hull designs divided among island groups?

=== technology/paper-money
title: Paper Money
type: technology
date: c. 1000
themes: trade
src: 42e681c0f67f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jiaozi, paper notes from private merchants in Chengdu, Sichuan, appeared in the late 10th century as a lighter alternative to heavy iron coin. The [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]] government took over issuance in the 1020s, usually called the first government-issued paper money.
--- fact 1
Private merchant firms in Chengdu began issuing jiaozi, paper receipts redeemable for coin, from around the late 10th century; the exact founding year is undocumented.
--- fact 2
Several private issuers became insolvent and could not redeem their notes, prompting Song restrictions on private issuance.
--- fact 3
In 1023 CE the Song government founded a state monopoly bureau, the jiaozi wu, in Chengdu; standardized government notes circulated from 1024 CE, and sources differ on which year marks the true start.
--- fact 4
Song government jiaozi were valid for limited terms and required periodic paid reissue, unlike a permanent currency.
--- fact 5
Under the Yuan dynasty (13th-14th century) paper currency called jiaochao became the empire's main medium of exchange; Ming notes issued from 1375 later lost much value to overprinting.
--- context
Jiaozi built on established Chinese [[technology/paper|Paper]] manufacturing and merchant credit practices in Song commerce, part of a wider expansion of markets and credit across the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]]. Thread: [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer reads jiaozi as story-glue extended into an exchange-web: a printed promise substitutes for metal the species would otherwise carry, as long as everyone keeps believing it. In several surveyed cases that belief broke, when private issuers and later Yuan and Ming states overprinted notes, weakening any reading of paper money as inherently stable. An alternative, narrower reading treats it as simply a practical fix for a local coin shortage.
--- question 1
How precisely can the earliest private jiaozi issuance be dated, given that it seems to predate the surviving records of the 1023-1024 government takeover?

--- summary
Papyrus is a writing material made from strips of the papyrus plant, first used in Egypt. A blank roll from the tomb of Hemaka at Saqqara dates to about 2900 BCE, and the oldest inscribed papyri, from Wadi al-Jarf, to about 2560 BCE.
--- fact 1
A blank papyrus roll was found in the tomb of Hemaka, an official under the First Dynasty king Den, at Saqqara.
--- fact 2
A French team led by Pierre Tallet and Gregory Marouard found the oldest inscribed papyri at Wadi al-Jarf on the Red Sea coast in 2013. They include the logbook of an inspector named Merer, from about the 26th year of Khufu, recording limestone shipments for the Great Pyramid.
--- fact 3
Sheets were made from crossed layers of pressed stem strips and joined into rolls.
--- fact 4
Papyrus was exported around the Mediterranean and was a main writing material of Greece and Rome.
--- fact 5
Parchment and, from about the 10th to 11th century CE, [[technology/paper|Paper]] largely replaced it.
--- context
Papyrus is a [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] material of the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] that let the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] keep portable records, carrying the writing of [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. Finds tie it to the [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]] and the [[place/library-of-alexandria|Library of Alexandria]]. Threads: [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]], [[theme/technology|Technology]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer treats papyrus as an early step in memory-outsourcing that was light enough to travel: records kept in one place could be read in another. The oldest text found so far is a work logbook, not literature, which fits the view that administration drove early mark-code use. The reading would weaken if older religious or literary papyri turn up.
--- question 1
Were there inscribed papyri before the reign of Khufu that have not survived?
--- question 2
How much papyrus production did the state control?

=== technology/photography
title: Photography
type: technology
date: c. 1826
themes: art, science
src: d1bf6f8de139
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Photography is the process of fixing an image cast by light onto a treated surface, developed in Europe through several overlapping methods in the early 19th century.
--- fact 1
Nicephore Niepce produced the earliest surviving permanent photograph, View from the Window at Le Gras, using a bitumen-coated pewter plate; the date, 1826 or 1827, is debated.
--- fact 2
Niepce and Louis Daguerre partnered in January 1829 to refine the process; it ended with Niepce's death in 1833.
--- fact 3
The French Academy of Sciences announced Daguerre's daguerreotype process on 9 January 1839, with full details released that August, often called photography's public birth.
--- fact 4
William Henry Fox Talbot made the earliest surviving paper negative, at Lacock Abbey, in August 1835, later patenting his calotype process in 1841.
--- fact 5
The daguerreotype yielded a unique positive image while Talbot's calotype yielded a reproducible negative, so the two are separate, independently pursued inventions.
--- fact 6
Priority for inventing photography is contested, since workers in France and England reached working processes within about fifteen years, 1826 to 1841.
--- context
Photography developed in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], joining chemistry and optics with the fine arts, part of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]]. Faster processes soon made portraiture and documentation affordable, reshaping family remembrance and scientific record keeping.
--- observer
Photography extends memory-outsourcing, carrying the old habit of storing recollection outside the body into a faithful optical trace that can outlive its witnesses. It also opened a faster status-signal channel, as portrait sittings once reserved for painted commissions reached broader social strata within decades of 1839. This weakens if the images changed which memories persisted less than expected; an alternative view treats photography as intensifying an existing trend rather than starting one.
--- question 1
Should Niepce's earliest surviving image be dated 1826 or 1827?
--- question 2
How much credit do less documented contemporaneous experimenters deserve?

=== technology/porcelain
title: Porcelain
type: technology
date: c. 600 CE
themes: trade, art
src: 65a025a65138
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Porcelain is a hard, white, translucent ceramic made by firing kaolin clay at very high temperature. Developed in China, it became one of history's most widely traded manufactured goods.
--- fact 1
Proto-porcelain, a glazed, high-fired stoneware, appears in China by the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]], about 1600-1046 BCE, but is a cruder, distinct ware.
--- fact 2
True porcelain's start date is debated: the Eastern [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] by some scholars, the mature [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]] (618-907 CE) by others.
--- fact 3
Tang China produced white Xing ware in the north and green celadon Yue ware in the south, its two signature kiln traditions.
--- fact 4
Porcelain reached the Islamic world and later Europe via Indian Ocean and Silk Road trade from at least the Tang-Song period.
--- fact 5
European soft-paste imitations preceded true porcelain there by centuries; Meissen, Saxony, achieved Europe's first hard-paste porcelain around 1708-1709.
--- fact 6
The [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]] and later Ming kilns, including Jingdezhen, refined the technique and expanded exports.
--- context
Proto-porcelain in the Shang and Han is usually treated as a forerunner, not porcelain proper; the ware matured under the Tang and was refined under the Song and later Ming kilns, moving west along the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] and the Silk Road. Threads: [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
Porcelain sits in the tool-lineage as a craft perfected in one region then spread by an exchange-web spanning a hemisphere. In several surveyed cases, owning it served as a status-signal for households and courts far from its kilns. The reading weakens if it moved mainly as bulk cargo rather than prestige goods, and some historians instead stress its ordinary, utilitarian uses.
--- question 1
Did "true porcelain" begin as a Han innovation or a Tang maturation?
--- question 2
How much of the earliest export volume served Islamic-world demand versus domestic Chinese use?

=== technology/qanat
title: Qanat
type: technology
date: c. 714 BCE
themes: technology, earth-systems
src: f396fa7bea4e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A qanat is an underground channel that carries groundwater by gravity from a water-bearing slope to fields, reached by a line of vertical shafts. The earliest written record is an Assyrian account of 714 BCE describing qanats near Ulhu in Iran, and the system spread across Asia and North Africa.
--- fact 1
Sargon II of Assyria's account of his 714 BCE campaign against Urartu describes a district near Ulhu that stayed green with no river; later readers identify the source as qanats. The system may be older, and its origin is debated.
--- fact 2
Workers dig a gently sloping tunnel from the settlement up into the water table, with vertical shafts at intervals for removing soil and for ventilation.
--- fact 3
The qanat of Gonabad in northeastern Iran, one of the longest, has 427 shafts and about 33 kilometers of tunnels.
--- fact 4
More than 37,000 qanats are recorded in Iran; UNESCO inscribed eleven as "The Persian Qanat" in 2016.
--- fact 5
Similar systems are called karez, foggara, and falaj elsewhere in Asia and North Africa.
--- fact 6
Motor pumps in the 20th century lowered water tables and dried up many qanats.
--- context
The qanat is an [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] technology of the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], used under the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] and [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]]. Compare [[technology/irrigation-canals|Irrigation Canals]] and the [[technology/aqueduct|Aqueduct]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the qanat as a tool-lineage branch whose skill lived in specialist well-diggers, passed on by apprenticeship rather than text. A qanat also binds a community, since one tunnel serves everyone below it, making it story-glue and infrastructure at once. The reading would weaken if qanats were usually run by landlords or the state rather than by village agreement.
--- question 1
Where and when did the first qanat appear?
--- question 2
How were water shares divided, and who enforced them?

=== technology/radio
title: Radio
type: technology
date: c. 1895
themes: language, technology
src: 03eed7882685
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Radio is signaling by electromagnetic waves without wires. Practical wireless telegraphy emerged in the 1890s from several experimenters and grew into ship communication, broadcasting, and military use.
--- fact 1
In 1895, Popov showed a coherer-based lightning detector in St. Petersburg, Bose demonstrated millimeter-wave signaling in Calcutta, and Marconi began experiments in Italy.
--- fact 2
On 12 December 1901 Marconi reported hearing the Morse letter S at Signal Hill, Newfoundland, sent from Poldhu, England; signals were weak and witnesses few, so some doubted it.
--- fact 3
The 1909 Nobel Prize in Physics was shared by Marconi and Karl Ferdinand Braun for wireless telegraphy.
--- fact 4
In 1943 the US Supreme Court held Marconi's tuning patent invalid, citing earlier work by Stone, Lodge, and Tesla; it did not decide who invented radio.
--- fact 5
The Titanic's April 1912 distress calls went out by wireless; KDKA in Pittsburgh began scheduled broadcasts on 2 November 1920.
--- context
Radio extended the [[technology/telegraph-and-submarine-cables|Telegraph and Submarine Cables]] by sending coded signals without a wire and developed alongside the [[technology/telephone|Telephone]]. Ships and, in [[event/world-war-i|World War I]], militaries used it, and broadcasters later built mass audiences. It grew from [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] electrical engineering in [[region/europe|Europe]], Russia, India, and the United States. Threads: [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
Radio looks like a tool-lineage step to which several inheritors of Hertz-era physics each added a part, so many claimants is what the pattern predicts. Broadcasting then gave one voice reach into many households, a new scale of story-glue. This reading would weaken if records showed one team's design was decisive and independent of the others. An alternative credits Marconi's system-building rather than any scientific first.
--- question 1
Was the 1901 Newfoundland reception fully verified, given weak signals and few witnesses?
--- question 2
How should credit divide among theorists, experimenters, and commercial system builders?

=== technology/refrigeration
title: Refrigeration
type: technology
date: c. 1834
themes: technology, trade
src: 9d2ac3e96a59
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Refrigeration is engineered cooling used to preserve food and other perishables. It moved from an 1834 lab-scale prototype to reliable industrial compression systems adopted by brewers, meatpackers, and shippers from the 1850s to the 1880s.
--- fact 1
In 1834, Jacob Perkins, US-born and working in Britain, patented a closed-cycle vapor-compression refrigerator using ether: a proof of concept, not commercially developed at the time.
--- fact 2
Between 1850 and 1853, Alexander Twining patented ether vapor-compression ice-making and is credited with early US commercial refrigeration by the mid-1850s.
--- fact 3
James Harrison built an experimental ice machine at Geelong, Australia, in 1851 and a commercial one in 1854; a dozen systems served breweries and meatworks by 1861.
--- fact 4
In 1882 the ship Dunedin, fitted with a compressor, completed the first successful frozen-meat shipment from New Zealand to Britain, after an 1876 attempt had failed.
--- fact 5
Carl von Linde patented an improved ammonia compression process in Germany in the mid-1870s and founded a refrigeration firm in 1879 that had sold about 747 machines by 1890.
--- context
Refrigeration exemplifies [[theme/technology|Technology]] taking decades to move from principle to industrial reliability, part of the wider [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]]. Uptake tracked [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] [[region/europe|Europe]]'s brewing and meatpacking industries from the 1850s, and 1880s refrigerated shipping extended [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] to long-distance perishable goods.
--- observer
Seen as a single tool-lineage, refrigeration looks assembled by many hands over five decades: Perkins's 1834 device supplied a working principle that later engineers independently re-derived once materials and refrigerants matured. Its larger effect was widening the exchange-web, letting perishable food cross oceans and loosen diets from local seasons. This reading weakens if earlier, comparably reliable compression devices turn up.
--- question 1
How directly did Perkins's design influence Twining's and Harrison's later, separately patented systems?
--- question 2
When did refrigerated rail transport become commercially significant relative to ocean shipping?

--- summary
The Rosetta Stone is a stele inscribed in 196 BCE with a decree in Egyptian hieroglyphs, Demotic, and Greek, whose discovery in 1799 allowed Egyptian hieroglyphs to be deciphered.
--- fact 1
It records a decree of the priests of Memphis honoring Ptolemy V.

French soldiers found it in 1799 near Rashid (Rosetta) during Napoleon's Egyptian campaign.
--- fact 3
Britain took it after the French defeat in 1801, and it has been in the British Museum since 1802.
--- fact 4
Thomas Young made early progress on the Demotic text.
--- fact 5
Jean-Francois Champollion announced his decipherment of hieroglyphs in 1822.
--- context
The stone unlocked the writing of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] and is a landmark in the history of [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. Its Greek text allowed comparison with a known language, unlike the more distant [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]]. Rosetta Stone sits in the [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a script unread for about fourteen centuries was recovered because a single administrative decree happened to be repeated in three writing systems. A record survives to be read only if someone else keeps the key.

Where should the stone be housed?
