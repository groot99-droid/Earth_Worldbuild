=== place/chaco-canyon
title: Chaco Canyon
type: place
date: c. 850 CE – 1150
themes: religion, science, trade
src: 2d7f488a3424
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chaco Canyon, in northwestern New Mexico, was the ceremonial and trade center of the Ancestral Puebloan world from about 850 to 1150 CE.
--- fact 1
Its 'great houses', such as Pueblo Bonito, had hundreds of rooms.
--- fact 2
A network of roads radiated from the canyon.
--- fact 3
Buildings and rock carvings align with the sun and moon, such as the 'Sun Dagger' on Fajada Butte.
--- fact 4
Chaco is considered sacred by modern Pueblo peoples. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.
--- context
Chaco is a major site of the ancient peoples of [[region/north-america|North America]]. Its ties to [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] included traded goods such as macaw feathers and chocolate residues.
--- observer
The Observer notes a place built to keep time by the sky. Astronomy and architecture were joined, and the results were legible to those who knew the calendar.
--- question 1
What was the social structure of the Chaco system?

=== place/chang-an
title: Chang'an
type: place
date: c. 202 BCE – 904 CE
themes: trade, religion, art
src: 8a46dde106f8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chang'an was the capital of the Western Han dynasty from 202 BCE and, in a rebuilt form, of the Sui and Tang dynasties, on the site of present-day Xi'an in Shaanxi, China, until it was dismantled in 904 CE.
--- fact 1
Chang'an was founded as the Han capital after 202 BCE, with palace construction beginning in 200 BCE; the census of 2 CE recorded about 250,000 residents.
--- fact 2
The Sui dynasty built a new planned city, Daxing, southeast of the Han site from 582, and the Tang, from 618, kept its plan and the name Chang'an.
--- fact 3
Tang Chang'an was a grid of walled wards, with gates closed at night, enclosing about 84 square kilometers, with a central avenue and two state-supervised markets. Estimates of its population, with suburbs, run from several hundred thousand to about one million.
--- fact 4
Chang'an received envoys and merchants from Central Asia, Persia, Korea, Japan and Tibet. The monk Xuanzang returned there in 645 with Buddhist texts from India, and the Nestorian Stele, recording a Christian community, was erected there in 781.
--- fact 5
Chang'an fell to rebels in 756 during the An Lushan Rebellion and was briefly held by Tibetan forces in 763. In 904 the warlord Zhu Wen forced the court to move to Luoyang and had the city's palaces and houses pulled down.
--- fact 6
The plan of Chang'an influenced the layout of the Japanese capitals Nara and Kyoto.
--- context
Chang'an was founded just after the fall of the dynasty created by the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]], and it served as the capital of the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]]. It was the eastern end of the routes described in [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], where [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] were prominent traders, and its plan was echoed in [[place/nara|Nara]] and [[place/kyoto|Kyoto]]. Chang'an sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], with its peak in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], in the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Tang Chang'an turned social order into geometry: walled wards, gates closed at night, two supervised markets, and one avenue leading to the palace. A plan with claim-lines this exact was later copied at Nara and Kyoto, which suggests that a floor plan can carry story-glue about what a capital should look like. Its end in 904 shows the reverse, that a capital's meaning could be moved by moving the court and taking down the buildings. This reading would weaken if the Japanese copying turns out to have been driven mainly by practical drainage and defense rather than a shared idea of order.
--- question 1
How densely built was Tang Chang'an inside its walls, especially in the southern wards?
--- question 2
How faithfully were the plans of Nara and Kyoto copied from Chang'an, and by what route did the design travel?

=== place/chauvet-cave
title: Chauvet Cave
type: place
date: c. 36 ka
themes: art
src: 573f99a3af51
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chauvet Cave in the Ardeche region of southern France contains some of the oldest known figurative paintings, made by early Homo sapiens about 36,000 years ago.
--- fact 1
The cave was found in December 1994 by Jean-Marie Chauvet, Eliette Brunel, and Christian Hillaire.
--- fact 2
It holds hundreds of paintings and engravings of animals including horses, lions, mammoths, rhinoceroses, and bears.
--- fact 3
Radiocarbon dates place the main painted phase at about 37,000 to 33,500 years ago, in the Aurignacian, with a second phase of use about 31,000 to 28,000 years ago.
--- fact 4
A rockfall sealed the cave entrance, preserving the art until its discovery.
--- fact 5
It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2014; a full-scale replica, the Caverne du Pont d'Arc, opened nearby in 2015.
--- context
Chauvet is a benchmark for [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]] in Europe, and shows the artistic abilities of early [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] at a time when the [[species/woolly-mammoth|Woolly Mammoth]] was a common sight on the steppe. Chauvet Cave sits in the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the earliest painted animals show skilled drawing, not clumsy beginnings, suggesting that a longer tradition lies behind them. Access to the cave is now restricted and a replica is visited instead, a modern echo of the choice to make images rather than only look at the animals.
--- question 1
Were the paintings tied to ritual, storytelling, or something else?

=== place/chichen-itza
title: Chichen Itza
type: place
date: c. 800 CE
themes: religion, art
src: 774a9a74a150
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chichen Itza is a major Maya-Toltec city on the Yucatan Peninsula that flourished from about 800 to 1100 CE and is known for its stepped pyramid, El Castillo.
--- fact 1
It is in the northern Yucatan, where it prospered after many southern lowland cities declined.
--- fact 2
El Castillo, the Temple of Kukulcan, has 91 steps on each of its four stairways plus a top platform, totaling 365.
--- fact 3
At the spring and autumn equinoxes, sunlight creates a serpent-like shadow on the staircase.
--- fact 4
The site has the largest ball court in the Americas, about 166 meters (545 feet) long, and a sacred cenote used for offerings.
--- fact 5
It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1988.
--- context
Chichen Itza thrived as the [[event/classic-maya-collapse|Classic Maya Collapse]] reshaped the [[culture/maya|Maya]] world, and it is often compared with the later capital [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]. Chichen Itza sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the pyramid seems built to put a moving shadow on a staircase at a particular hour, and that visitors gather to watch it today. A structure works as a calendar and a show at once.
--- question 1
How much of the equinox effect was intended?

=== place/cusco
title: Cusco
type: place
date: c. 1200
themes: religion, war, art
src: 71701b641b47
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cusco is a city in the Andes of southern Peru, at about 3,400 meters, that Inca tradition says was founded around 1200 CE and that served as the capital of the Inca Empire until the Spanish took it in 1533.
--- fact 1
Cusco lies at about 3,400 meters in the Peruvian Andes; Inca tradition credits its founding to Manco Cápac around 1200, but archaeology shows that the Killke culture lived in the region from about 900 to 1200 and that the city grew slowly before Pachacuti.
--- fact 2
Under Pachacuti, whose reign is traditionally dated from 1438, and his successors, Cusco was the center of the Inca Empire, with the Coricancha (Temple of the Sun) at its ritual heart and the fortress of Sacsayhuamán above the city.
--- fact 3
Spanish accounts, notably Bernabé Cobo's in the 17th century, describe 41 lines called ceques radiating from the Coricancha and linking about 328 shrines (huacas).
--- fact 4
Some writers say the city was laid out in the shape of a puma, a claim that remains debated.
--- fact 5
Francisco Pizarro's force entered Cusco on 15 November 1533, and Manco Inca besieged the Spanish there for about ten months in 1536 to 1537 without success.
--- fact 6
The Spanish built churches and houses on Inca foundations; an earthquake in 1650 destroyed almost all colonial buildings and one in 1950 damaged more than a third of the city's structures, while many Inca walls remained standing.
--- fact 7
Túpac Amaru II was executed in Cusco's Plaza de Armas in 1781 during his rebellion against Spanish rule.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed Cusco in 1983, and it had 428,450 residents in 2017.
--- context
Cusco was the capital of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] and the seat of [[person/pachacuti|Pachacuti]], whose reign is linked to the empire's expansion, and it fell in the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-inca-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]]. It is the usual gateway to [[place/machu-picchu|Machu Picchu]], and [[person/tupac-amaru-ii|Túpac Amaru II]] was executed there. Inca administrators kept records on knotted cords, the [[technology/quipu|Quipu]]. Cusco sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Cusco was arranged as a map of belief and rule at once: the ceque lines tied kin groups and shrines to the capital, and the Coricancha placed the ritual center where the emperor's authority sat, a story-glue that could be read by walking the city. Its Inca walls outlasted earthquakes that felled Spanish buildings, and the Spanish built on them, so a later claim-line rests on an earlier one. Much of what is known of the plan comes from Spanish writers with their own aims, since Inca knotted-cord records are only partly understood. The reading would weaken if excavations show the ceque system was less regular than Cobo's account implies.
--- question 1
How regular was the ceque system in practice, and how much of Cobo's account reflects Inca practice rather than Spanish understanding?
--- question 2
How much of Cusco's Inca plan and stonework lies beneath the colonial city?

=== place/cordoba
title: Córdoba
type: place
date: c. 150 BCE
themes: religion, art, science
src: 1f7dbe8fa411
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Córdoba is a city on the Guadalquivir River in Andalusia, southern Spain, founded by Rome in the 2nd century BCE and best known as the capital of Muslim Iberia and of the Umayyad caliphate of Córdoba (929 to 1031).
--- fact 1
Córdoba, Roman Corduba, was founded in the 2nd century BCE (169 or 152 BCE, a contested date), became the colony Colonia Patricia in 46 to 45 BCE and later the capital of the province of Baetica.
--- fact 2
Muslim forces took the city in 711 or 712, and in 716 it became the provincial capital of al-Andalus.
--- fact 3
Abd al-Rahman I, an Umayyad prince who escaped the dynasty's overthrow by the Abbasids in 750, took power in 756; Abd al-Rahman III declared himself caliph in 929, and the caliphate ended in 1031 when Hisham III was expelled.
--- fact 4
The Great Mosque was begun in 785 to 786 and enlarged into the 10th century, and the palace-city of Madinat al-Zahra, begun about 936, was sacked in the civil war of the early 11th century.
--- fact 5
Estimates of Córdoba's population around 1000 CE range from about 100,000 to 1,000,000, and medieval accounts credit its libraries with very large collections.
--- fact 6
Muslims, Christians and Jews lived in the city under Muslim rule, non-Muslims with a protected but unequal legal status, and historians debate how far the term "convivencia" (coexistence) fits; Ibn Rushd (Averroes) and Moses Maimonides were both born in Córdoba in the 12th century.
--- fact 7
Ferdinand III of Castile took the city on 29 June 1236 and the mosque became a cathedral; from 1523 a cathedral nave was built inside it.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed the Mosque-Cathedral in 1984 and extended the listing to the whole historic center in 1994.
--- context
Córdoba was a provincial capital of the [[culture/romans|Romans]] before it became the seat of the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]]'s Iberian branch in [[culture/al-andalus|Al-Andalus]], a state that claimed the caliphate in rivalry with the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]]. Its conquest belongs to the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. Córdoba sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] by its earliest founding, with its peak in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], in the region [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Great Mosque of Córdoba carries several claim-lines in one structure: a Muslim ruler's place of worship enlarged over two centuries, then a cathedral with a nave set inside it, so the building itself records who held the city in each period. A court that gathered scholars, books and craftsmen while claiming the caliphate was making a status-signal aimed at rival caliphs; because the figures for the city's size and libraries are uncertain, the reading rests on the building more than on numbers. The reading would weaken if the court's patronage proves to have been driven mainly by internal politics.
--- question 1
How large was Córdoba in the 10th century, given estimates that differ by a factor of ten?
--- question 2
How did Muslims, Christians and Jews live alongside one another in practice, and how did that change?

=== place/doggerland
title: Doggerland
type: place
date: c. 10000 – 6000 BCE
themes: earth-systems
src: 4349ebd53546
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Doggerland was a lowland region of hunter-gatherers that connected Britain to continental Europe and lay where the North Sea is today until it was gradually flooded by rising seas.
--- fact 1
After the last ice age, sea level rose as ice sheets melted, and the land now under the southern North Sea was progressively submerged.
--- fact 2
It was largely underwater by around 6000 BCE, though a 2020 study argues that remnant islands survived for centuries after the tsunami and that the last land was submerged about a thousand years later.
--- fact 3
The Storegga Slide, a huge submarine landslide off Norway, produced a tsunami around 6200 BCE that struck the coasts of the region.
--- fact 4
Fishing vessels have dredged up animal bones and worked bone points from the seabed, and surveys have mapped submerged river valleys.
--- fact 5
Its inhabitants were Mesolithic hunter-gatherers.
--- context
Doggerland is a European counterpart to [[place/beringia|Beringia]], a lost landscape drowned by post-glacial sea-level rise, and it followed the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]] and the [[event/younger-dryas|Younger Dryas]]. Doggerland sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a landscape once lived on by humans now lies under fishing grounds and wind farms, its record kept only as tools brought up in nets. The land bridge between Britain and Europe is a reminder that coastlines are provisional.
--- question 1
How large was the population, and how did it respond to rising water?

=== place/elmina-castle
title: Elmina Castle
type: place
date: 1482
themes: trade, war
src: 5491ade725cc
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Elmina Castle is a fortress on the coast of Ghana built by the Portuguese in 1482 as São Jorge da Mina, the first European fort on the Gulf of Guinea, and later a holding place for people sold into the Atlantic slave trade.
--- fact 1
Elmina Castle stands at the town of Elmina in Ghana's Central Region; the Portuguese built it in 1482 by agreement with local rulers, to trade for gold, and named it São Jorge da Mina ("Saint George of the Mine").
--- fact 2
In the 16th century the Portuguese also brought enslaved people to Elmina from elsewhere on the African coast and exchanged them for gold with African traders.
--- fact 3
The Dutch captured Elmina in 1637, and Portugal ceded its claims in 1642; under Dutch rule the castle became a base of the Dutch West India Company, whose trade came to include large-scale export of enslaved people to the Americas.
--- fact 4
Enslaved people were held in dungeons in the castle before being shipped across the Atlantic; the total number who passed through Elmina is unknown.
--- fact 5
Britain took over the Dutch Gold Coast possessions in 1872, and Ghana became independent in 1957.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Forts and Castles of Ghana, including Elmina, in 1979.
--- fact 7
Elmina Castle is now a museum and memorial, and Ghana's "Year of Return" in 2019 drew large numbers of visitors from the African diaspora.
--- context
Elmina Castle was a node of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]], which ended in its legal form with the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]], and Ghana's independence in 1957 belongs to the wider story of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]]. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Elmina began as a fortified place for trading gold and became a holding place for people sold as commodities, so one set of walls served two exchange-webs in sequence. The Observer records the suffering of those held in its dungeons and shipped across the Atlantic, and notes that the site now works as a memorial where descendants come to mark losses for which few individual records survive, a grief-rite built around a building. The reading would weaken if better records show that most enslaved people shipped from the Gold Coast passed through other forts and not through Elmina.
--- question 1
How many people passed through Elmina Castle on their way to the Americas?
--- question 2
How did the local town of Elmina take part in the trade and change because of it?

=== place/forbidden-city
title: Forbidden City
type: place
date: 1406 – 1924
themes: art, religion, kinship
src: 153a86b4f02a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Forbidden City is the former imperial palace complex at the center of Beijing, China, built from 1406 to 1420 for the Ming dynasty and used as the residence and ceremonial center of Ming and Qing emperors until 1924.
--- fact 1
The Forbidden City was built under the Yongle Emperor from 1406 to 1420, as part of his move of the Ming capital from Nanjing to Beijing.
--- fact 2
Twenty-four emperors, 14 Ming and 10 Qing, lived and ruled there.
--- fact 3
The Forbidden City covers about 72 hectares, is surrounded by walls about 8 meters high and a moat, and has roughly 980 surviving buildings arranged along a north-south axis.
--- fact 4
Commoners were barred from entering, and yellow glazed roof tiles were reserved for imperial use.
--- fact 5
Puyi, the last Qing emperor, abdicated in 1912 after the Xinhai Revolution but was allowed to remain in the Inner Court until 1924, when he was expelled; the Palace Museum opened in 1925.
--- fact 6
Part of the palace collection was moved south from 1933 to escape Japanese advances, and some of it was taken to Taiwan in 1948 and 1949, where it forms the core of the National Palace Museum in Taipei.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the imperial palaces of Beijing in 1987.
--- context
The Forbidden City was the palace of the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] and the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]], and the imperial system it housed ended with the [[event/xinhai-revolution|Xinhai Revolution]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Forbidden City is rank made into architecture: nested walls and courtyards on one axis, restricted gates and reserved roof colors, so that a visitor's position in the plan matched their position in the court. Its story-glue was the idea of the emperor as the link between heaven and earth at the center of the world, which the layout was built to state. The same walls now admit visitors by ticket in the millions, so the site's function has shifted from limiting access to displaying a dynastic past as national heritage. A historian could weaken this reading by showing that the layout followed earlier palace conventions and practical needs more than a deliberate message.
--- question 1
How much of the standing Forbidden City dates from the original Ming construction, given repeated fires and rebuilding?
--- question 2
How did the ceremonial use of the palace change between the Ming and the Qing?

=== place/ganges-river
title: Ganges River
type: place
date: c. 1500 BCE
themes: earth-systems, religion, trade
src: a20d6377cc33
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Ganges River (Ganga) is a river of northern India and Bangladesh about 2,525 kilometers long, flowing from the Himalaya to the Bay of Bengal, whose plain has supported some of South Asia's densest populations and which is sacred in Hinduism.
--- fact 1
The Ganges rises at the Gangotri glacier in the Himalaya and crosses the northern Indian plain to the Bay of Bengal, where together with the Brahmaputra and Meghna it forms the world's largest delta; its basin supports several hundred million people.
--- fact 2
Farming communities occupied the middle Ganges plain from at least the 2nd millennium BCE, and the river became the axis of the later Vedic states and of 1st-millennium BCE kingdoms such as Magadha.
--- fact 3
In Hinduism the river is worshiped as the goddess Ganga; ashes of the dead are immersed in it, pilgrims bathe at sites such as Varanasi, and the Kumbh Mela at Prayagraj, where the Ganges meets the Yamuna, draws tens of millions of people.
--- fact 4
Sewage and industrial waste pollute long stretches of the river; India launched the Ganga Action Plan in 1985 and the Namami Gange program in 2014, and water quality remains poor in many stretches.
--- fact 5
The Farakka Barrage, completed in 1975, diverts water and has been a source of dispute between India and Bangladesh, and a treaty of 1996 governs the sharing of the river's dry-season flow.
--- fact 6
In 2017 a High Court in Uttarakhand declared the Ganges and Yamuna to be legal persons, and the Supreme Court of India stayed the ruling.
--- context
The Ganges River was the setting of the [[event/vedic-period|Vedic Period]] as it moved east and of later states such as the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]]; [[place/varanasi|Varanasi]] is its most important sacred city. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] (its early farming communities) and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Ganges is treated both as a resource and as a person, and that the two views collide in its condition: a river that is worshiped is also a sewer for millions, which suggests that reverence and upkeep are separate things. A river that crosses a national border shows that religious and hydrological geography does not follow state claim-lines, as the Farakka dispute shows. The reading would weaken if measurements show that ritual practice contributes little to the river's pollution compared with sewage and industry.
--- question 1
How much do religious practices contribute to the pollution of the Ganges compared with sewage and industry?
--- question 2
How will the river's flow change as Himalayan glaciers shrink?

=== place/goree-island
title: Gorée Island
type: place
date: c. 1444
themes: trade, kinship, war
src: b7ea647d4de8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Gorée Island is a small island off Dakar, Senegal, held in turn by the Portuguese, Dutch, British and French from the 15th century, and a symbol of the Atlantic slave trade, though historians debate how many enslaved people left from it.
--- fact 1
Gorée Island lies about 3 kilometers off Dakar; the Portuguese landed in 1444, the Dutch took the island in 1617, and the French took it in 1677, after which it changed hands several times between Britain and France until 1817.
--- fact 2
Gorée was a trading post for goods such as gum and hides and for enslaved people; a community of Afro-European merchant women, called signares, ran trade and property on the island, and some owned enslaved people.
--- fact 3
The House of Slaves, built in 1776 and having a doorway to the sea called the "Door of No Return", is a pilgrimage site for visitors.
--- fact 4
Many historians argue that Gorée was a modest embarkation point compared with larger ports on the Atlantic coast and that the House of Slaves did not hold the numbers often claimed, while others stress its symbolic importance; the debate is unresolved.
--- fact 5
African merchants and rulers took part in the trade alongside European ones.
--- fact 6
Visitors to Gorée include Nelson Mandela in 1991, Pope John Paul II in 1992 and US President Barack Obama in 2013.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Island of Gorée in 1978.
--- context
Gorée Island was a small port within the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]], which ended in its legal form with the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] (its first European contact) and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Gorée is a case where a place's symbolic weight and its documented role differ: as a memorial it commemorates the whole of the Atlantic slave trade, while the records suggest that a smaller share of captives left from it than from larger ports. Both can be held at once, since a memorial's function as a grief-rite does not depend on the count, and the Observer records the suffering of the enslaved without ranking sites by numbers. The signares show a kin-lattice and property system in which African women held power within the trade, a reminder that the trade was embedded in local societies. The reading would weaken if new archival counts show that Gorée handled a much larger share of the trade than the current estimates suggest.
--- question 1
How many enslaved people left from Gorée, and in what years?
--- question 2

=== place/great-wall-of-china
title: Great Wall of China
type: place
date: c. 700 BCE
themes: war, technology, trade
src: adeb14c23c2a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Great Wall of China is a series of walls, ditches, towers and forts built across northern China by successive states and dynasties from about the 7th century BCE to the 17th century CE, mainly to control movement between farming societies and the steppe.
--- fact 1
The Great Wall began as separate walls built by Warring States kingdoms such as Qi, Yan, Zhao and Qin from about the 7th century BCE (the dating of the earliest walls is uncertain); after unifying China in 221 BCE, Qin Shi Huang linked and extended walls in the north.
--- fact 2
The Han dynasty extended walls and signal towers far to the northwest along the Gansu corridor, to protect the routes westward and to counter the Xiongnu.
--- fact 3
The best-known standing sections, in brick and stone, date to the Ming dynasty (1368 to 1644), and building intensified after Mongol forces captured the Ming emperor Zhengtong at Tumu in 1449.
--- fact 4
A Chinese government survey published in 2012 reported about 21,000 kilometers of walls, trenches and natural barriers built across all dynasties, of which about 8,850 kilometers are Ming.
--- fact 5
Soldiers, conscripted laborers and convicts built the wall; how many died is unknown, and the large figures in later stories are not documented.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Great Wall in 1987. The claim that it is visible to the naked eye from the Moon is false.
--- context
The Great Wall of China links the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]], the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], the [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]] and the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]], and it protected routes used in [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. It sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] (its earliest walls), in the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Great Wall of China is a claim-line built at very large scale, yet it worked less as a sealed barrier than as a filter, with towers, garrisons and gates that let a state watch and tax movement between two ways of living. The image of one unbroken wall is partly a later story-glue, and some historians argue it grew mainly in the 19th and 20th centuries as a national symbol. Specialists could weaken this reading by showing that the wall's military effect was larger than a filtering account allows.
--- question 1
How much did the walls change frontier outcomes compared with diplomacy and regulated trade?
--- question 2
How many people worked on and died building each major section of the Great Wall?

=== place/harappa
title: Harappa
type: place
date: c. 3300 – 1900 BCE
themes: trade, technology, language
src: eb7f22d34670
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Harappa is an archaeological site in Punjab, Pakistan, on a dry bed of the Ravi River, occupied from about 3300 BCE and a major city of the Indus Valley Civilization until its urban phase declined after about 1900 BCE.
--- fact 1
Harappa was occupied from about 3300 BCE, and its urban "Mature Harappan" phase ran from about 2600 to 1900 BCE, with population estimates of about 20,000 to 40,000.
--- fact 2
Harappa had planned streets, brick drains and standardized weights, and its bricks followed a common size ratio used at other Indus sites.
--- fact 3
Harappa's seals, carved with animals and signs in a script that has not been deciphered, are found in many Indus cities, and Indus objects such as seals and carnelian beads have been found in Mesopotamian cities.
--- fact 4
Daya Ram Sahni began excavations at Harappa in 1921, after British engineers had taken bricks from the site in the 1850s for railway ballast.
--- fact 5
The city's urban phase declined after about 1900 BCE, and occupation continued at a smaller scale; proposed causes include changes in monsoon rainfall and river courses, and the causes are debated.
--- context
Harappa gave its name to the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], whose other great city was [[place/mohenjo-daro|Mohenjo-daro]], and its planned streets belong to the story of [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Harappa left thousands of inscribed seals that cannot yet be read, so its people's own account of themselves is missing, and what is known of its order comes from bricks, weights and drains that suggest a shared administrative logic. The lack of obvious palaces or royal tombs has led some scholars to propose that its rule differed from that of contemporary Mesopotamia and Egypt, though the proposal is debated. The reading would weaken if the script is deciphered and shows a more conventional royal order.
--- question 1
What do the signs on the Harappan seals say?
--- question 2
Why did the urban phase of Harappa decline after about 1900 BCE?

=== place/hattusa
title: Hattusa
type: place
date: c. 1650 – 1200 BCE
themes: war, language, religion
src: d1a65722d4a1
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hattusa was the capital of the Hittite Empire, at Boğazkale in central Turkey, made the royal seat in about 1650 BCE and abandoned around 1200 BCE, and it preserved an archive of about 30,000 clay tablets.
--- fact 1
Hattusa lies at Boğazkale in Çorum Province, Turkey; a Hattian settlement existed there earlier, and King Hattusili I made it the Hittite capital in about 1650 BCE.
--- fact 2
Hattusa was ringed by walls with monumental gates, including the Lion Gate and the Sphinx Gate, and had a Great Temple and a nearby rock sanctuary at Yazılıkaya.
--- fact 3
The capital moved briefly to Tarhuntassa under Muwatalli II and returned to Hattusa under Mursili III.
--- fact 4
Excavators, beginning with Hugo Winckler in 1906, found about 30,000 clay tablets in the royal archives, written in Hittite, Akkadian, Luwian, Hurrian and other languages and holding treaties, rituals and letters, including a version of the treaty with Egypt made after the Battle of Kadesh.
--- fact 5
Bedřich Hrozný identified Hittite as an Indo-European language in 1915.
--- fact 6
Hattusa was abandoned around 1200 BCE, during the Bronze Age Collapse; its central buildings burned, though whether the city was abandoned before or after the fire is debated.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Hattusha in 1986.
--- context
Hattusa was the capital of the [[culture/hittites|Hittites]], whose army fought at the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]] and whose empire ended in the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]]. The Hittite language recorded in its archive is central to the study of the [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. Hattusa sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Hattusa is a case of memory-outsourcing at imperial scale: a state kept its treaties, rituals and letters on clay in several languages, and the archive survived in part because fire hardens clay, so the destruction of the city helped preserve its records. Treaties written in the diplomatic language of the day, Akkadian, show rulers of rival powers binding one another with story-glue in the form of oaths sworn before gods. The archive records what the palace chose to keep, and the reading would weaken if the tablets prove to reflect chance survival more than the state's actual priorities.

Why was Hattusa abandoned, and was it burned before or after it was left?
--- question 2
How large was the population of Hattusa at its peak?

=== place/havana
title: Havana
type: place
date: 1519
themes: trade, war
src: 2bc5c3a8d48a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Havana is the capital of Cuba, on the island's northwest coast, established on its present harbor site in 1519 and long the assembly point for Spanish treasure fleets crossing the Atlantic.
--- fact 1
Havana was founded as San Cristóbal de la Habana; the settlement moved twice and was established on its present harbor site in 1519.
--- fact 2
From the 1560s Spanish fleets carrying silver from Mexico and Peru assembled at Havana for the crossing to Spain, and the city was fortified with La Real Fuerza (built 1558 to 1577) and El Morro (built 1589 to 1630).
--- fact 3
Britain captured Havana after a siege in 1762 during the Seven Years' War and returned it to Spain in 1763 in exchange for Florida; Spain then built the La Cabaña fortress.
--- fact 4
Sugar and coffee plantations, worked by enslaved people, expanded Cuba's economy from the late 18th century; hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans arrived in Cuba, many through Havana, and slavery there was abolished in 1886.
--- fact 5
The US battleship Maine exploded in Havana harbor on 15 February 1898, killing about 260 crew, an event that helped lead to the Spanish-American War.
--- fact 6
Since 1959 Havana has been the capital of a socialist state, and the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 placed Cuba at the center of the Cold War.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Old Havana and its fortifications in 1982.
--- context
Havana was a key port for the silver flow from [[place/potosi|Potosi]] and the mainland, for the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and for the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]], and it changed hands during the [[event/seven-years-war|Seven Years' War]]. It was a focal point of the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. Havana sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Havana grew because a state needed a fixed place to gather and guard a moving stream of wealth: silver had to be assembled somewhere before the crossing, and fortifications followed the cargo. Its later wealth from sugar rested on enslaved labor, which the Observer records as suffering and not only as an economic input. The waterfront was claimed in turn by the leading naval power of each era, and a historian could challenge this by showing that local Creole elites and enslaved people shaped the city as much as outside powers did.

--- summary
Hegra, also called Mada'in Salih, is an archaeological site in the AlUla region of northwestern Saudi Arabia, the second major city of the Nabataean kingdom after Petra, known for 111 monumental rock-cut tombs.
--- fact 1
Hegra lies north of the AlUla oasis on a caravan route between southern Arabia and the Mediterranean, and was part of the Nabataean kingdom from about the 1st century BCE.
--- fact 2
UNESCO records 111 monumental tombs cut into sandstone outcrops, 94 of them with decorated facades, along with dozens of wells; it inscribed Hegra in 2008, the first UNESCO site in Saudi Arabia.

--- fact 4

--- fact 5
Islamic tradition associates the site with the people of Thamud mentioned in the Quran, and travelers traditionally avoided it.
--- fact 6
The Ottoman Hejaz Railway built a station at Mada'in Salih in 1907 to 1908.
--- context
Hegra was a city of the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]] alongside [[place/petra|Petra]], on the routes of the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]], and was absorbed by the [[culture/romans|Romans]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that at Hegra a claim-line is engraved beside the grave: tomb inscriptions record who may be buried, who may not sell, and what penalty follows, so that property law reached past death in stone. A trading people put its most durable writing into grief-rites and inheritance, while most of its everyday commercial records have not survived. The reading rests on the surviving inscriptions being representative of Nabataean practice, which is uncertain because they come from a few unusually well-preserved sites.

=== place/hiroshima
title: Hiroshima
type: place
date: 1589
themes: war, science
src: 0ec122c0cd21
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hiroshima is a city on the delta of the Ōta River in western Honshu, Japan, founded as a castle town in 1589 and, on 6 August 1945, the first city struck by a nuclear weapon.
--- fact 1
Hiroshima was founded as a castle town in 1589 by the daimyo Mōri Terumoto and grew into a military and port center; its population before the bombing was about 350,000.
--- fact 2
At 8:15 a.m. on 6 August 1945 the US B-29 Enola Gay dropped a uranium bomb, "Little Boy", which exploded about 600 meters above the city.
--- fact 3
About 70,000 to 80,000 people are estimated to have been killed immediately, and total deaths by the end of 1945 are estimated at 90,000 to 166,000, from burns, injuries and radiation. The dead included civilians, soldiers, Korean forced laborers and a small number of Allied prisoners of war.
--- fact 4
Survivors, called hibakusha, faced radiation illness, increased cancer risks and discrimination; the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission began long-term studies of them in 1947, continued by the Radiation Effects Research Foundation.
--- fact 5
A second atomic bomb was dropped on Nagasaki on 9 August, and Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945; whether the bombings were necessary to end the war remains debated among historians.
--- fact 6
The ruined Genbaku Dome was preserved as a memorial within the Peace Memorial Park, and UNESCO inscribed it in 1996.
--- context
Hiroshima was the target of the first nuclear attack of [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]] and gave its name to the beginning of the [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]]. The city of [[place/kyoto|Kyoto]] had been on the list of possible targets and was removed from it. Hiroshima sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Hiroshima marks the first use of a nuclear weapon against a city, in a single morning, and records the deaths as suffering that continued for decades among survivors. The city has since turned its ruined center into a memorial, keeping the shell of one building standing and collecting survivors' testimony, a grief-rite and an act of memory-outsourcing meant to reach people who were not there. Historians disagree about whether the bombing was necessary to end the war, and this reading takes no side in that dispute. It would weaken if the memorial's message proves to matter less to visitors than to the city that built it.
--- question 1
How many people died in Hiroshima by the end of 1945, and how many later of radiation-related causes?
--- question 2
How has the memory of the bombing been taught and contested in Japan and elsewhere?
