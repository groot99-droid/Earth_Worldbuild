=== place/nazca-lines
title: Nazca Lines
type: place
date: c. 400 BCE – 500 CE
themes: religion, science
src: 3b2dc4d4f579
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Nazca Lines are a group of geoglyphs in the desert of southern Peru, made between about 400 BCE and 500 CE by the Paracas and Nazca cultures, showing animal, plant and geometric shapes and hundreds of straight lines visible mainly from the air.
--- fact 1
The lines lie in the Nazca Desert between the towns of Nazca and Palpa, about 400 kilometers south of Lima, and cover about 50 square kilometers.
--- fact 2
They were made by removing a top layer of reddish, iron-oxide-coated pebbles to expose lighter ground beneath; the lines and figures cut roughly 10 to 15 centimeters deep.
--- fact 3
The combined lines run more than 1,300 kilometers, and the largest figures, such as a 134-meter condor and a 93-meter hummingbird, reach lengths of tens to a few hundred meters.
--- fact 4
Peruvian archaeologist Toribio Mejía Xesspe noted the lines on foot in 1927; American historian Paul Kosok observed them from the air in 1940 to 1941 and proposed astronomical alignments, a theory developed further by Maria Reiche; researchers Gerald Hawkins and Anthony Aveni concluded in 1990 that the evidence did not support a systematic astronomical purpose.
--- fact 5
Other proposed purposes include ritual paths connected to water and fertility, walked in ceremony, and markers tied to underground water sources; no single explanation is agreed on.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Lines and Geoglyphs of Nazca and Pampas de Jumana in 1994.
--- fact 7
The site has been damaged by squatters, a limestone quarry, a 2014 Greenpeace protest that left marks near one geoglyph, and a 2018 truck crossing, and erosion and off-road vehicles remain ongoing threats.
--- context
The Nazca Lines were made well before the rise of [[place/cusco|Cusco]] and the Inca state, centered in the Andean highlands to the north-east. They sit in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a mark made to be read from the ground and a mark made to be read from the air are different kinds of pattern-taming, and the debate over the Lines is partly a debate over which kind their makers intended. Reiche's proposal that they served as an astronomical instrument was a claim about precision that later analysis did not confirm, which the Observer treats as an example of how a compelling reading can outrun its evidence. Because no written record from the makers survives, every purpose offered here is inference from the marks themselves. The reading would weaken if excavation along the lines turns up consistent ritual deposits that point to one purpose over the others.
--- question 1
Were the lines walked as ritual paths, viewed as an observatory, or used for another purpose entirely?
--- question 2
How did the people who made the Lines organize the labor needed to plan and clear such long, straight paths?

=== culture/ancestral-puebloans
title: Ancestral Puebloans
type: culture
date: c. 700 CE – 1300
themes: earth-systems, art
src: 0bc6a19b936d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Ancestral Puebloans built large stone and cliff settlements across the American Southwest from about 700 to 1300 CE, including the great houses of Chaco Canyon and the cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde, before drought and hardship led them to disperse into communities that became today's Pueblo peoples.
--- fact 1
Their architecture developed from simple pit houses into multi-room stone great houses, most visible today at Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde.
--- fact 2
Mesa Verde was occupied from about 450 to 1300 CE, and most of its famous cliff dwellings were built between 1190 and 1300.
--- fact 3
Chaco Canyon's role as a regional center declined from about 1130 to 1150, during a drought that began in 1130, and Mesa Verde was abandoned by about 1300 CE.
--- fact 4
A prolonged drought from 1276 to 1299, alongside food shortages, is the leading explanation for the departure from Mesa Verde, as communities moved south into what is now Arizona and New Mexico.
--- fact 5
Their descendants live today as the Hopi, Zuni and other Pueblo peoples of the Rio Grande region.
--- context
The Ancestral Puebloans built [[place/chaco-canyon|Chaco Canyon]] and [[place/mesa-verde|Mesa Verde]] in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], then dispersed under climatic pressure into the communities that became the Pueblo peoples. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that long droughts, rather than conquest or disease, ended two of the era's largest settlement complexes in North America, and that the people who left did not vanish but continued as named communities in the same region. The word "ancestral" acknowledges a continuity that popular labels like "vanished" once denied. A specialist could argue that drought alone cannot explain the timing and manner of abandonment and that social or political strain within the settlements compounded it, which would weaken a purely climatic reading.
--- question 1
How much did social or political conflict contribute to abandonment, alongside drought?
--- question 2
How direct is the line between specific Ancestral Puebloan communities and specific modern Pueblo nations?

=== culture/celts
title: Celts
type: culture
date: c. 800 – 50 BCE
themes: war, language
src: 9e39f1b21cde
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Celts were related peoples across Iron Age Europe, sharing related languages and, later, the La Tène artistic style, without forming a single state. Rome absorbed most continental Celts, and Celtic language and culture lasted longest in Ireland and parts of Britain.
--- fact 1
The Celts were many related tribal groups, including the Gauls of mainland Europe, speaking branches of the Celtic language family such as Gaulish.
--- fact 2
The La Tène culture, known for swirling metalwork, flourished from about 450 BCE until Roman conquest, following the earlier Hallstatt culture.
--- fact 3
Druids held major religious and political influence in many Celtic societies before Roman conquest, though their role varied by region and period.
--- fact 4
Rome defeated the Arverni in 121 BCE, and Julius Caesar completed the conquest of Gaul by 50 BCE despite resistance under leaders such as Vercingetorix.
--- fact 5
Rome targeted the druids directly, and continental and southern British Celts were largely assimilated, while Celtic culture lasted longer in Ireland and northern Britain.
--- context
The Celts inhabited much of [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] before conquest by the [[culture/romans|Romans]], in a period of wider contact across the ancient world that included [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that "Celtic" names a shared language family and later decorative style across many separate societies, not one people with one story-glue, and that Rome's targeting of the druids suggests it saw their meaning-engine as a source of resistance. Celtic identity lasted where terrain made conquest harder, in Ireland and the far north. A specialist could argue that the umbrella label was applied more consistently by later scholars than by the peoples themselves, which would weaken any reading of the Celts as one self-aware group.
--- question 1
How much shared identity did Celtic-speaking peoples recognize among themselves?
--- question 2
What was the druids' actual political role, and how real was the threat Rome saw in them?

=== culture/confucianism
title: Confucianism
type: culture
date: c. 551 BCE
themes: religion, kinship
src: d14dfde4a8b1
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Confucianism is a Chinese tradition of ethics and social order built on the teaching of Confucius (551 to 479 BCE), which became the official ideology of imperial China under the Han dynasty and shaped government and family life across East Asia.
--- fact 1
Confucius was an itinerant teacher during China's Spring and Autumn period who sought but largely failed to gain lasting influence as a government adviser in his own lifetime.
--- fact 2
His disciples compiled his sayings after his death as the Analects, the central text of the tradition.
--- fact 3
A key teaching, filial piety, extends genuine respect and care for parents into a model for loyalty within the wider political order.
--- fact 4
Under the Han dynasty, Confucianism became the state's official ideology, and an imperial examination system tested officials on Confucian texts, a practice that shaped Chinese government for centuries.
--- fact 5
The tradition spread beyond China to Vietnam, Korea and Japan, shaping their systems of government and social norms, including their own examination systems modeled on China's.
--- context
Confucianism developed in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] through the teaching of [[person/confucius|Confucius]], part of the wider [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]], and became state orthodoxy under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a teacher who failed to secure influence in his own time became, centuries after his death, the ancestor-weight behind an empire's entire method of selecting officials, with filial duty inside the kin-lattice serving as the template for loyalty to the state itself. A specialist could argue that the Han state's adoption of Confucianism reflects the state's own need for a stabilizing ideology as much as the teaching's inherent merit, which would weaken a reading of its rise as owed to its ideas alone.
--- question 1
How much did the Han state's adoption of Confucianism serve the state's own interests, apart from the teaching's content?
--- question 2
How uniformly was Confucianism practiced across China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam?

=== culture/franks
title: Franks
type: culture
date: c. 250 – 987 CE
themes: war, religion
src: f333462fba0f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Franks were a Germanic people who moved into Roman Gaul and built one of early medieval Europe's most powerful kingdoms, first under the Merovingian dynasty of Clovis I and later under the Carolingians, whose most famous ruler was Charlemagne.
--- fact 1
The Franks appear in Roman records in the third century CE on the lower Rhine and expanded into Gaul as Roman power weakened.
--- fact 2
Clovis I of the Merovingian dynasty defeated the last major Roman official in Gaul in 486 and had united the Frankish tribes by about 509.
--- fact 3
Clovis converted to Catholic Christianity, and his people's adoption of it helped unite the Franks and set them apart from neighboring Germanic kingdoms that followed other Christian traditions.
--- fact 4
The Merovingians ruled until the eighth century, when Pepin the Short, with papal support, founded the Carolingian dynasty.
--- fact 5
The Carolingian kingdom peaked under Charlemagne and later divided among his descendants. Its territories became the basis of medieval France, Germany and the Holy Roman Empire (not yet a vault note).
--- context
The Franks built their kingdom in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the early [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], filling the space left by the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]]. Their empire under [[person/charlemagne|Charlemagne]] was an ancestor of the Holy Roman Empire (not yet a vault note). See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler's personal conversion to his Roman subjects' majority faith became story-glue that welded conquerors and conquered into one people, setting the Franks apart from rival kingdoms that kept a minority creed. Two centuries later a change of dynasty was legitimized by the same meaning-engine, papal blessing replacing a discredited bloodline. A specialist could argue that Clovis's conversion served political advantage with his Gallo-Roman subjects as much as belief, which would weaken a reading of it as faith alone.
--- question 1
How much of Clovis's conversion reflects conviction versus calculated alliance with his Gallo-Roman subjects?
--- question 2
How unified was Frankish identity across the peoples the Merovingians and Carolingians ruled?
