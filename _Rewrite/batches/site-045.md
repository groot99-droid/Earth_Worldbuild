=== person/aisha-bint-abi-bakr
title: Aisha bint Abi Bakr
type: person
date: c. 614 – 678 CE
themes: religion, kinship, war
src: dfa3b6984716
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Aisha bint Abi Bakr (about 614 to 678 CE) was a wife of the Prophet Muhammad and a daughter of the first caliph, Abu Bakr. She is credited with transmitting more than two thousand hadith and took part in the Battle of the Camel in 656.
--- fact 1
Aisha was born about 614 in Mecca to Abu Bakr and Umm Ruman. She died in Medina in July 678 (58 AH), a date that is uncertain.
--- fact 2
Her marriage to Muhammad was contracted in Mecca and began in Medina about 623. Classical sources give her age as six or seven at the contract and nine at the start. These figures come from later hadith and biographies, and some modern scholars question them.
--- fact 3
She is credited with more than 2,000 hadith on ritual, inheritance, ethics and daily life, found in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, and she also taught.
--- fact 4
In 656 she opposed Ali ibn Abi Talib, demanding punishment of the killers of the caliph Uthman. Her forces lost the Battle of the Camel near Basra. Ali pardoned her, and she withdrew from politics.
--- fact 5
Sunni tradition honors her as a scholar. Shia tradition views her critically because of her opposition to Ali.
--- context
Aisha lived in the [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]] in the early [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], in the years of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. See [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]], [[person/khadija-bint-khuwaylid|Khadija bint Khuwaylid]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one woman's memory of a household became a source of guidance for millions, carried first by sound-code and written down generations later. Marriage into the Prophet's household made the kin-lattice a route to authority. A specialist could argue that attributions to her were shaped by later sectarian debate and that a chain of transmission does not show who spoke, which would weaken any reading that treats the reports as her own words.
--- question 1
How much of the hadith attributed to her can be traced to her own teaching?
--- question 2
Which reported ages and dates come from the earliest sources?

=== person/ajuricaba
title: Ajuricaba
type: person
date: c. 1727
themes: war, trade
src: 968e4d3bd989
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ajuricaba (died about 1727) was a leader of the Manaos people of the Rio Negro, in the Brazilian Amazon, who resisted Portuguese colonization and the enslavement of Indigenous people in the 1720s. Almost everything known about him comes from Portuguese sources.
--- fact 1
Ajuricaba led the Manaos (Manaó) people of the Rio Negro valley, in present-day Amazonas, Brazil. His birth year and origins are unclear; the date given here is that of his death.
--- fact 2
He resisted Portuguese colonization and the enslavement of Indigenous people in the 1720s. Portuguese accounts say he allied with Dutch traders from the Guianas, who were rivals of Portugal.
--- fact 3
The governor of Grão-Pará, João da Maia da Gama, sent forces against him. Some accounts say he was captured through the betrayal of allies.
--- fact 4
Portuguese accounts say that while he was being taken in chains toward Belém, he and his men rose against their guards, and he threw himself into the river and drowned, in 1727 (some sources say 1728).
--- fact 5
He is remembered in Brazil as a symbol of Indigenous resistance. The city of Manaus takes its name from the Manaos people.
--- context
Ajuricaba's resistance took place in the [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. For a later defense of forest communities in the same region, see [[person/chico-mendes|Chico Mendes]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ajuricaba is known almost entirely through the reports of officials who fought him, and that the same reports give the death, a leap into a river rather than captivity, that later writers made into a symbol. If the Dutch alliance in those reports is accurate, Amazonian leaders were bargaining inside an exchange-web of European rivalries rather than outside it. The reading would weaken if Manaos oral accounts described his aims differently.
--- question 1
What led him into direct conflict with the colonists?
--- question 2
What do Manaos oral accounts say about him?

=== person/akbar
title: Akbar
type: person
date: 1542 – 1605
themes: war, religion
src: d78f8f713d01
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Akbar (1542 to 1605) was the third Mughal emperor, who took the throne as a child in 1556 and built the empire into the dominant power of the Indian subcontinent, joining military conquest to an unusual policy of religious accommodation.
--- fact 1
Akbar was born in 1542 and became emperor in 1556 at age 13, at first guided by the regent Bairam Khan.
--- fact 2
He expanded Mughal territory through campaigns including the capture of Chittor in 1568, and later Gujarat and Bengal, and married into Rajput ruling families as alliances.
--- fact 3
In 1564 he abolished the jizya, a tax on non-Muslims, and promoted Sulh-i Kul, "peace with all," as a governing principle.
--- fact 4
In 1582 he introduced Din-i Ilahi, a court order drawing on several traditions, which never became a mass following and stayed largely confined to select courtiers.
--- fact 5
His court included the historian Abul Fazl, who wrote the Akbarnama, an official chronicle of his reign.
--- fact 6
He died in 1605 and was succeeded by his son Jahangir.
--- context
Akbar ruled in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], building the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] into a subcontinental power. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that an empire built by sanctioned harm was held together partly by treating its many meaning-engines as compatible rather than requiring one dominant faith, a hedge against permanent uprising in a realm of very mixed belief. Din-i Ilahi's small following shows the limits of a ruler's own synthesis, however genuine, as a substitute for existing traditions. A specialist could argue that Sulh-i Kul served statecraft as much as personal conviction, which would weaken a reading of Akbar as simply tolerant by nature.
--- question 1
How far was his religious policy personal conviction, and how far practical governance of a diverse empire?
--- question 2
How reliable is the Akbarnama as a record, given it was written for his own court?

=== person/al-biruni
title: Al-Biruni
type: person
date: c. 973 CE – 1048
themes: science, language
src: d61123e4f2c1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Al-Biruni (973 to after 1048) was a scholar from Khwarazm in Central Asia who wrote on astronomy, mathematics, geography, chronology and India. His Kitab al-Hind (about 1030) is a major medieval study of Indian science, religion and customs.
--- fact 1
Al-Biruni was born in 973 in Kath, in Khwarazm in present-day Uzbekistan, and died after 1048, perhaps about 1050.
--- fact 2
After the Ghaznavid ruler Mahmud conquered Khwarazm in 1017, he was taken to Ghazni, where he worked under Mahmud and his successors.
--- fact 3
He spent years in northwestern India, learned Sanskrit, and wrote the Kitab al-Hind, describing Indian science, religion, society and customs from Sanskrit texts and conversations with Indian scholars.
--- fact 4
His Chronology of Ancient Nations (about 1000) compares the calendars and dating systems of many cultures.
--- fact 5
He calculated the radius of the Earth as about 6,320 km by measuring the dip of the horizon from the top of a hill, close to the modern value.
--- fact 6
He discussed whether the Earth rotates on its axis and treated it as a question that astronomy alone could not settle.
--- fact 7
He exchanged questions on natural philosophy with Ibn Sina.
--- context
Al-Biruni's career ran from Khwarazm in the [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]] to India during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. He corresponded with [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] and worked in a region that had also produced [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]]. His India book discusses numerals; see [[technology/zero-and-place-value-notation|Zero and Place-Value Notation]]. See [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Biruni described a foreign meaning-engine and its sciences in their own terms, learning its sound-code first and stating that his book was a report and not an argument, an aim the Observer method shares. His work shows an exchange-web of scholars linking Central Asia and South Asia that could carry ideas even as armies crossed the same routes. A specialist could argue that he read Indian tradition through Islamic and Greek categories, which would weaken the reading of his account as a neutral report.
--- question 1
How far did he read Indian thought through Islamic and Greek categories?

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (about 780 to 850 CE) was a Persian scholar at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad whose work on algebra and Hindu-Arabic numerals shaped mathematics.
--- fact 1

--- fact 2
The word 'algorithm' derives from the Latinized form of his name.
--- fact 3
His work helped introduce Hindu-Arabic numerals and place-value arithmetic to the wider world.
--- fact 4
He also compiled astronomical tables and geographical data.

He worked in the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]]. His texts later influenced the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a chain of transmission: Indian numerals, Persian and Arabic scholarship, then Latin translation. Knowledge moved through translators and through empire.
--- question 1
How original was his work relative to earlier traditions?

=== person/albrecht-durer
title: Albrecht Dürer
type: person
date: 1471 – 1528
themes: art, technology
src: b47a785a3aca
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Albrecht Dürer (21 May 1471 to 6 April 1528) was a painter and printmaker from Nuremberg who brought the Italian Renaissance north. His woodcuts and engravings, sold across Europe, made him the first artist with an international reputation built largely on prints.
--- fact 1
Dürer was born in Nuremberg, the son of a Hungarian-born goldsmith. From 1486 he was apprenticed to Michael Wolgemut, whose workshop made woodcuts for book illustration.
--- fact 2
He travelled to Venice in 1494, and again in 1505 to 1507, where Giovanni Bellini treated him as an equal. In 1498 he published the Apocalypse, 15 large woodcuts that made him famous.
--- fact 3
From 1512 Emperor Maximilian I employed him and granted him a pension. On a trip to the Netherlands in 1520 to 1521 to have it confirmed by Charles V, his diary recorded his amazement at Aztec treasures sent from Mexico.
--- fact 4
He sympathised with Martin Luther's reforms. In his last years he published treatises on measurement, fortification and human proportion.
--- fact 5
His "AD" monogram served as a trademark, and he took action against copyists who used it.
--- fact 6
Key works include Self-Portrait at the Age of Twenty-Eight (1500), Young Hare (1502), Melencolia I (1514) and The Rhinoceros (1515).
--- fact 7
His prints were copied by artists from Italy to Persia and Mughal India, and Raphael exchanged drawings with him. In the 19th century German Romantics made him a national hero.
--- context
Dürer worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]], in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the years of the [[event/protestant-reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. His diary touches the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]], and his prints reached the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a maker who saw that a picture reproduced on paper travels farther than one hung in a room. Prints turned a workshop skill into a good on the exchange-web, and his monogram worked as a trademark he was ready to defend. His self-portraits present the artist as a learned, almost god-like creator, a status-signal claimed for the maker rather than the patron. The 19th-century cult of him as a German hero is a later story-glue, not a fact about his own time.
--- question 1
How far did his prints, rather than his paintings, set the terms of his fame in his own lifetime?

=== person/alice-guy-blache
title: Alice Guy-Blaché
type: person
date: 1873 – 1968
themes: art, technology
src: 9604bef072f0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Alice Guy-Blaché (1873–1968) was a French filmmaker who began directing at the Gaumont Film Company in Paris in 1896 and later became the first woman to found and run her own film studio. She is credited as one of cinema's earliest narrative directors, though her work was erased from film history for most of the twentieth century.
--- fact 1
Born in Saint-Mandé, France, in 1873, she became Léon Gaumont's secretary in 1894 as his company moved into motion pictures.
--- fact 2
After seeing an early demonstration film, she won permission to direct in her spare time; the result, La Fée aux choux (1896), is often called cinema's first narrative film.
--- fact 3
She rose to head of production at Gaumont, experimenting with hand-tinted colour and an early sound process, Phonoscènes, and in 1906 satirized gender roles in the reversal comedy Les Résultats du féminisme.
--- fact 4
In 1907 she married cameraman Herbert Blaché, moved to the United States, and in 1910 co-founded the Solax Company in New Jersey, later becoming its president.
--- fact 5
At Solax she directed social dramas like Making an American Citizen and Falling Leaves (1912), tackling immigration and domestic abuse more directly than most peers.
--- fact 6
Financial strain, Hollywood's rise, and an 1922 divorce ended Solax; she returned to France unable to direct again, and died in New Jersey in 1968.
--- fact 7
For decades her films were credited to male colleagues, until 1970s scholarship using her memoir and studio records restored her authorship.
--- context
Guy-Blaché worked in Paris and then New Jersey, within [[region/europe|Europe]]'s [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] as film crossed the Atlantic. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a subject who turned a device built to record motion into a stage for invented stories, folding a fairy tale into a new tool-lineage almost at once. More telling is what came after: for decades her work was catalogued under male colleagues' names, a status-signal shift erasing a working director from her own trade's memory even as the films survived. That her authorship needed a later memoir and buried studio records to be restored shows how thin an industry's memory-outsourcing can be for a low-status contributor. Her reversal comedy, imagining rule by women, reads as an experiment in showing an audience its own arrangements as built, not natural; her frustrated later years are known mainly from her own testimony.
--- question 1
How many of Guy-Blaché's hundreds of lost films might still surface, and how much of her Gaumont-era output remains misattributed even today?

=== person/alphonse-mucha
title: Alphonse Mucha
type: person
date: 1860 – 1939
themes: art, trade
src: 43da647f6bee
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Alphonse Mucha (1860 to 1939) was a Moravian-born designer whose decorative posters for Sarah Bernhardt and commercial clients defined the visual style of Art Nouveau. He resisted that label and saw his true work in a cycle of paintings on the history of the Slavic peoples.
--- fact 1
Mucha was born in Ivančice in Moravia, then part of Austria-Hungary, and worked as a theatrical scene painter before moving to Paris in 1887.
--- fact 2
In December 1894 a Paris printing house needed a poster for Sarah Bernhardt's play Gismonda at short notice over the holidays and turned to Mucha as the only designer available. Its success led Bernhardt to sign him to a six-year contract for posters, sets and costumes.
--- fact 3
In 1902 he published Documents décoratifs, a pattern book of his ornamental style for other designers and craftsmen.
--- fact 4
From about 1910 he devoted much of his career to The Slav Epic, a cycle of twenty monumental canvases on Slavic history, which he donated to the city of Prague. It is now on permanent public display there.
--- fact 5
His formula was elongated women within ornamental frames, reproduced in muted colour lithography on theatre posters, cigarette papers, bicycles and calendars, reaching shops, cafés and middle-class homes across Europe.
--- fact 6
After Nazi Germany occupied Czechoslovakia in 1939 he was arrested and interrogated by the Gestapo, and died months later, reportedly of complications following his detention.
--- fact 7
"Mucha style" is often used interchangeably with Art Nouveau, and his flowing hair and ornamental frames were borrowed by 1960s psychedelic concert posters.
--- context
Mucha worked in Paris and Prague, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and was arrested just before the outbreak of [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a designer whose fame began with a printing house's emergency, a poster needed over a holiday, so that a whole style began as a scheduling accident. Mass printing carried fine decoration into shops and cafés, while the artist thought his real work was a cycle on the history of a people, a story-glue of nationhood. The label "Art Nouveau", which he resisted, shows how a category can outrun the intention of the person it names. The link between his detention and his death is reported rather than established.
--- question 1
What caused Mucha's death in 1939, and how far was it connected to his interrogation?

=== person/amanirenas
title: Amanirenas
type: person
date: c. 40 – 10 BCE
themes: war, kinship
src: 505fbd6ec939
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Amanirenas was a queen (kandake) of the Kingdom of Kush who led the war against Roman Egypt from about 25 to 22 BCE and won a peace settlement from Augustus. Her birth and death years are unknown; the dates given here mark her approximate reign, about 40 to 10 BCE.
--- fact 1
Amanirenas ruled Kush from its capital region around Meroë; her birth and death years are unknown, and her reign is estimated.
--- fact 2
About 25 BCE Kushite forces attacked the Roman frontier in southern Egypt, taking towns including Syene, Elephantine and Philae, while the Roman prefect's army was away.
--- fact 3
The prefect Gaius Petronius counterattacked about 24 BCE and sacked the Kushite city of Napata.
--- fact 4
Peace was negotiated with Augustus about 21 to 20 BCE on Samos; Kush is reported to have paid no tribute.
--- fact 5
The Greek geographer Strabo describes a one-eyed, mannish queen "Candace" who led the Kushites; scholars generally identify her as Amanirenas.
--- fact 6
A bronze head of Augustus taken in the raid was found buried beneath the steps of a temple at Meroë in 1910 and is now in the British Museum.
--- context
Amanirenas ruled the [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]] in the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and fought the [[culture/romans|Romans]] under [[person/augustus|Augustus]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a bronze head of the emperor, buried where people would step on it, shows how captured images can serve as claims as well as art. Most of what is said about her personally comes from a Greek geographer who describes her by her body, while the Kushite record in Meroitic script is only partly readable, so her own side is mostly silent. The reading would weaken if her identification with Strabo's queen proved mistaken.
--- question 1
What did the Kushite side want from the war, and what did the peace terms provide?

=== person/amanitore
title: Amanitore
type: person
date: c. 1 – 25 CE
themes: war, kinship, art
src: 0538b1e29814
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Amanitore was a queen of the kingdom of Kush who co-ruled from the capital Meroe alongside a king, Natakamani, usually thought to be her son, during a prosperous period in the middle of the 1st century CE.
--- fact 1
Kushite royal women held the title kandake, often rendered "candace"; inscriptions and temple reliefs show Amanitore depicted with the same royal regalia and war imagery as Natakamani, suggesting genuinely shared rule rather than a purely ceremonial role.
--- fact 2
The pair sponsored a major building program, including temples at Meroe, Naqa and Amara, among the best-preserved Kushite monuments to survive.
--- fact 3
A relief at the Lion Temple at Naqa shows Amanitore, like Natakamani, smiting bound captives, an image of royal power normally reserved for kings elsewhere in the Nile Valley tradition.
--- fact 4
Her exact reign dates are not fixed by any king list; estimates for the coregency range across the early to mid-1st century CE, sometimes placed as contemporary with the Roman emperor Nero.
--- context
Amanitore co-ruled the [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]] from the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Amanitore's monuments give her the same iconography of conquest as a king, in a period when Kush's neighbor Rome rarely depicted its own empresses that way. Whether this reflects real personal authority or a Kushite convention of dual kingship that did not map onto individual power is difficult to settle from the images alone.
--- question 1
Did Amanitore hold power independently of Natakamani, or primarily through her position as his mother?

=== person/ambiorix
title: Ambiorix
type: person
date: c. 60 – 53 BCE
themes: war
src: 4fac8d09960e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ambiorix (active 54 to 53 BCE) was co-ruler of the Eburones, a Belgic tribe in what is now eastern Belgium, remembered for destroying a Roman legion during a revolt against Julius Caesar's occupation of Gaul.
--- fact 1
He shared kingship of the Eburones with an older co-ruler, Cativolcus, an arrangement Caesar's own account describes but does not fully explain.
--- fact 2
In the winter of 54 BCE he lured a Roman force under Quintus Titurius Sabinus and Lucius Aurunculeius Cotta out of its fortified camp with false promises of safe passage, then ambushed and destroyed it, killing both commanders.
--- fact 3
Caesar responded with a campaign of deliberate devastation against the Eburones intended to eliminate the tribe entirely, but Ambiorix himself escaped capture and vanishes from the historical record after 53 BCE.
--- fact 4
Nearly everything known about him comes from Caesar's own Commentarii de Bello Gallico, written to justify Caesar's conduct of the war to a Roman audience.
--- context
Ambiorix led resistance against the [[culture/romans|Romans]] in [[region/europe|Europe]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ambiorix survives in history almost entirely through the writing of the general whose legion he destroyed, an author with every incentive to portray the ambush as treacherous deceit rather than a defensible tactic of war waged by an occupied people. Ambiorix's own reasoning and any account other than Caesar's are lost.
--- question 1
What became of Ambiorix after 53 BCE, and did he survive Caesar's campaign of retaliation against his people?

--- summary
Amir Khusrau (1253 to 1325) was the leading court poet of the Delhi Sultanate, writing in Persian while drawing on the Indian world around him. He is regarded as the founding figure of Indo-Persian literature and is traditionally credited with founding qawwali, the devotional music of South Asian Sufi shrines.
--- fact 1
Khusrau was born in 1253 near Delhi, to a Turkic father who had migrated from Central Asia and an Indian mother. He referred to this mixed heritage throughout his work.
--- fact 2
He served as court poet under a rapid succession of Delhi sultans over more than four decades, through palace coups and dynastic changes.
--- fact 3
He wrote in Persian, the language of the Indo-Islamic courts. His five long narrative poems, dated about 1298 to 1302, answer the Khamsa of the Persian poet Nizami Ganjavi and retell Shirin and Khusrau, Layla and Majnun, and the legend of Alexander the Great.
--- fact 4
He was a devoted follower of the Sufi mystic Nizamuddin Auliya. Legend says Khusrau died within months of his master in 1325; their shrines stand together in Delhi.
--- fact 5
He is traditionally credited with founding qawwali, still performed at Sufi shrines across India, Pakistan and Bangladesh. Later legend also credits him with inventing the sitar and tabla, without firm historical evidence.
--- context
Khusrau lived in the [[culture/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer sees a poet who survived several dynasties by being useful to each, with a mixed parentage that he treated as a fact to be stated openly. Persian was the sound-code and mark-code of the courts, while the Indian setting supplied his material. What outlasted the sultans was the devotional music, which needed no palace, only a shrine and singers. Credit for instruments and forms rests on legend, so the picture of him as founder is partly the work of later admirers.
--- question 1
Which of the musical forms and instruments credited to him did he actually introduce?

=== person/amrita-sher-gil
title: Amrita Sher-Gil
type: person
date: 1913 – 1941
themes: art
src: 327063ce16d9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Amrita Sher-Gil (1913 to 1941) was a Hungarian-Indian painter, born in Budapest to a Sikh father and a Hungarian-Jewish mother, who trained in Paris before returning to India to paint its people, especially its women. Often called India's first great modern woman artist, she fused European Post-Impressionist technique with Ajanta murals and Mughal-Rajput miniature painting across barely a decade of work.
--- fact 1
Born in Budapest on 30 January 1913 to a Sikh father and a Hungarian-Jewish mother, she moved to India in 1921 and began painting as a child in Shimla.
--- fact 2
She studied art in Paris from 1929, and in 1933 became the youngest associate, and the only Asian, elected to the Grand Salon.
--- fact 3
Returning to India in 1934, a 1937 journey through South India and the Ajanta murals reshaped her style into simplified, monumental figures in flat colour.
--- fact 4
Three Girls (1935) won the Bombay Art Society's gold medal in 1937, the year she also painted Bride's Toilet and Brahmacharis; later works such as Village Scene (1938) and Haldi Grinders (1940) drew on Indian miniature painting to depict rural women's lives.
--- fact 5
She married her Hungarian cousin, Victor Egan, in 1938; the couple later settled at Saraya in Uttar Pradesh before moving to Lahore in 1941.
--- fact 6
She died in Lahore on 5 December 1941, aged 28, days before her first major solo exhibition was due to open.
--- fact 7
In 1976 India declared her paintings National Art Treasures; most now hang in New Delhi's National Gallery of Modern Art, and her work has since set auction records.
--- context
Sher-Gil worked in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], between British India and the art world of Paris. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]], given her Sikh-Hungarian-Jewish parentage and marriage within that extended family.
--- observer
The Observer notes a subject formed at the seam of two kin-lattices, a Sikh father and a Hungarian-Jewish mother, later married within that same extended family, and who crossed from Paris's mark-code academies to the mural and miniature conventions of Ajanta. That she painted Indian women as silent and waiting, while she herself moved freely between continents, suggests an observer describing a system's constraints from an unusually mobile vantage. The title "India's first great modern woman artist" reads as story-glue, a label a new nation needed for its account of modern culture; the 1976 declaration of her paintings as National Art Treasures converts private output into status-signal for the state. The record of her final illness is thin: it is reported only as sudden and fatal, with no cause given.
--- question 1
What caused Sher-Gil's sudden fatal illness in Lahore in December 1941?

=== person/anacaona
title: Anacaona
type: person
date: c. 1474 – 1504
themes: art, war, kinship
src: 1712e072f45c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anacaona (about 1474 to about 1504) was a Taíno cacica (chief) of Xaragua, a chiefdom in western Hispaniola, and a noted composer of ceremonial songs. She was executed by the Spanish governor Nicolás de Ovando after the killing of Taíno leaders in 1503.
--- fact 1
Anacaona was born about 1474; her birth year is approximate. She belonged to the ruling family of Xaragua, in present-day Haiti.
--- fact 2
She was the sister of the cacique Bohechío and the wife of Caonabo, a cacique whom the Spanish captured in 1494 or 1496, depending on the source.
--- fact 3
After Bohechío's death about 1500 she ruled Xaragua.
--- fact 4
Spanish sources describe her as a composer of areítos, songs that told the history of the Taíno and were performed at ceremonies.
--- fact 5
In 1503 Governor Nicolás de Ovando came to Xaragua with soldiers. Dozens of Taíno leaders were killed at a gathering, and she was taken to Santo Domingo and executed about 1504.
--- fact 6
Spanish accounts justified the killings by an alleged plot. The friar Bartolomé de las Casas said that such massacres were meant to instill fear.
--- fact 7
She is remembered in the Caribbean as a symbol of Indigenous resistance.
--- context
Anacaona's rule fell in the first years of Spanish colonization, which began the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] and reshaped the [[culture/taino|Taino]] world in the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Anacaona is known through Spanish writers, who describe both her songs and her killing, so the record keeps her as a poet largely because a hostile power wrote about her. A tradition of sung history carried a people's story-glue without writing and was recorded mainly by those who ended it. The reading of her as a poet would weaken if the earliest chroniclers say little of her songs and the image comes mostly from later writers.
--- question 1
Which details of her life come from the earliest Spanish sources and which from later writers?

=== person/andrea-palladio
title: Andrea Palladio
type: person
date: 1508 – 1580
themes: art
src: aee1c02300b8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Andrea Palladio (1508 to 1580) was a Renaissance architect from the Republic of Venice whose villas and churches, and an illustrated treatise, turned Roman classicism into a portable style. His influence is arguably the widest of any single architect in Western history.
--- fact 1
Palladio was born Andrea di Pietro della Gondola in Padua in 1508. He was apprenticed to a stonemasons' guild and moved to Vicenza to work as a carver.
--- fact 2
The nobleman Gian Giorgio Trissino gave him a classical education, renamed him "Palladio" after Pallas Athena, and took him to Rome to measure and draw ancient ruins.
--- fact 3
His 1549 remodelling of the medieval basilica of Vicenza made his name. For three decades he designed country villas for the Venetian nobility and churches in Venice.
--- fact 4
In 1570 he published I Quattro Libri dell'Architettura, part treatise and part illustrated catalogue of his buildings. He died in Vicenza in 1580.
--- fact 5
Key works include the Villa Rotonda (1567 to 1592), the Teatro Olimpico (1580 to 1585) and San Giorgio Maggiore in Venice (1566 to 1610).
--- fact 6
He worked out room proportions using simple musical ratios he believed produced harmony, and built in brick with stucco finished to imitate stone to keep costs down.
--- fact 7
The Four Books carried his style to England, where Inigo Jones and Lord Burlington built a Palladian movement, and to colonial America. Thomas Jefferson owned and annotated a copy and drew on it for Monticello and the rotunda of the University of Virginia.
--- fact 8
Vicenza's historic centre and the Palladian villas of the Veneto are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
--- context
Palladio worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]], in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a stonemason became an architect through a patron's gift of a classical education and a new name, and that a book carried his manner farther than his buildings could. Print detached a way of building from a place. In England and colonial America the style became a status-signal for landed families, a Roman past borrowed to dignify present ownership. The belief in musical ratios as a source of harmony was his own conviction, and it does not follow that viewers experience it that way.
--- question 1
How far did the Palladian movement follow his own buildings, and how far his book?

=== person/antoni-gaudi
title: Antoni Gaudí
type: person
date: 1852 – 1926
themes: art, religion
src: ef78d343966a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Antoni Gaudí (25 June 1852 to 10 June 1926) was the leading architect of Catalan Modernisme, the regional cousin of Art Nouveau. Almost all his mature work stands in Barcelona, and it culminates in the still-unfinished Sagrada Família.
--- fact 1
Gaudí was born in Reus, Catalonia, the youngest son of a coppersmith. He credited his father's workshop with teaching him to see three-dimensional space. He graduated from the Barcelona School of Architecture in 1878.
--- fact 2
The industrialist Eusebi Güell noticed a display case he designed for a glove-maker at the Paris World's Fair and became his patron for the rest of his career.
--- fact 3
He never married, grew increasingly devout, and by his sixties lived an almost monastic life devoted to the Sagrada Família.
--- fact 4
On 7 June 1926 he was struck by a tram while walking to church in shabby clothes. Mistaken for a beggar, he received delayed care and died on 10 June. He was buried in the crypt of his unfinished basilica.
--- fact 5
He modelled forms on nature and hung scale models of chains and sandbags, photographed upside down, to find the ideal shape for a compression-only arch. He revived trencadís, mosaic of broken tile, as a cheap weatherproof skin.
--- fact 6
Key works include Casa Vicens (1883 to 1885), Palau Güell (1886 to 1888), Park Güell (1900 to 1914), Casa Batlló (1904 to 1906) and Casa Milà (1906 to 1912).
--- fact 7
Modernist critics dismissed him after his death; Salvador Dalí and the Surrealists were early champions. Seven of his works are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Sagrada Família is funded by ticket sales and donations, and its main towers are targeted for completion around the centenary of his death.
--- context
Gaudí worked in Barcelona in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a builder who let gravity do the drawing: hanging chains, photographed inverted, turned a physical law into a design tool, so that the shape of the arch came from the world rather than from a pattern book. He gave his last decades to a building he knew he would not finish, which makes it a relay across generations, paid for by visitors and donors rather than a state treasury. The critics who dismissed him and the champions who later claimed him show how quickly a canon can reverse.
--- question 1
Which of his design intentions for the Sagrada Família are documented, and which are reconstructions by later builders?

=== person/artemisia-gentileschi
title: Artemisia Gentileschi
type: person
date: c. 1593 – 1656
themes: art
src: b0c8a3730f48
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Artemisia Gentileschi (1593 to about 1656) was an Italian Baroque painter, known for powerful heroines painted with Caravaggesque drama. She was the first woman admitted to Florence's Academy of the Arts of Drawing.
--- fact 1
Artemisia was born in Rome on 8 July 1593, the eldest child of the painter Orazio Gentileschi, a friend and follower of Caravaggio. She trained in his workshop and painted Susanna and the Elders by the age of 17.
--- fact 2
In 1611 she was raped by Agostino Tassi, a painter working with her father. At the seven-month trial in 1612 she was tortured with thumbscrews to test her testimony. Tassi was convicted but never served his sentence.
--- fact 3
She married the Florentine painter Pierantonio Stiattesi and moved to Florence, where she won the patronage of Grand Duke Cosimo II de' Medici, befriended Galileo Galilei and in 1616 was admitted to the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno. She learned to read and write in these years.
--- fact 4
From about 1630 she settled in Naples. About 1638 she travelled to London to join her father at the court of Charles I. She is thought to have died in Naples about 1656, possibly in the plague of that year.
--- fact 5
She specialised in strong women from the Bible and ancient history: Judith, Susanna, Jael, Cleopatra and Lucretia. She often lent her own features to saints and allegories.
--- fact 6
Key works include Susanna and the Elders (1610), Judith Slaying Holofernes (about 1620) and Self-Portrait as the Allegory of Painting (about 1638 to 1639).
--- fact 7
After her death many works were credited to her father or others. The art historian Roberto Longhi wrote about her in 1916, and from the 1970s feminist scholars, notably Mary D. Garrard, made her a central figure. The National Gallery, London, held an exhibition in 2020.
--- context
Artemisia worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], learning from [[person/caravaggio|Caravaggio]]'s example and following the way opened by [[person/sofonisba-anguissola|Sofonisba Anguissola]]. She befriended [[person/galileo-galilei|Galileo Galilei]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that for centuries the woman was known through a trial and a father rather than her paintings, so that a story of harm eclipsed the work. The court of 1612 tested a rape victim's testimony with torture, an instance of sanctioned harm used as a procedure for finding truth. Her recovery since the 1970s is itself a shift in story-glue: a later movement found in her a founding figure. Attribution of some works remains uncertain, so the portrait may still change.
--- question 1
Which works once credited to her father or others are now securely hers?

=== person/ashoka
title: Ashoka
type: person
date: c. 304 – 232 BCE
themes: religion, war, language
src: 9cdf72ee9b84
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ashoka (about 304 to 232 BCE) was a Mauryan emperor who ruled most of the Indian subcontinent and became a patron of Buddhism after a bloody war.
--- fact 1
He conquered the region of Kalinga around 261 BCE at great human cost; his own edicts record about 100,000 killed and 150,000 deported.
--- fact 2
He had edicts inscribed on rocks and pillars across his empire, promoting 'dhamma' (moral conduct).
--- fact 3
He supported the spread of Buddhism, including missions abroad.
--- fact 4
His lion capital is now a national emblem of India.
--- context
Ashoka ruled during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] and helped make [[person/siddhartha-gautama|Siddhartha Gautama]]'s teaching a regional and later global tradition.
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ashoka's edicts are an early case of a ruler publicly reflecting on the harm of war. Public inscriptions were a new way of speaking to a whole population at once.
--- question 1
How much of Ashoka's dhamma was policy and how much personal belief?
