=== event/second-italo-ethiopian-war
title: Second Italo-Ethiopian War
type: event
date: 1935 – 1936
themes: war, technology
src: d0f0e15fe0ab
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Second Italo-Ethiopian War (October 1935 to May 1936) was Italy's colonial invasion of Ethiopia, in which Italian forces used mustard gas, took the capital, and drove out Emperor Haile Selassie, while the League of Nations failed to stop them. Ethiopia was liberated in 1941.
--- fact 1
The war began on 3 October 1935, when General Emilio De Bono crossed the Mareb River into Ethiopia without a formal declaration of war. Emperor Haile Selassie I led the defence, and Benito Mussolini claimed victory in 1936.
--- fact 2
Italian forces took the capital, Addis Ababa, on 5 May 1936. Haile Selassie went into exile, first to Palestine and then to England.
--- fact 3
Italy used mustard gas in the war, in violation of the international agreements it had signed.
--- fact 4
Both Italy and Ethiopia were members of the League of Nations. The League failed to stop Italy or protect Ethiopia, and the episode is remembered as exposing its weakness.
--- fact 5
Italian rule was ended in 1941 by Ethiopian resistance fighters together with Allied forces.
--- context
The war reversed the outcome of the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]] of 1896, in which Ethiopia under [[person/menelik-ii|Menelik II]] had defeated an Italian army, and it stood apart from the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]] as a late colonial conquest. It is often seen as a prelude to [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]].
--- observer
The Observer records the use of chemical weapons against a state that had appealed to a body of nations for protection, and the failure of that body to act. A story-glue of collective security proved to be only as strong as the willingness of its members to bear a cost.
--- question 1
What was the number of Ethiopian civilians and soldiers killed by gas and by other means?
--- question 2
How much did Ethiopian resistance during the occupation contribute to the liberation of 1941, compared with Allied forces?

=== event/second-sino-japanese-war
title: Second Sino-Japanese War
type: event
date: 1937 – 1945
themes: war
src: fa08ae0d7e1f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Second Sino-Japanese War (1937 to 1945) was the full-scale war between Japan and China that began at the Marco Polo Bridge near Beijing and later merged into World War II. It included mass killings of civilians, among them at Nanjing, whose death toll is disputed.
--- fact 1
Full-scale war began on 7 July 1937 after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident near Beijing. It was an undeclared war that quickly grew into large-scale fighting in northern China.
--- fact 2
The incident hastened the formation of the Second United Front between the Kuomintang and the Chinese Communist Party.
--- fact 3
Japanese forces took Nanjing on 13 December 1937 and carried out mass killings there beginning that day. Estimates of the number of Chinese killed range from about 100,000 to more than 300,000. One scholarly assessment gives about 50,000 killed within the city walls in the first days and more than 100,000 but fewer than 200,000 in the city and surrounding counties by March 1938.
--- fact 4
The figures are contested. Some Japanese revisionists give much lower numbers, while some Chinese official figures are higher than the scholarly range.
--- context
The war followed Japan's industrialization and expansion after the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]] and merged into [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]] after 1941. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer records the mass killings at Nanjing as an atrocity, whatever the argument over the count. That the number itself has become a claim-line between governments and historians shows how a single figure can carry the weight of a whole nation's memory.
--- question 1
How should the Nanjing death toll be established, given records that are incomplete and sources that differ by nationality?
--- question 2
How many Chinese died in total in the war, including famine and disease?

=== event/siberian-traps-eruptions
title: Siberian Traps Eruptions
type: event
date: 252 – 251 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 2f6dde36e8aa
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Siberian Traps eruptions were one of the largest volcanic outpourings of the last 500 million years, in what is now northern Siberia, at about 252 to 251 million years ago. They coincide with the end-Permian mass extinction.
--- fact 1
The eruptions produced lava flows and intrusions over a huge area of northern Siberia, with estimates of the volume running from about one to several million cubic kilometres.
--- fact 2
Precise radiometric dating places the main phase within a window of about a million years or less that brackets the extinction, near 251.9 million years ago.
--- fact 3
Proposed killing mechanisms include large releases of carbon dioxide and methane that warmed the planet, ocean acidification and oxygen loss, and toxic gases from sulfur and halogens. Magma intruding coal, carbonate and salt-bearing rocks may have released large gas volumes.
--- fact 4
Marine and land records show a rapid warming of the tropics and oceans at the extinction; how much of it the eruptions caused is debated.
--- fact 5
The extinction is described in [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]].
--- context
The Siberian Traps Eruptions close the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]] and are the leading proposed cause of the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]], after which the [[era/triassic-period|Triassic Period]] opens the Mesozoic. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a volcanic episode lasting about a million years was enough to remove most of the life then living, and that comparisons with any modern process hold or fail on rates, not on totals. The Observer treats the timing question as central for that reason. The reading would weaken if the eruptions were shown to have started well before the extinction with little effect.
--- question 1
Which of the eruption's effects, from warming to toxic gases, did the most damage?
--- question 2
How closely does the eruption's timing match the extinction pulses?

mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Snowball Earth refers to glaciations in the late Proterozoic (the Sturtian and Marinoan, roughly 717 to 635 million years ago) in which ice may have reached the tropics.
--- fact 1
Glacial deposits formed near the paleo-equator support the hypothesis.
--- fact 2
The glacial deposits are capped by unusual carbonate rocks.
--- fact 3
Scientists debate whether the oceans froze completely or whether open water remained (a 'slushball').
--- fact 4
The Ediacaran biota appear soon after the last glaciation.
--- context
The glaciations come shortly before the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]] and may be linked to it. See [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Earth has gone through a nearly lifeless deep freeze and still recovered. Planetary systems can undergo extreme shifts and reset.
--- question 1
Was the ocean fully frozen?

=== event/spread-of-grasslands
title: Spread of Grasslands
type: event
date: 25 – 5 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 1bc890fce7dd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The spread of grasslands was the expansion of open grass-dominated vegetation across continents, from about 25 to 5 million years ago, in the Miocene. It changed animal life and set up the plant family that now supplies most of the world's staple crops.
--- fact 1
Grass pollen and microscopic plant silica are known from the latest Cretaceous or early Paleogene, but grasses were a minor part of vegetation for tens of millions of years.
--- fact 2
Open grasslands expanded from the early to middle Miocene, about 20 to 15 million years ago. Grasses using the C4 pathway, which suits hot, dry conditions and low carbon dioxide, spread widely about 8 to 6 million years ago, as shown by carbon isotopes in fossil soils and teeth.
--- fact 3
Proposed drivers include cooling and drying, seasonal rainfall, falling carbon dioxide and fire. Their relative roles are debated.
--- fact 4
Grazing mammals evolved high-crowned teeth and long limbs for running; horses shifted from browsing to grazing, and grazing antelope, cattle and their relatives diversified.
--- fact 5
The cereals, including wheat, rice and maize, are grasses; see [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]].
--- context
The Spread of Grasslands belongs mostly to the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and is a background to the story of the Miocene apes and early hominins. Its crop lineage is the source of [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]] and [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the staple foods of nearly every large human society come from a plant family that became widespread only in the last few tens of millions of years. Open landscapes are also the setting of a long-running hypothesis about human origins, which the Observer treats as a hypothesis, not a finding. It would weaken if early hominins proved to have lived mainly in closed woodland.
--- question 1
What mix of climate, carbon dioxide and fire drove the spread of C4 grasses?
--- question 2
How much did open grassland shape hominin evolution?

=== event/tanzimat-reforms

src: 1a9328cb39de
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Tanzimat (Reorganization) was a series of reforms in the Ottoman Empire between 1839 and 1876 that tried to modernize the state, promise equality before the law to all subjects, and move it toward constitutional government.
--- fact 1
The era ran from 1839 to 1876 under the sultans Abdülmecid I and Abdülaziz.
--- fact 2
The Edict of Gülhane, proclaimed on 3 November 1839, promised security of life, property, and honour for all subjects, fair taxation and conscription, public trials, and an end to tax farming.
--- fact 3
The Imperial Reform Edict of 18 February 1856 promised equality in education, government appointments, and justice regardless of creed.
--- fact 4
The period is said to end in 1876, when Abdulhamid II took the throne. The 1876 constitution created a parliamentary system but was suspended in 1878.
--- context
The Tanzimat was a response to European pressure and to earlier reformers in the empire. Compare [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]] in Japan and the reforms of [[person/muhammad-ali-of-egypt|Muhammad Ali of Egypt]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a state trying to change its own story-glue while the empire was still being defended, extending equality to subjects of every faith in the hope of holding a plural population together. Reforms promised on paper met the resistance of those with something to lose, and the constitution lasted only two years in practice.
--- question 1
How far were the Tanzimat promises of equality carried out in practice outside the capital?
--- question 2

mode: epic-fantasy

--- summary
The Agricultural Revolution was the gradual shift, beginning about 11,500 years ago, from foraging to farming and herding, which arose independently in several parts of the world.

The Observer calls this the Settling and treats it as the species' most consequential bargain: more food and more people in exchange for more work, more inequality, and more disease.

=== event/the-boring-billion
title: The Boring Billion
type: event
date: 1.8 Ga – 800 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: 0956f91b83eb
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Boring Billion is an informal name for the interval from about 1.8 to 0.8 billion years ago, a span with no well-established ice ages and little apparent change in the fossil record. Whether it was really uneventful is debated.
--- fact 1
The interval covers the [[era/mesoproterozoic-era|Mesoproterozoic Era]] and the edges of the eras on either side of it, and it lies between the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] and the ice ages of [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]].
--- fact 2
Chemical records suggest that atmospheric oxygen stayed well below today's level and that some waters at middle depth were rich in sulfide. Shortages of nutrients such as nitrogen and trace metals have been proposed as limits on life.
--- fact 3
Eukaryotes were present and diversifying slowly. The red alga Bangiomorpha, dated in different studies to between roughly 1.05 and 1.2 billion years ago, belongs to this interval.
--- fact 4
The supercontinent Nuna broke apart and Rodinia began to assemble.
--- fact 5
Some researchers argue that the name misleads, because records show fluctuations in oxygen and ecological change within the interval.
--- context
The Boring Billion lies within the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] and follows the [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]]. It ends with the changes of the Neoproterozoic, including the break-up of Rodinia and the glaciations of [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that "boring" is a verdict on the surviving record, not on the planet, and that a billion years is long enough for slow processes to matter. The reading would weaken if better records showed genuine stasis in oxygen, climate and eukaryote diversity across the whole interval.
--- question 1
Was the interval truly stable, or does a sparse record hide change?
--- question 2
What kept oxygen and complex life at low levels for so long?

=== event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon
title: Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon
type: event
date: 4.51 Ga
themes: earth-systems
src: c4ede0b43e4d
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
According to the giant-impact hypothesis, a Mars-sized body called Theia struck the young Earth about 4.5 billion years ago, and the debris formed the Moon.
--- fact 1
The hypothesis explains the Moon's relatively small iron core and its similarity to Earth's mantle in isotopes.
--- fact 2
Lunar rocks returned by the Apollo missions helped test the idea.
--- fact 3
The exact timing is uncertain, with estimates ranging from about 4.52 to 4.35 billion years ago: lunar zircons require the Moon to have formed within the first 60 million years of the Solar System, while the youngest estimates fall about 150 to 200 million years after it began.
--- fact 4
The Moon stabilizes Earth's axial tilt and produces most of Earth's tides.
--- context
The impact is an early event of the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]]. The Moon's stabilizing effect on climate is one reason Earth's long-term conditions stayed steady enough for complex life.
--- observer
The Observer sees a single accident with very long consequences. Human calendars, tides, and myths about the Moon all trace to a collision billions of years before any observer existed.
--- question 1
Was Theia a single impactor or were there several?

=== event/toba-eruption
title: Toba Eruption
type: event
date: c. 74 ka
themes: earth-systems
src: 2658da376265
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Toba eruption, about 74,000 years ago in Sumatra, was one of the largest volcanic eruptions of the past two million years, and its effects on human populations are debated.
--- fact 1
The eruption formed the caldera now occupied by Lake Toba in northern Sumatra, Indonesia.
--- fact 2
It ejected on the order of 2,800 to 3,000 cubic kilometers of material, and ash fell across South Asia and beyond.
--- fact 3
The Toba catastrophe hypothesis proposed that a volcanic winter reduced human populations to a small number of survivors.
--- fact 4
Archaeological sites in India, such as Jwalapuram, show human occupation continuing through the ash layer, which challenges the hypothesis.
--- fact 5
Most researchers now consider a severe global population bottleneck unlikely.
--- context
The eruption falls in the period when [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] were dispersing, near the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]]. It is often discussed with climate swings leading to the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. Toba Eruption sits in the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that people have long wanted a single catastrophe to explain a genetic feature of the species. The evidence so far suggests the population was more resilient than the story requires.
--- question 1
How large was the climatic cooling, and how long did it last?

=== event/tunguska-event
title: Tunguska Event
type: event
date: 1908
themes: earth-systems, science
src: 62dc174e368a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Tunguska event was an explosion in the atmosphere above the Podkamennaya Tunguska River in Siberia on 30 June 1908, which flattened about 2,000 square kilometers of forest.
--- fact 1
The blast is estimated at roughly ten to fifteen megatons of TNT equivalent.
--- fact 2
It occurred several kilometers above the ground and left no impact crater.
--- fact 3
About 80 million trees were knocked down over an area of about 2,000 square kilometers.
--- fact 4
The first scientific expedition to the site was led by Leonid Kulik in 1927.
--- fact 5
The cause is generally thought to be the airburst of a stony asteroid or a comet fragment tens of meters across.
--- context
The Tunguska event is the largest impact event in recorded history, a small-scale relative of the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]] and the [[event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon|Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon]]. It happened in a region newly reachable by the [[technology/trans-siberian-railway|Trans-Siberian Railway]]. Tunguska Event sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that an explosion greater than any nuclear device then in existence occurred in an empty forest and was noticed mostly by reindeer herders. Planetary hazards do not consult human population maps, and a few kilometers of difference in location would have changed the outcome.
--- question 1
Was the object an asteroid or a comet?

=== event/turkish-war-of-independence
title: Turkish War of Independence
type: event
date: 1919 – 1923
themes: war
src: 94dd17f6b339
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Turkish War of Independence (1919 to 1923) was fought by a nationalist movement under Mustafa Kemal against the Allied occupation of Anatolia and the invading armies of Greece and Armenia. It ended the Ottoman sultanate and established the Republic of Turkey.
--- fact 1
After World War I, Allied forces occupied parts of the Ottoman Empire, and Greece and Armenia moved to claim territory.
--- fact 2
Mustafa Kemal, later known as Atatürk, organized a nationalist movement centred at Ankara. He convened congresses at Erzurum and Sivas, and a national parliament met in Ankara on 23 April 1920. He was elected commander in chief and created a new army from remaining Ottoman forces and irregular units.
--- fact 3
On 1 November 1922 the new parliament abolished the sultanate, ending about 623 years of Ottoman rule.
--- fact 4
The Treaty of Lausanne of 24 July 1923 recognized Turkey's borders and sovereignty, and a population exchange between Greece and Turkey followed. The Republic of Turkey was proclaimed on 29 October 1923.
--- context
The war grew out of the defeat and breakup of the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] after [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and took place in the same region as the earlier [[event/armenian-genocide|Armenian Genocide]]. It belongs to the wider postwar reordering that includes [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] in later decades.
--- observer
The Observer notes that the same movement that fought off partition ended the dynasty in whose name the war began. An old legitimacy was set aside in three years, and a new one, built on a defined homeland, took its place.
--- question 1
How should the population exchange between Greece and Turkey be judged as an event that displaced very large numbers of people?
--- question 2
How did the new republic's founders treat the memory of the Ottoman period?

The Xinhai Revolution of 1911 to 1912 began with an army mutiny at Wuchang and ended the Qing dynasty, which had ruled China since 1644, replacing imperial rule with the Republic of China.

Sun Yat-sen led the republican movement but lacked the means to overthrow the Qing by himself. He reached a compromise with the powerful military leader Yuan Shikai, whose intervention led to the abdication of the child emperor Puyi on 12 February 1912.

The revolution ended the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]] after decades of internal rebellion and foreign pressure, including the [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]] and the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]]. The republic that followed was unstable, with power passing quickly to military leaders.
--- observer
The Observer notes a transfer of authority that was arranged by a compromise between a movement and an army, and not by the movement alone. A story-glue as old as the imperial title dissolved in a few months, but what replaced it had to be built while the guns were still in the hands of others.

=== event/younger-dryas
title: Younger Dryas
type: event
date: c. 10.9 ka – 9700 BCE
themes: earth-systems
src: 8e2c481c66ed
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Younger Dryas was a sudden return to near-glacial cold in the Northern Hemisphere that lasted from about 12,900 to 11,700 years ago, just before the start of the Holocene.
--- fact 1
It interrupted the warming that followed the last glacial period and ended abruptly, with several degrees of warming within decades.
--- fact 2
A leading explanation is a disruption of Atlantic Ocean circulation by a large pulse of glacial meltwater.
--- fact 3
A proposed extraterrestrial impact trigger, the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis, is disputed and not widely accepted.
--- fact 4
Its end is used to define the start of the Holocene, dated to about 11,700 years before 2000 CE.
--- fact 5
It overlaps the early stages of plant management in Southwest Asia, though the causal link is debated.
--- context
The Younger Dryas is the last major cold snap before the stable climate of the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]], within which [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] took place. It follows the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. Younger Dryas sits in the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the climate stability in which farming and cities arose was not inevitable. It was preceded by a violent flicker between cold and warm that lasted about a thousand years, and the Observer wonders how populations adapted to such swings within a few generations.
--- question 1
Did the cold snap influence the first cultivation of crops?

=== event/zanclean-flood
title: Zanclean Flood
type: event
date: c. 5.33 Ma
themes: earth-systems
src: fa300039c4d1
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Zanclean flood was the refilling of the Mediterranean Sea by Atlantic water through the Gibraltar region about 5.33 million years ago. It ended the Messinian salinity crisis and coincides with the start of the Pliocene.
--- fact 1
The Mediterranean had been cut off from the Atlantic and largely evaporated; estimates of how far the sea level had fallen range from hundreds of metres to more than a kilometre.
--- fact 2
Seismic images show a buried erosional channel running from the Gulf of Cádiz through the Strait of Gibraltar into the Alboran Sea, read as the path of the flood.
--- fact 3
A 2009 study modelled a catastrophic flood with a peak discharge on the order of a hundred million cubic metres per second, refilling the basin within months to a couple of years.
--- fact 4
Other studies propose a slower or two-stage refilling, and the speed of the flood is debated.
--- fact 5
The return of the Atlantic ended the isolation of the basin and restored marine life to it.
--- context
The Zanclean Flood ends the [[event/messinian-salinity-crisis|Messinian Salinity Crisis]] within the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] and falls at the start of the Pliocene. It belongs to the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one of the largest floods in Earth's history left no human witness and survives only as a buried channel and seismic images. The largest events in the record can be the ones least available to any memory. The reading would weaken if the refilling proved to have been slow and gradual.
--- question 1
Was the refilling a single catastrophic flood or a slower, staged process?
--- question 2
How dry did the Mediterranean become before the flood?
