--- summary
The domestic sheep was bred from the wild mouflon in the region from the northern Zagros Mountains to south-eastern Anatolia between about 9000 and 8500 BCE, first kept for meat, milk and skins, and only later selectively bred for wool, and it now numbers well over a billion animals worldwide.
--- fact 1
Sheep were domesticated from the mouflon, a wild sheep of southwestern Asia, in an estimated window between about 9000 and 8500 BCE, in the region from the northern Zagros Mountains to south-eastern Anatolia, and the practice had spread to Mehrgarh in the Indus Valley by about 7000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Early domestic sheep were kept mainly for meat, milk and skins rather than fleece; archaeological evidence, including statuary from Iran, suggests deliberate selection for woolly sheep began around 6000 BCE, some two to three thousand years after initial domestication, a shift sometimes called the secondary products revolution.
--- fact 3
The earliest known woven wool garments date to around 4000 BCE, and Neolithic farmers in what is now France, among the Castelnovien people, were keeping domestic sheep by about 6000 BCE.
--- fact 4
Sheep are raised in large numbers today across Australia, New Zealand, the southern regions of South America, South Africa and the British Isles, and China and India also hold very large flocks by absolute count; the world's domestic sheep population was estimated at around 1.2 billion in 2019.
--- context
Sheep were among the earliest animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] and, alongside crops such as wheat and barley, formed one pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent; they were commonly herded alongside the [[species/goat|Goat]], domesticated in a similar place and period. Sheep sit in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: that the gap of two to three thousand years between sheep domestication and deliberate wool breeding suggests early herders did not domesticate the animal with fleece production already in mind, but discovered and then bred for a secondary use only once the animal was already established in their kin-lattice, a pattern the Observer reads as evidence that a domesticated species' eventual economic role can differ substantially from the reason it was first tamed. The animal's spread across so much of the pastoral world since then shows a tool-lineage of herding techniques traveling with the species itself. The reading would weaken if evidence of early wool use is found that predates the currently accepted timeline for woolly-sheep breeding.
--- question 1
What use did early herders make of sheep in the two to three thousand years before wool breeding began, beyond meat, milk and skins?
--- question 2
How much did sheep domestication in the northern Zagros and south-eastern Anatolia influence, or draw on, the roughly contemporary domestication of goats in the same region?
