=== person/john-curtin
title: John Curtin
type: person
date: 1885 – 1945
themes: war, kinship
src: cfcdfcae5de4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
John Curtin (1885 to 1945) was Prime Minister of Australia from 1941 to 1945, leading the country through the Pacific war against Japan during the Second World War.
--- fact 1
A former journalist and trade unionist who had opposed conscription during the First World War, he became Labor Party leader in 1935 and prime minister in October 1941.
--- fact 2
After Japan entered the war in December 1941 and bombed Darwin in February 1942, he redirected Australian forces from the European theatre and, over British objections, recalled Australian troops from the Middle East to defend against Japanese advance.
--- fact 3
He committed Australia more closely to the United States for wartime defense, stating in December 1941 that Australia looked to America "free of any pangs" about its traditional ties to Britain.
--- fact 4
He introduced wartime economic controls and expanded the Australian government's role in social welfare, but died in office in July 1945, weeks before Japan's surrender, worn down by the strain of the war years.
--- context
John Curtin led Australia, in [[region/oceania|Oceania]], through the Pacific War during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Curtin's turn toward the United States marked a lasting realignment of Australian strategic dependence away from Britain, made under the pressure of direct attack rather than as a considered peacetime choice. That a leader who had opposed conscription in one war ended up directing total national mobilization in the next is a reminder that circumstance can override a leader's earlier convictions.
--- question 1
How much of Australia's postwar alignment with the United States was set irreversibly by Curtin's wartime decisions, versus later Cold War pressures?

=== person/jose-marti
title: José Martí
type: person
date: 1853 – 1895
themes: language, war, art
src: bf184c2890e0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
José Martí (1853 to 1895) was a Cuban poet, journalist and organizer who founded the Cuban Revolutionary Party in 1892 and helped launch the 1895 war of independence against Spain. He was killed in one of its first battles.
--- fact 1
Martí was born in Havana on 28 January 1853. Arrested at 16 over a letter criticizing a schoolmate who joined the Spanish army, he was sentenced to six years' hard labor and deported to Spain in 1871. He later lived in Mexico, Guatemala, Venezuela and, from 1880, mainly New York.
--- fact 2
He published Ismaelillo (1882), Versos sencillos (1891) and the essay "Nuestra América" (1891), which argued that Latin American countries should govern from knowledge of their own peoples.
--- fact 3
In 1892 he founded the Cuban Revolutionary Party and its newspaper Patria, arguing for a republic open to all races and against United States expansion into Cuba.
--- fact 4
He landed in eastern Cuba on 11 April 1895 and was killed at Dos Ríos on 19 May in his first engagement. Cuba became formally independent in 1902, under limits set by the United States.
--- fact 5
Both the Cuban government and Cuban exile groups have since claimed him as a symbol.
--- context
Martí worked in the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], in the last years of Spanish rule in the Americas, echoing [[person/simon-bolivar|Simón Bolívar]] and the [[event/latin-american-wars-of-independence|Latin American Wars of Independence]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a nation not yet in existence was first assembled as words: poems, newspaper articles and speeches among exiles. It was a story-glue made in mark-code before any claim-line had been drawn. A specialist could argue that his own plans fit the record less closely than the later statues do, which would weaken a reading of him as the origin of Cuban identity rather than one of its shapers.
--- question 1
Which positions later attributed to him are in his own writings?
--- question 2
What did he plan for the republic after independence?

=== person/jose-maria-velasco
title: José María Velasco
type: person
date: 1840 – 1912
themes: art, science
src: 337dd8d7a63c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
José María Velasco (6 July 1840 to 26 August 1912) was the most important Mexican painter of the 19th century, known for panoramic views of the Valley of Mexico. He was also a trained naturalist who illustrated scientific publications on botany, zoology and geology.
--- fact 1
Velasco was born in Temascalcingo, in the State of Mexico. His family moved to Mexico City in 1849. In 1858 he entered the Academy of San Carlos, where the Italian painter Eugenio Landesio taught him to observe nature closely and paint outdoors. He also studied botany, zoology, anatomy, physics and mathematics.
--- fact 2
In 1877 he succeeded Landesio as professor of landscape painting. Among his students was Diego Rivera.
--- fact 3
He worked as a scientific illustrator for the National Museum and on expeditions, and painted archaeological sites such as Teotihuacan.
--- fact 4
He won a medal at the Paris Universal Exposition of 1889 and exhibited at the 1893 Chicago World's Columbian Exposition. One view of the Valley of Mexico was given to Pope Leo XIII and is now in the Vatican Museums.
--- fact 5
He worked from outdoor sketches to large studio compositions. His panoramas set a detailed foreground of cacti and rocks against a luminous distance, and often include railways, bridges and haciendas alongside nature and ruins.
--- fact 6
Key works include The Valley of Mexico from the Santa Isabel Mountain Range (1875), The Pyramid of the Sun at Teotihuacan (1878) and The Curved Bridge over the Metlac Ravine (1881).
--- fact 7
He died in Mexico City during the early years of the Mexican Revolution. His views record lakes and open plains since swallowed by Mexico City. The Museo José María Velasco in Toluca opened in 1992.
--- context
Velasco worked in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], painting [[place/teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] and the valley around former [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]], and died during the [[event/mexican-revolution|Mexican Revolution]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a landscape painter who was also a trained naturalist, so that one picture served as an image of a nation and a catalogue of plants, rocks and light. After independence a country needed a visual identity, and a panorama of its valley supplied one, a story-glue in paint. Because the lakes and plains he recorded have been built over, the paintings have since become a record of a place that no longer exists, a reading he did not necessarily intend.
--- question 1
How much of his composed panoramas is observation, and how much idealisation?

=== person/jose-rizal
title: José Rizal
type: person
date: 1861 – 1896
themes: language, science
src: 289875cf6055
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
José Rizal (1861 to 1896) was a Filipino physician, writer and reformer whose novels criticized Spanish colonial rule. Spanish authorities executed him in Manila on 30 December 1896, and he is regarded as a national hero of the Philippines.
--- fact 1
Rizal was born on 19 June 1861 in Calamba, in the Philippines. He studied in Manila, Madrid, Paris and Heidelberg and became an eye doctor.
--- fact 2
He wrote the novels Noli Me Tángere (1887) and El Filibusterismo (1891), which portrayed abuses by Spanish friars and officials; the colonial authorities banned them.
--- fact 3
In 1892 he founded La Liga Filipina, a reform society, and was exiled to Dapitan on Mindanao, where he practiced medicine and taught until 1896.
--- fact 4
The Philippine Revolution began in August 1896. Rizal, who had volunteered as a doctor in Cuba, was arrested on his way there, brought back to Manila and convicted of rebellion. He had said he opposed an armed uprising at that time.
--- fact 5
He was shot by firing squad on 30 December 1896, aged 35. Before his death he wrote a poem now known as "Mi último adiós."
--- context
Rizal wrote in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] and Europe during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], as Spain's colony in the Philippines, tied to the world since the [[event/manila-galleon-trade|Manila Galleon Trade]], moved toward revolution. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Rizal used the novel, a foreign literary form, as a mark-code weapon: fiction gave readers a shared picture of abuses that formal reports could not. His execution turned a reformer into a symbol for revolutionaries who had taken a path he had not endorsed, showing how a story-glue for a new nation can be built from a person's death. The reading would weaken if fuller records showed he was closer to the revolutionaries than his statements suggest.
--- question 1
How close was his connection to the revolutionary movement that used his name?

=== person/joao-iii
title: João III
type: person
date: 1502 – 1557
themes: trade, religion
src: d1febebf476b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
João III (1502 to 1557) was King of Portugal from 1521 to 1557, presiding over the height of Portugal's Asian trading empire while also introducing the Inquisition and formal colonization of Brazil.
--- fact 1
Under his reign Portuguese trading posts and fortresses across the Indian Ocean, from East Africa to Goa, Malacca and Macau, reached their fullest extent, built on the routes opened by earlier explorers such as Vasco da Gama.
--- fact 2
He introduced the Portuguese Inquisition in 1536, targeting Jewish converts (New Christians) suspected of secretly practicing Judaism, at the urging of factions at his court and with eventual papal approval.
--- fact 3
He divided Brazil into hereditary captaincies from 1534 to encourage settlement and defense of the colony, a system that had mixed results and was later partly replaced by direct royal government.
--- fact 4
He was a patron of Portuguese scholarship, moving the university at Lisbon to Coimbra in 1537, but the enormous cost of maintaining the Asian trading empire strained the crown's finances by the end of his reign.
--- context
João III ruled Portugal in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], overseeing its trading empire and the colonization of Brazil. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that João III's reign shows a single government running two very different colonial projects at once, a trade-post empire in Asia built on existing networks and a settlement colony in Brazil built from the ground up, each with different costs and consequences that later history would treat very differently.
--- question 1
How much did the financial strain of maintaining the Asian trading posts contribute to Portugal's later loss of influence there to the Dutch and English?

=== person/kamehameha-i
title: Kamehameha I
type: person
date: c. 1750 – 1819
themes: war, trade
src: c8cc2ae63f6f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kamehameha I (about 1750 to 1819) was a chief of Hawaiʻi Island who united the Hawaiian Islands under one rule by 1810, founding the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi, whose last monarch was Liliuokalani.
--- fact 1
Kamehameha was born at Kohala on Hawaiʻi Island. Estimates of his birth year range from 1736 to 1761. He died on 8 May 1819, though some sources say 14 May.
--- fact 2
After his uncle, the ruler Kalaniʻōpuʻu, died in 1782, he became guardian of the war god Kūkaʻilimoku and defeated his cousin Kīwalaʻō at the Battle of Mokuʻōhai, gaining Hawaiʻi Island after years of war.
--- fact 3
He used Western firearms and cannon and advisers such as John Young and Isaac Davis. In 1795 he invaded Oʻahu, and at Nuʻuanu hundreds of defenders were driven over the Pali cliff.
--- fact 4
In 1810 Kauaʻi and Niʻihau came under his authority by negotiation with their ruler Kaumualiʻi, who stayed on as a tributary king.
--- fact 5
The Law of the Splintered Paddle (Māmalahoe Kānāwai), protecting noncombatants, is said to come from a 1782 incident in which fishermen struck him with a paddle.
--- fact 6
His son Liholiho succeeded him as Kamehameha II. His burial place was hidden by custom and is unknown.
--- context
Kamehameha I lived in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] among the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]], whose voyagers had settled Hawaiʻi centuries earlier in the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. The kingdom he founded ended with [[person/liliuokalani|Liliuokalani]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a chief built one state by joining an old priestly and war lineage to imported gunpowder, choosing tribute over killing the last rival ruler. His hidden grave was a grief-rite that also protected his bones from rivals. A specialist could argue the unification also depended on Western trade and on divisions among his opponents, which would weaken a reading of the outcome as his design alone.
--- question 1
When was he born, and how did his age shape his own accounts of his rise?
--- question 2
How much of the 1810 unification was negotiation, and how much the threat of force?

=== person/kate-sheppard
title: Kate Sheppard
type: person
date: 1848 – 1934
themes: kinship, religion
src: 178b66e288f7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kate Sheppard (1848 to 1934) was an English-born campaigner who led the movement for women's suffrage in New Zealand. The Electoral Act of 1893 made New Zealand the first self-governing country in which women voted in national elections.
--- fact 1
Sheppard was born on 10 March 1848 in Liverpool, England, and emigrated to New Zealand, arriving in 1869. She died in Christchurch on 13 July 1934.
--- fact 2
From 1887 she was national superintendent for franchise and legislation of the Women's Christian Temperance Union, which made votes for women a central aim.
--- fact 3
Petitions to Parliament carried about 10,000 signatures in 1891, about 20,000 in 1892 and 31,872 in 1893, the largest petition presented to Parliament until then.
--- fact 4
The Governor signed the Electoral Act on 19 September 1893, and women voted for the first time in the election of 28 November 1893.
--- fact 5
The Act gave the vote to women of all races, including Māori women in the Māori electorates, but women could not stand for Parliament until 1919.
--- fact 6
Her portrait has been on the New Zealand ten-dollar note since 1991.
--- context
Kate Sheppard worked in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], as part of the international [[event/women-s-suffrage-movements|Women's Suffrage Movements]]. See [[culture/maori|Maori]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a claim-line around who counted as a citizen was moved by counting, a temperance network turning shared conviction into signatures a legislature could tally. The line moved in steps, with the vote won in 1893 and the right to stand only in 1919. A specialist could argue the vote came from many organizers and sympathetic politicians besides her, which would weaken a reading that centers one person.
--- question 1
How much of the 1893 result owed to her leadership, and how much to other organizers?
--- question 2
How did Māori women take part in the campaign?

=== person/katsushika-hokusai
title: Katsushika Hokusai
type: person
date: 1760 – 1849
themes: art, trade
src: 502d3d0ec86b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Katsushika Hokusai (1760 to 1849) was a Japanese ukiyo-e artist from Edo, creator of The Great Wave off Kanagawa. He produced paintings, book illustrations and, in his seventies, the landscape print series that made him world-famous.
--- fact 1
Hokusai was born in 1760 in the Katsushika district of Edo (modern Tokyo). As a teenager he worked in a lending library and trained as a woodblock carver. About 18 he entered the studio of Katsukawa Shunshō.
--- fact 2
He changed his artistic name more than 30 times and is said to have moved house more than 90 times. He lived in poverty for much of his life. His daughter Katsushika Ōi became a fine painter and assisted him in his old age.
--- fact 3
In 1814 he began publishing the Hokusai Manga, fifteen volumes of sketches. About 1830 to 1832 he designed Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, which includes The Great Wave off Kanagawa (about 1831).
--- fact 4
In the postscript to One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji (1834) he wrote that nothing he drew before age 70 was worth noticing. He died in Edo on 10 May 1849, at 88, reportedly saying that with five more years he could have become a true painter.
--- fact 5
Ukiyo-e was a collaborative art: he drew the design, specialist carvers cut it into blocks, one for each colour, and printers pressed the paper by hand. The Fuji series used the newly imported Prussian blue pigment.
--- fact 6
He learned Western perspective from Dutch prints available in Japan and combined it with Japanese flat colour and strong design.
--- fact 7
After Japan opened to trade in the 1850s he became a sensation in Europe. Monet, Degas, Van Gogh and Cassatt learned from him, and Debussy put The Great Wave on the cover of La Mer. The 1,000-yen note issued in 2024 carries the wave, and the Sumida Hokusai Museum opened in Tokyo in 2016.
--- context
Hokusai worked in Edo under the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the famous print is a workshop's output, a designer's drawing multiplied by carvers and printers into thousands of impressions, so the "artwork" is a process rather than a single object. Its blue arrived by exchange-web, and its European fame came by the same webs decades later. The stories of 30 names, 90 houses and a deathbed remark present an artist who narrated his own maturation, and part of that narrative is reported rather than documented.
--- question 1
How large was Katsushika Ōi's contribution to his late work?

=== person/khadija-bint-khuwaylid
title: Khadija bint Khuwaylid
type: person
date: c. 555 – 619 CE
themes: trade, religion, kinship
src: f7f1afc72a64
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Khadija bint Khuwaylid (about 555 to 619) was a merchant of Mecca and the first wife of Muhammad. Her dates and many details come from Muslim biographies written more than a century after her death.
--- fact 1
Khadija belonged to the Quraysh tribe of Mecca and, according to tradition, financed trading caravans to Syria and hired agents to lead them.
--- fact 2
Tradition says Muhammad led one of her caravans and that she proposed marriage, about 595. Accounts differ on her age then, and a common figure is 40.
--- fact 3
Muslim tradition holds that she was the first person to accept Muhammad's message after his first revelation, about 610.
--- fact 4
Tradition names four daughters, including Fatima, and sons who died young.
--- fact 5
She died about 619, shortly before Muhammad's move to Medina in 622, a year remembered as the Year of Sorrow.
--- fact 6
The earliest biographies, by Ibn Ishaq and Ibn Saʿd, were written in the 8th and 9th centuries.
--- context
Khadija's life falls before the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] and is closely tied to [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] in the [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]] of the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that early Muslim biographers, writing more than a century later, describe a woman who held capital, employed a man, proposed to him, and then funded and comforted the first movement around him. It is hard to say whether the record preserves her role or shapes it to serve later ideals. Descent through her daughter Fatima became a status-signal for many later families, so her kin-lattice carried weight long after her death. The reading would weaken if the tradition simplified her marriages and ages.
--- question 1
How much of the early account of her wealth and trade is independently supported?

=== person/knud-rasmussen
title: Knud Rasmussen
type: person
date: 1879 – 1933
themes: science, language, trade
src: afadfadf3008
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Knud Rasmussen (1879 to 1933) was a Danish-Greenlandic explorer and ethnographer who recorded Inuit oral traditions and led the Fifth Thule Expedition (1921 to 1924), a dog-sled journey from Greenland across Arctic Canada to Alaska.
--- fact 1
Knud Rasmussen was born on 7 June 1879 in Jakobshavn (Ilulissat), Greenland, to a Danish missionary father and a mother of part Greenlandic Inuit descent, and grew up speaking Greenlandic.
--- fact 2
In 1910 he and Peter Freuchen founded the Thule Trading Station at Cape York in northwest Greenland, which helped fund his expeditions.
--- fact 3
On the Fifth Thule Expedition he traveled with Greenlandic Inuit companions across northern Canada to Alaska. The expedition's archaeologist, Therkel Mathiassen, excavated sites that led to the identification of the Thule culture.
--- fact 4
He collected songs, stories and accounts of belief from the Iglulik and other Inuit groups, and wrote *Across Arctic America* (1927).
--- fact 5
He fell ill on the Seventh Thule Expedition and died in Copenhagen on 21 December 1933.
--- context
Rasmussen's work covered the peoples described in [[culture/inuit-and-the-thule-expansion|Inuit and the Thule Expansion]], across Arctic coasts near [[place/beringia|Beringia]], in [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Rasmussen stood between two kin-lattices, Danish and Greenlandic, and that speaking Greenlandic from childhood let him record songs and accounts an outside visitor might have missed. His route followed a chain of related Inuit communities, a reminder that a language and kin network can run across lines drawn by states. His records depend on Danish trade and expedition funding, so they reflect his interests and informants as well as the communities. Inuit scholars could argue that Inuit-produced accounts deserve more weight than his volumes, which would weaken any reading that treats them as the standard record.
--- question 1
How do Inuit accounts of the same events compare with his versions?

=== person/kristina-of-sweden
title: Kristina of Sweden
type: person
date: 1626 – 1689
themes: religion, science, art
src: 8c70c89fa75d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kristina (1626 to 1689) was Queen of Sweden from 1632 to 1654, known for her patronage of scholars and the arts, her unexpected abdication, and her secret conversion to Catholicism in a firmly Protestant kingdom.
--- fact 1
She became queen at age six after her father Gustavus Adolphus died in battle, ruling under a regency until she assumed full power in 1644.
--- fact 2
She invited scholars including Rene Descartes to her court in Stockholm; Descartes died there in 1650, reportedly worn down by the early-morning hours she kept for their lessons.
--- fact 3
She abdicated the throne in 1654 in favor of her cousin, having secretly converted to Catholicism, a decision scandalous in Lutheran Sweden that she only announced publicly after leaving the country.
--- fact 4
She spent most of her later life in Rome under the pope's protection, becoming a prominent patron of music and art, and was one of very few women buried in St. Peter's Basilica.
--- context
Kristina ruled Sweden in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] before her abdication and conversion. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kristina gave up one of Europe's more secure thrones for a religious conviction she could not act on while she held it, a rare case of a monarch choosing personal belief over political power outright rather than being forced from office by rivals or war.
--- question 1
How much of her abdication was driven by religious conviction alone, versus a broader discomfort with the constraints of Swedish court life and expectations of marriage?

=== person/kupe
title: Kupe
type: person
date: c. 900 CE – 1100
themes: trade, kinship
src: 682ca5464ed3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kupe is a legendary Polynesian navigator credited in Maori oral tradition with being the first to reach and name Aotearoa (New Zealand); no fixed historical date can be assigned to him.
--- fact 1
Traditions describe Kupe sailing from the ancestral homeland Hawaiki, in some versions pursuing the giant octopus Te Wheke-a-Muturangi, and reaching the North Island of New Zealand.
--- fact 2
He is credited with naming numerous coastal landmarks, several of which are still used today, and with returning to Hawaiki afterward to describe the land he had found.
--- fact 3
Different iwi (tribal) traditions place him at varying points, and 19th- and 20th-century European writers synthesized these into a single traditional date of roughly 925 to 1000 CE that many historians now treat with caution.
--- fact 4
No archaeological or written record fixes Kupe's voyage; the earliest confirmed archaeological evidence for human settlement of New Zealand dates to around the 13th century CE, later than most traditional accounts imply.
--- context
Kupe belongs to the oral traditions of the [[culture/maori|Maori]], part of the wider spread of the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]] across [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kupe functions less as a single dateable person than as a way of organizing a founding story: giving named ancestors, named canoes and named landmarks to a settlement process that archaeology now dates centuries later than the traditional accounts suggest. That gap does not make the tradition false, but it means the story and the settlement record answer different questions.
--- question 1
How much of the Kupe tradition reflects a single voyage rather than a composite of several early journeys?

=== person/lady-six-sky
title: Lady Six Sky
type: person
date: c. 682 – 741 CE
themes: war, kinship, religion
src: c2dffc867089
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Lady Six Sky (Wak Chanil Ajaw; active 682 to 741 CE) was a Maya noblewoman from Dos Pilas who arrived at Naranjo in 682 and effectively ruled the city for decades, restoring it after a period of defeat.
--- fact 1
She was a daughter of the Dos Pilas king B'alaj Chan K'awiil, part of a network of the powerful city of Calakmul's allies against its rival Tikal.
--- fact 2
Naranjo's royal line had been broken after a defeat by Caracol, and Lady Six Sky was installed there in 682, apparently to re-establish the dynasty.
--- fact 3
Monuments record her overseeing military campaigns and presiding over her young son K'ahk' Tiliw Chan Chaahk's accession in 693, after which she continued to appear on inscriptions as a ruling figure into the 700s.
--- fact 4
She never took the full royal title used by male kings of Naranjo, and continued to use the emblem glyph of her home city Dos Pilas throughout her life, so her exact formal status is debated by epigraphers.
--- context
Lady Six Sky's career took place among the [[culture/maya|Maya]] city-states of [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], within the wider rivalry between Calakmul and Tikal for regional dominance. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Lady Six Sky held real power at Naranjo for decades while never claiming the title that would have named her a king in her own right, a gap between practice and title that Maya inscriptions do not explain. Reading her as a ruling queen assumes that authority and title track together; the monuments suggest they did not always.
--- question 1
Did Lady Six Sky rule in her own right, or as an unusually powerful regent for her son?

=== person/laozi
title: Laozi
type: person
date: c. 550 BCE
themes: religion, language
src: 38d0ae03d70a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Laozi (Lao Tzu, "Old Master") is the traditional author of the Daodejing and a founding figure of Daoism, said to have lived in the 6th century BCE. Whether he was a single historical person is disputed.
--- fact 1
Laozi is traditionally placed in the 6th century BCE, in the time of Confucius. The date is uncertain and no date of death is known.
--- fact 2
The historian Sima Qian, writing about 100 BCE, identifies him as Li Er, keeper of archives at the Zhou court, and also lists other men who might have been meant, so the uncertainty was already old.
--- fact 3
Sima Qian's account says he met Confucius, then left the Zhou domain to the west and wrote the text at the Hangu Pass at the request of the gatekeeper Yin Xi. This account is traditional.
--- fact 4
The Daodejing has about 5,000 Chinese characters in 81 short chapters. Its earliest excavated versions, from Guodian (about 300 BCE) and Mawangdui (tomb sealed 168 BCE), fit the view of most scholars that the text took shape in the 4th to 3rd centuries BCE.
--- fact 5
Later religious Daoism honored him as a deity, Laojun, and the Tang dynasty's imperial family, named Li, claimed him as an ancestor.
--- context
Laozi is a figure of the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]], often paired with [[person/confucius|Confucius]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a book was attached to a person, and the person to a place and a story, a pattern in which the text arrives first and the biography follows. Whether one man wrote it matters less to its history than what later people did with the figure: the Tang emperors used descent from him as a story-glue for their claim to rule. A single well-dated author would weaken this reading, while evidence of several hands over generations would strengthen it.
--- question 1
Was there a single author, or was the Daodejing assembled over generations?

=== person/lautaro
title: Lautaro
type: person
date: c. 1534 – 1557
themes: war
src: 850eed3f7bc4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Lautaro (about 1534 to 1557) was a Mapuche toqui, or war leader, who organized effective resistance to Spanish conquest in what is now southern Chile, after years spent as a captured servant in the Spanish camp.
--- fact 1
As a young captive, he served the Spanish conquistador Pedro de Valdivia and observed Spanish tactics, horsemanship and weapons before escaping back to his people.
--- fact 2
He introduced cavalry tactics, relay messenger systems and coordinated ambushes to Mapuche warfare, adapting methods he had learned from the Spanish.
--- fact 3
In December 1553 his forces defeated a Spanish column at the Battle of Tucapel and captured and killed Valdivia, a major blow to the early Spanish conquest of Chile.
--- fact 4
He was killed in a surprise Spanish attack on his camp at the Mataquito River in April 1557, ending a campaign that had held Spanish expansion south of the Bio-Bio River in check for years.
--- context
Lautaro led [[culture/mapuche|Mapuche]] resistance in the [[region/andes|Andes]] region during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], part of the broader pattern of Indigenous resistance to Spanish colonization in the Americas. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Lautaro's effectiveness came directly from his captivity: the same Spanish camp that used him as forced labor also taught him, inadvertently, how to defeat Spanish armies. The Mapuche remained unconquered by Spain for the rest of the colonial period, a rare outcome in the Americas that his early campaigns helped establish.
--- question 1
How much of Lautaro's tactical innovation was his own, versus already circulating among Mapuche leaders resisting the Spanish?

=== person/leo-tolstoy
title: Leo Tolstoy
type: person
date: 1828 – 1910
themes: art, language, religion
src: af4bf57df2fb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Leo Tolstoy (1828 to 1910) was a Russian novelist, author of the realist epics War and Peace and Anna Karenina. A religious crisis in the second half of his life turned him toward shorter fiction and writing on faith, mortality and nonviolence that influenced Gandhi.
--- fact 1
Tolstoy was born in 1828 into an aristocratic family at Yasnaya Polyana, the estate south of Moscow he inherited and managed for most of his life. He served briefly as an army officer in the Crimean War.
--- fact 2
In the 1860s and 1870s he wrote War and Peace (1869) and Anna Karenina (1877), and founded a school for the children on his estate.
--- fact 3
After Anna Karenina he underwent a spiritual crisis, described in A Confession. He rejected the Russian Orthodox Church, embraced an ascetic, pacifist reading of Christian ethics and increasingly disowned his earlier fiction as morally frivolous.
--- fact 4
His later works include The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886), The Kreutzer Sonata (1889) and Resurrection (1899), shorter, starker and more didactic than the novels.
--- fact 5
War and Peace argues in long digressions that the forces driving historical events lay far beyond the control of any single general or emperor.
--- fact 6
His writing on nonviolent resistance to evil directly influenced Mahatma Gandhi, who corresponded with him near the end of his life.
--- fact 7
He died in 1910 of pneumonia at a remote railway station, after collapsing on a flight from his estate. Yasnaya Polyana is preserved as a museum.
--- context
Tolstoy wrote in the Russian Empire, in [[region/europe|Europe]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and his ethics passed through [[person/mahatma-gandhi|Mahatma Gandhi]] into later traditions of nonviolence. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a writer who argued that history is driven by forces beyond any general or emperor, then in later life disowned his own fiction and preached nonviolence, so that one person tested two accounts of what moves people. The line from Tolstoy to Gandhi is a meaning-engine's ethics passing from hand to hand by letter, and from there into political movements. The flight from the estate and the death at a railway station make an ending that invites retelling, so the account of his last days deserves the same care as any other late-life narrative.
--- question 1
How much of his later disavowal of his own novels was reasoned, and how much a crisis?

=== person/leonardo-da-vinci
title: Leonardo da Vinci
type: person
date: 1452 – 1519
themes: art, science
src: ba103999b6c5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Leonardo da Vinci (15 April 1452 to 2 May 1519) was an Italian painter, engineer and anatomist. He left few finished paintings, with counts differing but fewer than 25 attributed major works, some of them unfinished, including the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper, and thousands of notebook pages of studies.
--- fact 1
Leonardo was born on 15 April 1452 near Vinci in Tuscany, the illegitimate son of a notary, Ser Piero, and a young woman named Caterina. He was apprenticed in Florence to Andrea del Verrocchio.
--- fact 2
About 1482 he moved to Milan. In a letter to Duke Ludovico Sforza he stressed his skills as a military engineer before his art. In Milan he painted The Last Supper.
--- fact 3
After the French invaded Milan in 1499 he moved between Mantua, Venice and Florence, worked briefly as a military engineer for Cesare Borgia, and in Florence began the Mona Lisa.
--- fact 4
In 1516 King Francis I invited him to France and gave him the manor of Clos Lucé near Amboise. He died there on 2 May 1519. His pupil Francesco Melzi inherited his paintings, tools, library and personal effects, and by tradition his notebooks.
--- fact 5
His notebooks hold thousands of pages of drawings and mirror-writing, with studies of anatomy, flight, hydraulics and optics. He dissected corpses to understand muscles and bones.
--- fact 6
He pioneered sfumato, soft modelling without hard outlines. Key works include the Virgin of the Rocks (about 1483 to 1493, sources differ), Lady with an Ermine (about 1489 to 1491), Vitruvian Man (about 1485 to 1490, sources differ), The Last Supper (about 1495 to 1498) and the Mona Lisa (about 1503 to 1519).
--- fact 7
His experiments with materials sometimes failed: The Last Supper began to decay within decades.
--- fact 8
The Codex Leicester was bought by Bill Gates in 1994. In 2017 the Salvator Mundi, attributed to Leonardo, sold for $450 million, a record for a painting; its attribution remains disputed.
--- context
Leonardo worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes one individual who made pictures, studied anatomy and flight, and recorded it in mirror-writing that mostly stayed in notebooks. Because the notebooks were scattered after his death, his pattern-taming reached his contemporaries through paintings rather than publication, and only later did the notes show how far it went. His present fame also rests on status-signal: a price of $450 million for a disputed attribution measures a market as much as a picture.
--- question 1
Is the Salvator Mundi by Leonardo, and how much of it?

=== person/li-qingzhao
title: Li Qingzhao
type: person
date: c. 1084 – 1155
themes: art, language
src: c6a5baab01e4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Li Qingzhao (1084 to about 1155) was a Chinese poet of the Song dynasty, widely regarded as the greatest woman writer in the classical Chinese tradition. Her ci, a lyric verse form, moved from a settled, cultured life to loss and exile after northern China fell to invaders.
--- fact 1
She was born in 1084 in Jinan, Shandong, to a literary family, and is thought to have died about 1155.
--- fact 2
In 1101 she married Zhao Mingcheng, a scholar who shared her interest in antiquities. Together they compiled the Jinshi lu, a catalog of bronze and stone inscriptions.
--- fact 3
When the Jin dynasty invaded northern China in 1127, the couple fled south with part of their collection, much of which was lost along the way. Zhao Mingcheng died in 1129.
--- fact 4
After his death she wrote an afterword to their Jinshi lu describing their marriage and their losses. She briefly remarried around 1132 and divorced amid scandal soon after.
--- fact 5
Her poems, mostly short ci in personal, direct language, describe love, separation, and later grief and displacement. Fewer than 100 of her poems survive, out of a larger body assembled and praised by later editors.
--- context
Li Qingzhao lived in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], within the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]], whose north fell to Jin invasion in 1127. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that her verse recorded an ordinary domestic partnership, book-collecting and companionship, that a state's collapse turned into flight and loss, and that this turn from ease to grief became the reason later readers kept her work in mark-code. A specialist could argue that fewer than a hundred surviving poems, out of a much larger and partly lost body, cannot support firm claims about how her style changed over her life, which would weaken any confident before-and-after reading.
--- question 1
How much of her attributed work is authentic, given how much was lost after 1127?
--- question 2
What became of her after her brief second marriage?

=== person/liliuokalani
title: Liliuokalani
type: person
date: 1838 – 1917
themes: art, trade, kinship
src: 35c626e2ec66
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Liliuokalani (1838 to 1917) was the last monarch of the Kingdom of Hawaii, reigning from 1891 until she was deposed in January 1893 by a group of residents backed by United States troops.
--- fact 1
Liliuokalani was born in Honolulu on 2 September 1838 and died there on 11 November 1917.
--- fact 2
She succeeded her brother Kalākaua on 29 January 1891 and was the kingdom's only reigning queen.
--- fact 3
In January 1893 she moved to issue a constitution restoring royal powers reduced by the 1887 "Bayonet Constitution."
--- fact 4
On 17 January 1893 a Committee of Safety of mostly American and European residents overthrew her while United States Marines were ashore. She yielded to the United States government to avoid bloodshed and asked it to reinstate her.
--- fact 5
After a failed royalist uprising she was arrested and signed an abdication in January 1895.
--- fact 6
Native Hawaiians submitted mass petitions against annexation in 1897, and the United States annexed Hawaii in 1898.
--- fact 7
She composed songs, including "Aloha ʻOe," and published a memoir, *Hawaii's Story by Hawaii's Queen*, in 1898.
--- fact 8
In 1993 the United States Congress apologized to Native Hawaiians for the overthrow, carried out with the participation of agents and citizens of the United States.
--- context
Liliuokalani ruled a Native Hawaiian state in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Native Hawaiians descend from the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]]; see [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Liliuokalani defended her kingdom's claim-line with the instruments her opponents also used: a written constitution, diplomatic appeals, and a published memoir. Her songs carried attachment to place and people in a form that outlasted the polity. The 1993 apology shows a claim-line redrawn by force being named as wrongful without being redrawn back. The reading of her as the main carrier of the resistance would weaken if the 1897 petitions show it rested chiefly on Native Hawaiian organizations.
--- question 1
How much of the resistance to annexation came from Native Hawaiian organizations rather than from the queen?

=== person/louis-comfort-tiffany
title: Louis Comfort Tiffany
type: person
date: 1848 – 1933
themes: art, technology
src: db1fb0766d38
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Louis Comfort Tiffany (1848 to 1933) was an American designer whose iridescent Favrile glass, stained-glass windows and lamps became the signature look of American Art Nouveau. Recent scholarship credits many of his studio's best-known designs to staff designers, above all Clara Driscoll.
--- fact 1
Tiffany was born in New York in 1848, the son of the jeweller Charles Lewis Tiffany. He trained as a painter under George Inness before turning to interior design and decorative arts in the 1870s.
--- fact 2
His firm redecorated state rooms in the White House in 1882 for President Chester Arthur; the work was later removed. In 1885 he founded what became Tiffany Studios, and in 1894 he patented Favrile glass.
--- fact 3
His Byzantine-style chapel for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago won international acclaim. He built Laurelton Hall on Long Island as a showcase of his work.
--- fact 4
Favrile glass builds iridescent, metallic colour into molten glass through mineral oxides. "Drapery glass" was manipulated while hot to form folds like fabric.
--- fact 5
Financial losses during the Depression forced the studio's closure. He died in New York in 1933.
--- fact 6
Much of the detailed design of individual pieces, especially the lamps, was done by staff designers, above all Clara Driscoll, who led the women's glass-cutting department.
--- fact 7
For most of the 20th century Tiffany was credited as sole designer. The mid-2000s rediscovery of Driscoll's letters credited her and the women of the "Tiffany Girls" department with several famous lamps, including the Dragonfly, a correction now generally accepted by museums and scholars.
--- context
Tiffany worked in [[region/north-america|North America]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. [[person/christopher-dresser|Christopher Dresser]] travelled to Japan in 1876 and 1877 partly on commission from Tiffany & Co., the jewellery firm founded by Tiffany's father. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a name on the product credited one man for the work of a studio, and that the correction came from letters found a century later. The name worked as a brand, a status-signal that gathered credit upward, while the women who cut the glass and designed much of the work were recorded only in a side channel. The pattern of a maker's name absorbing a workshop is old. This case is unusually well documented because the letters survive.
--- question 1
Which other famous Tiffany Studios pieces were designed by staff rather than by Tiffany?
