=== person/peter-the-great
title: Peter the Great
type: person
date: 1672 – 1725
themes: technology, war, trade
src: 79a1071a8955
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Peter I, called Peter the Great (1672 to 1725), ruled Russia from 1682, jointly at first and alone from 1696, until 1725, driving a sweeping program of westernizing reform and transforming Russia into a major European power.
--- fact 1
He traveled incognito through Western Europe in 1697 to 1698 on the "Grand Embassy," working for a time as a shipwright in the Netherlands to study European shipbuilding and technology firsthand.
--- fact 2
He fought a long war against Sweden, the Great Northern War from 1700 to 1721, winning a decisive victory at the Battle of Poltava in 1709 and gaining territory on the Baltic Sea that made Russia a major naval power.
--- fact 3
He founded the city of St. Petersburg in 1703 on newly won Baltic territory, building it as a planned European-style capital and moving the seat of government there from Moscow.
--- fact 4
He reorganized the Russian government, military, and church administration along Western European lines, and required Russian nobles to adopt Western dress, in a well-known instance personally cutting off boyars' traditional long beards to symbolize the change.
--- context
Peter the Great ruled Russia in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], reorienting the country toward Western European models. See [[theme/technology|Technology]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Peter's reforms were imposed from the top down, often coercively, on a nobility and church that had not asked for Westernization, a pattern that produced fast institutional change but left the underlying tension between imposed modernization and existing tradition unresolved for later Russian history to work through.
--- question 1
How much of Peter's Westernizing program reshaped everyday life beyond the court and nobility, versus remaining a project of the elite?

=== person/philip-ii
title: Philip II
type: person
date: 1527 – 1598
themes: religion, war, trade
src: f9191eef11b0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Philip II (1527 to 1598) was King of Spain from 1556 to 1598, ruling an empire that spanned the Iberian Peninsula, the Netherlands, parts of Italy, and vast territories in the Americas and the Philippines, which were named after him.
--- fact 1
He inherited Spain, its Italian territories and the Spanish Americas from his father, Charles V, in 1556, and briefly held the English throne jointly through his marriage to Mary I of England from 1554 to 1558.
--- fact 2
He directed a long, costly war to suppress a revolt in the Spanish Netherlands, beginning in 1566, that continued in various phases for decades and was never fully resolved during his reign.
--- fact 3
He sent the Spanish Armada against England in 1588 to support a planned invasion and end English support for Dutch rebels and privateering against Spanish shipping; storms and English naval tactics destroyed much of the fleet.
--- fact 4
He presided over Spain's peak territorial extent while also driving the crown into multiple bankruptcies, in 1557, 1560, 1575 and 1596, from the cost of near-constant warfare funded partly by American silver.
--- context
Philip II ruled Spain in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], directing wars in the Netherlands and against England. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Philip II's reign shows how a flow of American silver, often assumed to guarantee power, instead financed a level of continuous warfare that bankrupted the crown multiple times; wealth extracted from the Americas did not translate into lasting Spanish military dominance in Europe.
--- question 1
How much did the repeated bankruptcies of Philip's reign weaken Spain's long-term capacity to compete with rising powers such as England and the Dutch Republic?

=== person/pieter-bruegel-the-elder
title: Pieter Bruegel the Elder
type: person
date: c. 1525 – 1569
themes: art
src: ab795cbe8b50
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Pieter Bruegel the Elder (about 1525 to 1569) was the most important Netherlandish painter of the 16th century. He made peasants, proverbs and the turning seasons his subjects, in crowded panels and sweeping landscapes. Only about 40 of his paintings survive.
--- fact 1
Bruegel was probably born between 1525 and 1530, perhaps near Breda in the Duchy of Brabant. In 1551 he became a master in the painters' guild of Antwerp.
--- fact 2
In 1552 he travelled to Italy. His drawings of the Alps on the return journey fed the mountain views of his later paintings.
--- fact 3
In Antwerp he designed prints for the publisher Hieronymus Cock, some in the manner of Hieronymus Bosch. In 1563 he married Mayken Coecke and moved to Brussels, where he painted most of his masterpieces.
--- fact 4
He died in Brussels in September 1569. His sons Pieter Brueghel the Younger and Jan Brueghel the Elder became successful painters and founded a dynasty.
--- fact 5
Key works include Netherlandish Proverbs (1559), Children's Games (1560), The Tower of Babel (1563), The Hunters in the Snow (1565) and The Peasant Wedding (1567).
--- fact 6
He often used a high viewpoint over dozens of small figures, and built pictures around proverbs, games or Bible stories retold in Flemish dress.
--- fact 7
He painted during the religious conflicts and Spanish repression that led to the Dutch Revolt. Many scholars see hidden commentary on these events in his work.
--- fact 8
According to the early biographer Karel van Mander, he and a friend dressed as peasants to join village weddings and fairs. The Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna holds the world's largest group of his paintings.
--- context
Bruegel worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]], in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], and built on the crowded moral landscapes of [[person/hieronymus-bosch|Hieronymus Bosch]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter who made ordinary people the subject, in scenes built from proverbs and games that a viewer was meant to decode. Such pictures keep records of festival, work and kin-lattice that the written record of the age leaves out. Scholars who find hidden commentary on the religious conflicts of his time are reading a silence, and the story of the artist in peasant dress rests on one early biographer, so both are best held loosely.
--- question 1
Did his pictures carry coded commentary on the Spanish repression, or is that a later reading?

=== person/piye
title: Piye
type: person
date: c. 744 – 714 BCE
themes: war, religion
src: 5ae87554e91b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Piye (reigned about 744 to 714 BCE) was a king of Kush who conquered Egypt and is usually counted as the first ruler of Egypt's Twenty-fifth (Kushite) Dynasty. His birth year is unknown; the dates given here are those of his reign.
--- fact 1
Piye ruled Kush from Napata, near Jebel Barkal in present-day Sudan. His reign is dated about 744 to 714 BCE; some scholars count his father Kashta as the dynasty's first ruler.
--- fact 2
About 728 BCE he marched north against Tefnakht, prince of Sais, and a coalition of Delta rulers, and defeated them.
--- fact 3
His Victory Stele, found at Jebel Barkal in 1862 and now in Cairo, records the campaign in Egyptian hieroglyphs, presents him as serving the god Amun, and lists the rulers who submitted to him.
--- fact 4
He did not remain in Egypt; he returned to Kush and left local rulers in place.
--- fact 5
He expanded the temple of Amun at Jebel Barkal.
--- fact 6
He was buried in a pyramid at El-Kurru, with horses. He was succeeded by Shebitku, whom sources describe as either his son or his brother; recent evidence places Shabaka's reign after Shebitku's, not before.
--- context
Piye ruled the [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]] and took control of the land of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] in the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]; his successors later fought the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler from the southern edge of the Egyptian world claimed its center by appealing to the center's own gods, script and burial forms, a case of ancestor-weight working for an outsider. His stele is a royal victory text, so its portrait of a pious and merciful king may be more status-signal than record. The reading of his rule as a conquest would weaken if other evidence showed Kushite influence in Upper Egypt was already established before his campaign.
--- question 1
How far did Kushite influence in Upper Egypt predate his campaign?

observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Qin Shi Huang (259 to 210 BCE) was the first emperor of a unified China, who standardized writing, currency, and measures and built a large tomb complex including the terracotta army.
--- fact 1
He became king of Qin at thirteen and completed the unification of China in 221 BCE.
--- fact 2
He standardized the script, weights and measures, and axle widths.
--- fact 3
Later historians, hostile to the Qin, described book burning and harsh punishments; some of these accounts are debated.
--- fact 4
His tomb complex near Xi'an contains thousands of life-size clay soldiers.
--- context
See [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]]. His state was inherited and continued by the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how a ruler tried to make a huge population legible: one script, one currency, one set of weights. Standardization is a technology of control and also of trade.
--- question 1
How reliable are the traditional accounts of his rule?

=== person/queen-amina-of-zazzau
title: Queen Amina of Zazzau
type: person
date: c. 1576 – 1610
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: f25257d673ca
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Queen Amina of Zazzau is a traditional figure said to have ruled Zazzau (Zaria), a Hausa state in present-day northern Nigeria, for about 34 years around the turn of the 16th and 17th centuries. She is known from later tradition, and her dates and status are disputed.
--- fact 1
Some sources give Amina's reign as 1576 to 1610; her birth and death years are uncertain (some accounts give 1533 and 1610), and the dates here are those of the reign. Whether she held the title of queen is disputed, and she does not appear on the regnal lists. Some scholars instead place her in the early to mid fifteenth century, because the Kano Chronicle calls her a contemporary of Muhammad Dauda, who ruled from 1421 to 1438.
--- fact 2
Much of what is known comes from the Kano Chronicle, a late 19th-century compilation of older oral tradition, and from Muhammad Bello's history Infaq al-Maysur (dated to 1812 or to about 1836, sources differ), written more than two centuries later.
--- fact 3
Tradition says she led Zazzau's army, expanded its territory, and increased its wealth through control of trade routes.
--- fact 4
Earthen walls around Hausa towns are traditionally attributed to her.
--- fact 5
Scholars generally accept that she existed, but the details of her life come from tradition.
--- fact 6
She is remembered in northern Nigeria as a warrior queen.
--- context
Amina's story belongs to the Hausa states of [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], which the [[culture/sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]] later absorbed in the early 19th century. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Amina is known mainly from records written two centuries or more after her supposed reign, by a scholar-ruler of the state that later absorbed her city. Her absence from the regnal lists alongside her fame in tradition suggests that a community's memory may gather several rulers' deeds under one name, though it may equally mean the lists are incomplete. The reading would weaken if a source from her own time gave a different account of who ruled Zazzau then.
--- question 1
Which of the deeds attributed to her can be tied to a source from her own period?

=== person/queen-suriyothai
title: Queen Suriyothai
type: person
date: c. 1548
themes: war, kinship
src: 0f6f7ed78a92
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Queen Suriyothai was a consort of King Maha Chakkraphat of the Ayutthaya kingdom, in present-day Thailand, traditionally said to have died in 1548 defending her husband on elephant-back during a Burmese invasion. Her birth year is unrecorded, and her story rests on Siamese chronicles alone.
--- fact 1
She was a consort of Maha Chakkraphat, who became king of Ayutthaya in 1548.
--- fact 2
Later that year the Burmese king Tabinshwehti invaded Ayutthaya. Siamese chronicles say Suriyothai, disguised and riding an elephant, placed herself between her husband and an enemy commander and was killed by his blow.
--- fact 3
No Burmese chronicle records this episode, and historians debate whether the woman who died was Suriyothai herself or a princess in her retinue.
--- fact 4
A memorial stupa, the Chedi Si Suriyothai, stands near Ayutthaya on the traditional site of her death.
--- fact 5
Thai nationalist commemoration of her grew in the twentieth century, including a widely seen 2001 film.
--- context
Queen Suriyothai's story is set in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] at the start of the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], in the [[culture/ayutthaya-kingdom|Ayutthaya Kingdom]] during its wars with Burma. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a death recorded only by the side that claims a battlefield can still outlast the state that recorded it, becoming a grief-rite performed anew by each generation that builds or visits her memorial. A national retelling three centuries later shows ancestor-weight added long after the fact. A specialist could argue that the story conflates an unnamed retainer's death with the queen's, which would weaken any reading of Suriyothai as a documented individual rather than a chronicle figure later fixed by nationalist memory.
--- question 1
Was the woman who died on the elephant actually the queen, or a member of her retinue?
--- question 2
How much of the standard story was shaped by twentieth-century nationalism rather than the original chronicles?

=== person/queen-victoria
title: Queen Victoria
type: person
date: 1819 – 1901
themes: technology, kinship, trade
src: 8ce4d4f52be1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Victoria (1819 to 1901) was Queen of the United Kingdom from 1837 to 1901, the longest-reigning British monarch until Elizabeth II, presiding over a period of industrial growth and imperial expansion later named after her.
--- fact 1
She became queen at eighteen after the death of her uncle William IV, and married her cousin Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha in 1840, with whom she had nine children who married into royal families across Europe.
--- fact 2
Her reign saw Britain's industrial economy expand rapidly and its colonial empire grow substantially, and she was proclaimed Empress of India in 1876.
--- fact 3
After Albert's death in 1861 she withdrew from public life for years, a long seclusion that made her briefly unpopular before she gradually resumed public duties.
--- fact 4
Constitutional practice during her reign shifted royal power further toward a largely ceremonial role exercised through elected ministers, even as the monarchy's cultural and symbolic prominence grew.
--- context
Victoria reigned over the United Kingdom in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], a period of rapid industrial and imperial growth. See [[theme/technology|Technology]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Victoria's name became attached to an entire era of technological and imperial change she did not personally drive, since real political power had already shifted substantially to elected ministers well before her reign began; the "Victorian" label describes a society more than a ruler's own decisions.
--- question 1
How much personal political influence did Victoria retain in practice, behind the era's growing constitutional convention of royal neutrality?

=== person/rachel-carson

type: person

src: fbb91dfe787a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rachel Carson (1907 to 1964) was an American marine biologist and writer whose book 'Silent Spring' (1962) helped launch the modern environmental movement.
--- fact 1
Silent Spring documented the harm that pesticides such as DDT caused to birds and ecosystems.
--- fact 2
The book prompted government action; the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency was created in 1970 and DDT was banned for most uses in the U.S. in 1972.
--- fact 3
Chemical industry groups attacked her work.
--- fact 4
She died of breast cancer in 1964.
--- context
Carson wrote during the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]], as human effects on ecosystems grew. Her ideas foreshadowed public concern over [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a single book led a species to notice a side effect of its own success. Carson made one of the first widely read arguments that human actions have planetary consequences.
--- question 1

=== person/raja-ravi-varma
title: Raja Ravi Varma
type: person
date: 1848 – 1906
themes: art, religion, technology
src: 892511393b65
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Raja Ravi Varma (29 April 1848 to 2 October 1906) was the most famous Indian painter of the 19th century, from the princely state of Travancore. He brought European academic oil painting to scenes from the Hindu epics, and his mass-produced prints put images of gods and heroines into homes across India.
--- fact 1
Ravi Varma was born at Kilimanoor, near Thiruvananthapuram, into an aristocratic family related to the ruling house. His uncle, the painter Raja Raja Varma, noticed his talent. At 14 he was taken to the palace under Maharaja Ayilyam Thirunal and learned oil painting partly by watching the European portraitist Theodore Jensen.
--- fact 2
A portrait won a prize at the 1873 Vienna exhibition, and his work was shown at the 1893 Chicago World's Columbian Exposition. He painted a series of Puranic subjects for Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III of Baroda in 1888 to 1890.
--- fact 3
In 1894 he set up a lithographic press in Bombay, later moved to Malavli near Lonavala, producing colour prints (oleographs) of deities and heroines that sold in huge numbers. Critics accused him of vulgarising sacred subjects.
--- fact 4
In 1904 the British Viceroy awarded him the Kaisar-i-Hind Gold Medal. He died at Kilimanoor.
--- fact 5
He combined Victorian academic realism with Indian subjects and costumes. His heroines were based on real women and staged at dramatic moments.
--- fact 6
The early filmmaker Dadasaheb Phalke worked at his press before making India's first feature film in 1913. Later nationalist artists of the Bengal School, such as Abanindranath Tagore, rejected his Western style.
--- fact 7
The Kerala government's highest art award, the Raja Ravi Varma Puraskaram, bears his name.
--- context
Ravi Varma worked in princely Travancore and beyond in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] under the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]], in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a press, not a temple, gave many people their picture of the gods. Lithography and oil painting together gave a meaning-engine a standard face, so that a shared image of Rama or Shakuntala travelled by tool-lineage rather than by ritual. The critics who called him a vulgariser and the later nationalists who rejected his Western manner show the same prints read as democratising and as foreign. Both readings remain live, and neither is settled by the pictures themselves.
--- question 1
How far did his prints standardise how the gods were pictured in later calendar art, film and comics?

=== person/ramesses-ii
title: Ramesses II
type: person
date: c. 1303 – 1213 BCE
themes: war, art, religion
src: dcd9d4792f08
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ramesses II (about 1303 to 1213 BCE), also called Ramesses the Great, was the third pharaoh of Egypt's Nineteenth Dynasty. He ruled for about 66 years, fought the Hittites at Kadesh, made one of the earliest surviving peace treaties, and built temples across Egypt and Nubia.
--- fact 1
Ramesses II came to the throne in 1279 BCE by the most widely used chronology and reigned until 1213 BCE. Other chronologies differ by several years.
--- fact 2
About 1274 BCE he fought the Hittite king Muwatalli II at Kadesh, in present-day Syria. Both sides claimed victory. Ramesses had it carved on temple walls, though most historians think the Hittites gained more.
--- fact 3
About 1259 BCE he made a treaty with the Hittite king Hattusili III. It survives in Egyptian and Hittite versions, each saying the other side asked for peace first.
--- fact 4
He built the capital Pi-Ramesses, the Ramesseum and the temples at Abu Simbel, moved in the 1960s to escape the Aswan High Dam reservoir. Many monuments carry his name over those of earlier kings.
--- fact 5
He is estimated to have had more than 90 children and to have lived to about 90. His thirteenth son, Merneptah, succeeded him.
--- fact 6
Later tradition links him to the pharaoh of the Exodus. Scholars dispute this, and no Egyptian source records such an event.
--- context
Ramesses II ruled in the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]. See [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]], [[culture/hittites|Hittites]], [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a battle with no clear winner was carved on several temples as a triumph, while the treaty that ended the rivalry survives in two versions that each say the other side asked first. Building on a huge scale, and carving his name over predecessors', worked as status-signal and story-glue. A specialist could argue that royal inscriptions followed conventions expected of any pharaoh, which would weaken a reading of monumentality as personal ambition.
--- question 1
How did Kadesh look from the Hittite side, given that most Hittite records are fragmentary?
--- question 2
Which monuments bearing his name did he build, and which did he rework?

=== person/razia-sultana
title: Razia Sultana
type: person
date: c. 1205 – 1240
themes: war, kinship
src: e923082f58a7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Razia Sultana (about 1205 to 1240) ruled the Delhi Sultanate from 1236 to 1240, the only woman to hold the throne in its history. She was the daughter of Iltutmish, who by tradition had named her his successor.
--- fact 1
Razia was a daughter of the sultan Iltutmish, who reigned from 1211 to 1236. Tradition holds that he named her his heir over his sons, though he seems to have chosen Ruknuddin Firuz shortly before his death.
--- fact 2
After Iltutmish died in 1236 her half-brother Ruknuddin Firuz took the throne but was deposed within months, and Razia became sultan with the support of the people of Delhi and some nobles.
--- fact 3
According to chroniclers she held court in public and led armies; coins were issued in her name.
--- fact 4
She appointed Jamal ud-Din Yaqut, an Abyssinian former slave, master of the stables, a promotion Turkic nobles resented.
--- fact 5
In 1240 Malik Altunia, the governor of Bhatinda, rebelled. She was imprisoned at Bhatinda and then married him in September 1240; they were defeated at Kaithal and killed in October 1240, and accounts differ on the details.
--- fact 6
The main contemporary account is by the chronicler Minhaj al-Siraj Juzjani, who praised her abilities but wrote that her sex counted against her.
--- context
Razia ruled the [[culture/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the complaints recorded against Razia concern who held high posts and who displayed the status-signals of rule, more than how she governed. Her only detailed account comes from a court chronicler who admired her and still described her sex as a defect. A specialist could argue that court histories overstated gender as the cause, since a quarrel between nobles and a strong sultan would look the same in the record.
--- question 1
How much of the opposition to her was about her sex, and how much about court factions?

=== person/rembrandt-van-rijn
title: Rembrandt van Rijn
type: person
date: 1606 – 1669
themes: art
src: a1560da0b051
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rembrandt Harmenszoon van Rijn (15 July 1606 to 4 October 1669) was the greatest artist of the Dutch Golden Age, a painter, etcher and draughtsman. His roughly 80 self-portraits form an extraordinary autobiography, from early fame and wealth to bankruptcy and loss.
--- fact 1
Rembrandt was born in Leiden, the ninth child of a prosperous miller. He trained locally and for six months in Amsterdam with Pieter Lastman. About 1631 he moved to Amsterdam.
--- fact 2
The Anatomy Lesson of Dr Nicolaes Tulp (1632) made him the city's most sought-after portraitist. In 1634 he married Saskia van Uylenburgh. Three of their children died in infancy; only Titus, born 1641, survived. Saskia died in 1642, the year he completed The Night Watch.
--- fact 3
Debts led to bankruptcy in 1656, and his house and collection were sold. He kept working. His partner Hendrickje Stoffels died in 1663 and Titus in 1668.
--- fact 4
He died on 4 October 1669 and was buried in an unmarked grave in the Westerkerk.
--- fact 5
He used chiaroscuro to direct attention and mood. His late paintings are built from thick, rough layers applied with brush, palette knife and even fingers.
--- fact 6
As a printmaker he combined etching with drypoint and varied each impression. Key works include The Hundred Guilder Print (about 1647 to 1649) and The Return of the Prodigal Son (about 1661 to 1669).
--- fact 7
He ran a large workshop. Since 1968 the Rembrandt Research Project has reattributed many paintings. Goya named him one of his three masters.
--- context
Rembrandt worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], in Amsterdam, then a booming commercial capital and home port of the [[event/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a market of merchants, guilds and civic companies that paid for group portraits, while the same painter's late work found far fewer buyers, yet he kept working through bankruptcy and bereavement. The self-portraits are memory-outsourcing of a private face on a scale unusual for the age. Because he ran a large workshop, the line between his hand and his pupils' is a scholarly judgment that has moved since 1968, so the "Rembrandt" of any decade is partly a decision.
--- question 1
Which paintings once credited to Rembrandt belong to his pupils, and which reattributions will hold?

=== person/robert-the-bruce
title: Robert the Bruce
type: person
date: 1274 – 1329
themes: war, kinship
src: 8f7dd2b3590f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Robert the Bruce (1274 to 1329) was King of Scotland from 1306 to 1329, leading Scottish resistance to English rule and securing recognition of Scottish independence shortly before his death.
--- fact 1
He claimed the Scottish throne in 1306 after killing a rival claimant, John Comyn, in a church at Dumfries, an act that led to his excommunication by the pope.
--- fact 2
Early in his reign he suffered several defeats and periods as a fugitive, including, according to a later popular legend, drawing perseverance from watching a spider repeatedly try to build its web.
--- fact 3
He decisively defeated a much larger English army under Edward II at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314, the most significant Scottish military victory of the wars of independence.
--- fact 4
The Treaty of Edinburgh-Northampton in 1328 saw England formally recognize Scottish independence and Robert's kingship, a year before his death in 1329, reportedly from leprosy.
--- context
Robert the Bruce led Scotland, in [[region/europe|Europe]], during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] wars of independence against England. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Robert's path to the throne began with a killing in a church, not a conventional succession, and that his later reputation as Scotland's founding hero sits somewhat uneasily beside that origin; national founding stories often smooth over the more compromised beginnings of the figures they celebrate.
--- question 1
How much of the spider legend and similar popular stories about Robert's perseverance date to his own lifetime versus later national mythmaking?

=== person/rumi
title: Rumi
type: person
date: 1207 – 1273
themes: religion, language, art
src: 0e3591ed9601
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rumi (Jalal ad-Din Muhammad Rumi, 1207 to 1273) was a Persian-language poet and Sufi teacher who spent his adult life in Konya, in Anatolia. His long poem, the Masnavi, and his lyric poems are among the most widely read works of Persian literature.
--- fact 1
Rumi was born in 1207 in Balkh or nearby Wakhsh, in present-day Afghanistan or Tajikistan; sources differ. His family moved west, and he was living in Konya, in the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum, by about 1228.
--- fact 2
He trained as a scholar and preacher like his father, Baha ad-Din Walad, and taught in Konya.
--- fact 3
In 1244 he met a wandering mystic, Shams of Tabriz. Their friendship changed his life, and he began to write poetry. Shams disappeared about 1248, and accounts differ, with some saying he was killed and others that he simply moved on.
--- fact 4
He wrote the Masnavi, a long poem in six books, and thousands of lyric poems collected in the Divan of Shams. He wrote mostly in Persian.
--- fact 5
The Masnavi runs to some 25,000 to 27,000 couplets, with counts differing by source, composed over his last decade or so at the request of his disciple Husam al-Din Chalabi, who wrote the verses down as Rumi recited them. Some later readers called it "the Quran in Persian".
--- fact 6
Fihi Ma Fihi ("In It What Is In It") is a prose collection of his informal talks to disciples, valued as the clearest record of how he taught.
--- fact 7
He died in Konya on 17 December 1273. His followers founded the Mevlevi order, known for a ritual of whirling.
--- fact 8
Loose English adaptations from the late 20th century onward made him, by some measures, one of the best-selling poets in the United States.
--- context
Rumi's family left the east in the years of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], and he lived in [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a personal loss became the source of a body of verse that a community then turned into ritual, the whirling of the Mevlevi order, and a lineage. It shows one relationship carried across centuries by both sound-code and practice. A specialist could argue that later followers shaped the account of Shams and of Rumi's life, which would weaken any reading that treats the biography as settled.
--- question 1
How much of the standard biography comes from accounts written by his followers after his death?

=== person/sacagawea
title: Sacagawea
type: person
date: c. 1788 – 1812
themes: language, trade
src: cd6b0934d591
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sacagawea (about 1788 to 1812, or by another account 1884) was a young woman of disputed Shoshone or Hidatsa birth who traveled with the Lewis and Clark expedition from 1804 to 1806 as an interpreter, and later became one of the most commemorated women in the United States.
--- fact 1
Sacagawea was born about 1788, by tradition among the Lemhi Shoshone in present-day Idaho, though some recent work argues she was born Hidatsa.
--- fact 2
About 1800 she was taken by a Hidatsa raiding party. As a teenager she married the trader Toussaint Charbonneau, and her son Jean Baptiste was born on 11 February 1805.
--- fact 3
Charbonneau and Sacagawea joined the Lewis and Clark expedition at Fort Mandan in 1804. In August 1805 she met her brother Cameahwait, a Shoshone leader, whose horses the party needed to cross the Rockies.
--- fact 4
Historians describe her chiefly as an interpreter and a sign of peaceful intent, more than a guide, though she gave directions in a few places.
--- fact 5
A fur-trade clerk recorded in 1812 that Charbonneau's wife had died, but Shoshone oral tradition holds she lived until 1884 on the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming. Historians disagree.
--- fact 6
Her name is spelled Sacagawea, Sacajawea and Sakakawea, the last from Hidatsa and favored by specialists.
--- context
Sacagawea lived in [[region/north-america|North America]] on the eve of the westward expansion that led to events such as [[event/indian-removal-and-the-trail-of-tears|Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a teenage mother with little say over her own life became one of the most commemorated women in the country, while the record was written almost entirely by the men she traveled with. Later suffragists made her story-glue for women's independence, a use she left no writing to confirm. A specialist could argue that neither her origin nor her death can be settled from the present documents, which would weaken any confident telling of her life.
--- question 1
Was the woman who died in 1812 the same person as the woman buried in 1884?
--- question 2
What did she think of the journey, given no account in her own words survives?

=== person/saladin
title: Saladin
type: person
date: c. 1137 – 1193
themes: war, religion, kinship
src: 260fbf01b46b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Saladin (Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, about 1137 to 1193) was a Kurdish-born Muslim commander who founded the Ayyubid dynasty, ruled Egypt and much of Syria, and recaptured Jerusalem from the Crusaders in 1187.
--- fact 1
Saladin was born about 1137 in Tikrit, in present-day Iraq, to a Kurdish family, and died in Damascus on 4 March 1193.
--- fact 2
He rose in the service of the Zengid ruler Nur ad-Din and became vizier of Fatimid Egypt in 1169.
--- fact 3
In 1171 he ended the Fatimid Caliphate and restored Egypt's allegiance to the Abbasid caliph; his family, the Ayyubids, went on to rule Egypt and Syria.
--- fact 4
After Nur ad-Din died in 1174 he took Damascus and brought much of Syria and northern Mesopotamia under his rule.
--- fact 5
On 4 July 1187 his army destroyed a Crusader army at the Battle of Hattin. Jerusalem surrendered on 2 October 1187, and its Latin inhabitants could ransom themselves, though many who could not pay were enslaved.
--- fact 6
In the Third Crusade he fought Richard I of England. The Treaty of Ramla (September 1192) left the Crusaders a coastal strip and allowed Christian pilgrims to visit Jerusalem.
--- context
Saladin's wars took place in [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]; see [[event/crusades|Crusades]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Jerusalem, sacred to three traditions, was taken by force in 1099 and again in 1187, with very different treatment of the defeated, and that Saladin's reputation for restraint rests largely on his own court biographers and on Crusader writers who admired it. The war against the Crusaders also served as a story-glue that let him unite rival Muslim rulers under his command. A specialist could argue that the terms of 1187 reflect a negotiated surrender rather than his character, which would weaken the reading of restraint as a personal trait.
--- question 1
How much of his reputation for restraint comes from his own court biographers?

=== person/sappho
title: Sappho
type: person
date: c. 630 – 570 BCE
themes: art, language
src: 11bb1105ca3a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sappho (about 630 to about 570 BCE) was a Greek lyric poet from the island of Lesbos whose songs of love, longing and ritual were collected in at least eight books, possibly nine, in antiquity. Only fragments survive, plus one complete poem.
--- fact 1
Sappho was born about 630 BCE on Lesbos in the eastern Aegean, in Mytilene or Eresos according to ancient sources; her dates are approximate.
--- fact 2
She composed songs in Aeolic Greek to be sung with a lyre, and the Sapphic stanza is named after her.
--- fact 3
Ancient scholars gathered her poems in at least eight books, possibly nine. Only fragments survive, apart from one complete poem, a prayer to Aphrodite (Fragment 1).
--- fact 4
New papyrus fragments published in 2004 and 2014 added lines, including a poem that mentions her brothers.
--- fact 5
Many surviving poems express desire for women. Scholars debate whether her circle on Lesbos was a religious association, a school for girls, or a social group.
--- fact 6
Ancient sources say she spent time in exile in Sicily and had a daughter, Cleis. The biography is largely unreliable.
--- fact 7
The words "sapphic" and "lesbian" derive from her name and her island.
--- context
Sappho wrote in Greek, in the [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]], among the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]] of the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Sappho was copied for centuries and then largely lost, so the poet known today is a set of quotations and scraps, some recovered on papyri from Egyptian sites. Most claims about her life come from writers who lived centuries later and read her poems as autobiography. Any portrait therefore reflects what survived and what later readers wanted from a story-glue about desire and grief. New papyri could change the picture, so the reading should be held loosely.
--- question 1
What was the purpose of the circle of women around her?

=== person/savitribai-phule
title: Savitribai Phule
type: person
date: 1831 – 1897
themes: kinship, language
src: edccd29bd521
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Savitribai Phule (1831 to 1897) was an Indian educator, poet and social reformer in Maharashtra who, with her husband Jyotirao Phule, opened one of the first schools for girls in Pune in 1848. She died of plague in 1897 after nursing patients.
--- fact 1
Savitribai Phule was born on 3 January 1831 in Naigaon, Maharashtra, and married Jyotirao Phule in 1840 as a child. He taught her to read.
--- fact 2
In 1848 the couple opened a school for girls in Pune. She is often called India's first woman teacher; earlier girls' schools existed, so the "first" claim is contested, and historians also credit Fatima Sheikh as a co-worker.
--- fact 3
Accounts say she was harassed with stones and dung on her way to teach.
--- fact 4
She and Jyotirao later opened further schools, including for children of lower castes, and in 1863 they founded a home for the prevention of infanticide.
--- fact 5
She published a book of poetry, *Kavya Phule*, in 1854.
--- fact 6
She died on 10 March 1897 in Pune after catching plague while nursing patients during an outbreak.
--- context
Savitribai Phule worked in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] under the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], when schooling and caste were contested subjects. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that in her setting, literacy in the mark-code was itself a marker of caste and gender privilege, and she moved it across those lines at real personal cost. Her poems survive as her own writing, unlike much of what is said about her, which comes from later admiring accounts. A specialist could argue that the popular "first female teacher" story hides other women who taught girls, which would weaken the reading that she acted alone.
--- question 1
How much of the harassment story comes from contemporary sources and how much from later tradition?

=== person/seondeok
title: Seondeok
type: person
date: c. 595 – 647 CE
themes: religion, science, kinship
src: 1e9259a0dd02
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Queen Seondeok (about 595 to 647 CE) ruled the Korean kingdom of Silla as its first reigning queen, a period marked by Buddhist temple building, early astronomical observation and continued warfare with the rival kingdoms of Baekje and Goguryeo.
--- fact 1
She succeeded her father, King Jinpyeong, in 632 after he left no surviving son, becoming Silla's first female monarch in her own right.
--- fact 2
Her reign saw the construction of Cheomseongdae, one of the oldest surviving astronomical observatories in East Asia, and the Hwangnyongsa nine-story pagoda, built to help protect the kingdom according to Buddhist belief.
--- fact 3
Silla faced repeated attacks from Baekje during her reign, including the loss of border fortresses, and she sent her general Kim Yushin and diplomat Kim Chunchu, later King Muyeol, to negotiate a military alliance with Tang China.
--- fact 4
A rebellion by the noble Bidam against her rule broke out in 647 and was suppressed by Kim Yushin shortly after her death that same year, which some accounts link to the unrest.
--- context
Seondeok ruled in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and the Tang alliance her court pursued eventually enabled Silla's unification of most of the Korean peninsula after her reign. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that later chroniclers, writing under a dynasty uneasy about female rule, framed Seondeok's reign partly through omens and prophecy rather than policy, which can make her recorded wisdom look more like folklore than governance. The observatory and pagoda, built objects rather than reported speech, are harder to dismiss and point to a court capable of sustained, costly projects.
--- question 1
How much of the surviving account of her reign reflects later Silla or Goryeo-era attitudes toward female sovereignty rather than contemporary record?

=== person/sequoyah
title: Sequoyah
type: person
date: c. 1770 – 1843
themes: language, technology
src: dcf2bdab7f2e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sequoyah (about 1770 to 1843) was a Cherokee silversmith who created the Cherokee syllabary, a writing system for the Cherokee language, completed by 1821. It is one of the few writing systems whose invention by a single known person is well documented.
--- fact 1
Sequoyah was born about 1770 in the Cherokee town of Tuskegee, in present-day Tennessee; the birth year is approximate.
--- fact 2
He did not read or write English. He began work on a writing system for Cherokee about 1809 and completed it by 1821.
--- fact 3
The syllabary has 85 characters (86 in early versions), each standing for a syllable.
--- fact 4
The system spread rapidly among the Cherokee. The Cherokee Phoenix, a newspaper printed in Cherokee and English, began publication in 1828 and was the first Native newspaper in the United States.
--- fact 5
He later lived among Cherokee who had moved west of the Mississippi River. He died in August 1843 in Mexico while searching for a group of Cherokee said to have settled there.
--- context
Sequoyah's work belongs to the history of writing; see [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. The Cherokee were among the nations forced west by [[event/indian-removal-and-the-trail-of-tears|Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]]. He lived in [[region/north-america|North America]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a man who could not read any writing designed a mark-code for his own sound-code, and that according to the accounts a whole people took it up within a few years, a rare case where the inventor of a writing system is known. That it spread among a people under pressure on their land shows memory-outsourcing serving to hold a community together and to deal with a state that ran on paper. The reading would weaken if evidence showed he drew more on existing writing systems than the accounts suggest.
--- question 1
How did he arrive at the syllable approach, and how much did he know of other writing systems?
