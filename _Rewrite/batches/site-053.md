=== person/truganini
title: Truganini
type: person
date: c. 1812 – 1876
themes: war, kinship
src: f43ed8dca844
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Truganini (about 1812 to 1876) was a Tasmanian Aboriginal woman of the Nuenonne people who grew up on Bruny Island and lived through the British colonization of Tasmania and died in Hobart. She was later widely called "the last Tasmanian Aboriginal," a label that Tasmanian Aboriginal people reject.
--- fact 1
Truganini was born about 1812, reportedly at Recherche Bay in southern Tasmania, as a member of the Nuenonne people, and she grew up on Bruny Island.
--- fact 2
Accounts say members of her family were killed or abducted by colonists during the Black War of the 1820s and early 1830s.
--- fact 3
In the 1830s she worked with George Augustus Robinson, a government-appointed conciliator who persuaded surviving Aboriginal people to move to Flinders Island, where disease and hardship were severe.
--- fact 4
She later lived at Oyster Cove, south of Hobart, and died on 8 May 1876.
--- fact 5
Her skeleton was exhumed in 1878 and displayed in the Tasmanian Museum from 1904 until 1947, despite her reported fear of mutilation.
--- fact 6
In 1976 her remains were cremated and the ashes scattered in the D'Entrecasteaux Channel.
--- fact 7
Tasmanian Aboriginal people today descend from other women, such as Fanny Cochrane Smith, and the community continues.
--- context
Truganini's life falls within the British colonization that followed the [[event/first-fleet-and-the-colonization-of-australia|First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]], which reshaped the lives of the [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]] in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Truganini's life is documented almost entirely by colonial officials, and that the record of her last decades is dominated by what was done to her body after death, when her own wishes about burial were not honored. The phrase "the last Tasmanian" turned a living community into an ending, a story-glue that served the settlers' account of what had happened. A specialist could argue that the label reflects a narrow test of descent, and that this reading understates how much of her own aim can be recovered.
--- question 1
What can Aboriginal oral histories add to the colonial record of her life?

=== person/trung-sisters
title: Trưng Sisters
type: person
date: 40 – 43 CE
themes: war, kinship
src: 675c36b7d465
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Trưng Sisters, Trưng Trắc and Trưng Nhị (Chinese Zheng Ce and Zheng Er), led a revolt against Han Chinese rule in northern Vietnam from 40 to 43 CE. Their birth years are unknown; the dates given here are those of the revolt.
--- fact 1
The Trưng Sisters came from Mê Linh in the Red River region, then part of the Han commandery of Jiaozhi.
--- fact 2
They began the revolt in 40 CE. Chinese sources link it to harsh rule by the Han governor Su Ding, and Vietnamese tradition adds that he executed Trưng Trắc's husband, Thi Sách.
--- fact 3
They took towns in several southern commanderies and are recorded as ruling as queens for about three years.
--- fact 4
The Han general Ma Yuan led an army south and defeated them by 43 CE.
--- fact 5
The Chinese history *Hou Hanshu* says they were captured and beheaded, while Vietnamese tradition says they drowned themselves in a river; this second account is traditional.
--- fact 6
Temples in Vietnam honor the sisters today, and streets are named for them (Hai Bà Trưng).
--- context
The Trưng Sisters resisted the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the sisters are remembered in Vietnam as founders of national resistance, a story-glue for a people whose own state came centuries later. The two main records, Chinese court histories and later Vietnamese chronicles, tell different versions of their end, so each side's writers shaped the memory. The reading would weaken if evidence showed the local lords who backed the revolt acted chiefly for their own interests rather than for a shared cause.
--- question 1
Who were the local leaders who backed the revolt, and what did they want?

=== person/tupaia
title: Tupaia
type: person
date: c. 1725 – 1770
themes: science, language
src: 52e2c7e7f8b4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Tupaia (about 1725 to 1770) was a priest and navigator from Ra'iatea in the Society Islands who sailed with James Cook on the Endeavour in 1769 and drew a chart of Pacific islands. He died at Batavia in 1770.
--- fact 1
Tupaia was born about 1725 on Ra'iatea and became a priest and navigator. After warriors from Borabora took Ra'iatea, he took refuge on Tahiti.
--- fact 2
In July 1769 he joined Cook's Endeavour, usually said to be at Tahiti (sources differ on the place), as a guide and interpreter, with a young servant, Taiata.
--- fact 3
In August 1769 he began a chart of the Pacific with Cook, Joseph Banks and other officers. Sources differ: it is said to show about 130 islands, of which he could name about 74, and it is often described as centered on Ra'iatea.
--- fact 4
The chart did not match European map conventions, and modern scholars have argued that it follows a Polynesian way of organizing islands by voyages and directions.
--- fact 5
On reaching New Zealand in October 1769 he could speak with Māori, whose language is closely related to Tahitian, and he interpreted for the crew.
--- fact 6
He died at Batavia on 20 December 1770, of illness, and Taiata died there in the same month.
--- context
Tupaia belonged to the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]], whose ancestors made the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. That he could speak with [[culture/maori|Maori]] in New Zealand reflects the shared origin of their languages. See [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Tupaia carried sailing knowledge in memory and moved it onto paper for outsiders, who found it hard to read because it did not use their grid. Two tool-lineages of navigation met on one ship, and the meeting is preserved only because one side wrote things down. The reading would weaken if scholars showed the chart's layout owed more to the European officers who helped draw it than to Tupaia.
--- question 1
How much of the chart's layout came from Tupaia and how much from the officers who drew it with him?

=== person/tupac-amaru-ii
title: Túpac Amaru II
type: person
date: 1738 – 1781
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 7fb7744891ce
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Túpac Amaru II (José Gabriel Condorcanqui, born 1738 or about 1742, died 1781) was a hereditary Indigenous chief and trader who led a large rebellion against Spanish colonial rule in the Andes in 1780 and 1781. He was executed in Cusco.
--- fact 1
José Gabriel Condorcanqui was born in 1738 (some sources say about 1742) in the highlands near Cusco. He became a kuraka (hereditary local chief) and a successful muleteer and trader.
--- fact 2
He took the name Túpac Amaru, claiming descent from the Inca ruler Túpac Amaru, whom the Spanish executed in 1572.
--- fact 3
Grievances behind the rebellion included higher Spanish taxes, forced sales of goods to Indigenous communities, and the mita, forced labor in mines.
--- fact 4
On 4 November 1780 he seized a local Spanish official, Antonio de Arriaga, who was executed shortly after (10 November 1780 per two sources), which began the rebellion.
--- fact 5
He issued proclamations ending the mita and other abuses, and the rebellion spread across the southern highlands.
--- fact 6
Spanish forces captured him in April 1781, and he was executed in Cusco on 18 May 1781 together with his wife Micaela Bastidas, who had helped lead the rebellion.
--- context
Túpac Amaru II claimed the legacy of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]], which the Spanish had ended in the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-inca-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]], in the [[region/andes|Andes]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. His rebellion is often placed in the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Túpac Amaru II framed a dispute over taxes and labor in the language of descent, using the ancestor-weight of the Inca name to call a wide and mixed population to act together. His trade and colonial education gave him footing in both worlds, and the rebellion shows how a story-glue from the past can serve a present grievance. The reading would weaken if sources showed that most support came from local grievances that his Inca claim did little to mobilize.
--- question 1
How much of the rebellion's support came from the Inca claim and how much from local grievances?

=== person/ulugh-beg
title: Ulugh Beg
type: person
date: 1394 – 1449
themes: science, art
src: 16965471c167
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ulugh Beg (1394 to 1449) was a Timurid ruler and astronomer who built an observatory at Samarkand and produced one of the most accurate star catalogues before the telescope.
--- fact 1
He became governor of Samarkand in 1409, at about sixteen.
--- fact 2
He built a madrasa (1417 to 1420) and, in 1420, an observatory.
--- fact 3
His star catalogue, completed by about 1437, lists at least 1,018 stars.
--- fact 4
He died in 1449 and was buried in the Gur-e-Amir mausoleum in Samarkand, beside his grandfather Timur.
--- context
Ulugh Beg made [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]] a center of learning in the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]], continuing traditions of [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]] and the [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a ruler who preferred the sky to the battlefield, and whose star catalogue outlasted his dynasty. Pattern-taming can flourish in unexpected places.
--- question 1
How accurate were Ulugh Beg's measurements?

=== person/utagawa-hiroshige
title: Utagawa Hiroshige
type: person
date: 1797 – 1858
themes: art, trade
src: 5502075f2d8b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Utagawa Hiroshige (1797 to 1858) was the last great master of the ukiyo-e woodblock print, a lyrical designer of rain, snow, moonlight and travellers on the road. His series on the Tōkaidō highway and on Edo were huge commercial successes and later inspired European artists.
--- fact 1
Hiroshige was born in Edo in 1797 into the Andō samurai family, whose hereditary job was fire warden at Edo Castle. Both parents died when he was about 12, and he inherited the post.
--- fact 2
About 1811 he became a pupil of the print designer Utagawa Toyohiro, who gave him the name Hiroshige. He handed the fire warden post to a relative and turned to landscape after Hokusai's Fuji series opened a new market.
--- fact 3
According to tradition he travelled the Tōkaidō in 1832 with an official delegation. The prints he published in 1833 to 1834 made him the most popular landscape artist in Japan.
--- fact 4
In 1856 he became a Buddhist monk but kept working on One Hundred Famous Views of Edo. He died in Edo on 12 October 1858, probably in a cholera epidemic.
--- fact 5
He designed the prints; carvers and printers made them. He used soft colour gradations (bokashi) and, in the Edo series, a vertical format with a large foreground object framing a distant view. "Hiroshige blue" was made with imported Prussian blue.
--- fact 6
Van Gogh painted copies of two of his Edo prints. Whistler's Thames nocturnes, Monet's Japanese bridge at Giverny and 1890s Art Nouveau posters show his influence, and [[person/frank-lloyd-wright|Frank Lloyd Wright]] was a major collector and dealer of his prints.
--- context
Hiroshige worked in Edo under the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], in the shadow and success of [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a fire warden's son who turned to landscape when a rival's success opened a market, so a commercial gap shaped the series as much as vision did. The road, a place of transit, became a collectible image, and the prints became the classic picture of old Edo. The design was one person's, the making a workshop's, and "Hiroshige blue" rests on an imported pigment, so a national image was built partly on a foreign material. The Tōkaidō journey is a tradition, not a documented fact.
--- question 1
Did Hiroshige actually travel the Tōkaidō in 1832, or did he work from other sources?

=== person/vincent-van-gogh
title: Vincent van Gogh
type: person
date: 1853 – 1890
themes: art
src: 36ee40d4a356
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Vincent van Gogh (30 March 1853 to 29 July 1890) was a Dutch painter who worked in France. He painted for only about ten years, produced around 2,100 works, and sold almost nothing, yet his vivid colour and expressive brushwork made him the most famous artist of modern times.
--- fact 1
Van Gogh was born in Zundert in the southern Netherlands, the son of a Protestant minister. At 16 he joined the art dealers Goupil & Cie and was dismissed in 1876. He then tried teaching and preaching among coal miners in the Borinage. In 1880 he decided to become an artist.
--- fact 2
His younger brother Theo, an art dealer, supported him for the rest of his life. His letters, mainly to Theo, are among the most moving documents of any artist's life.
--- fact 3
In 1886 he moved to Paris, where he met Pissarro, Toulouse-Lautrec, Émile Bernard and Gauguin and discovered Impressionist colour and Japanese prints. He painted copies of two Hiroshige prints.
--- fact 4
In 1888 he moved to Arles. Gauguin joined him in October, and the partnership collapsed in December after Van Gogh cut off part of his own ear. He entered the asylum at Saint-Rémy in May 1889, where he painted The Starry Night.
--- fact 5
In May 1890 he moved to Auvers-sur-Oise and painted about one canvas a day. He shot himself on 27 July 1890 and died two days later, aged 37.
--- fact 6
His work comprises around 2,100 pieces, including some 860 oil paintings. Key works include The Potato Eaters (1885), Sunflowers (1888) and Wheatfield with Crows (1890).
--- fact 7
Theo died six months after Vincent. Theo's widow, Johanna van Gogh-Bonger, promoted the work and published the letters in 1914. The Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam opened in 1973.
--- context
Van Gogh worked in the Netherlands, Belgium and France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and learned from Japanese prints by [[person/utagawa-hiroshige|Utagawa Hiroshige]] and [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]]. He revered [[person/rembrandt-van-rijn|Rembrandt van Rijn]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a person who sold almost nothing in life is now among the most famous of painters, and that the change ran through one channel: his brother's support and, after both men were dead, his sister-in-law's publication of the letters. The letters made the life legible, and the popular myth of the tortured genius, built later by a novel and a film, rests on them. That myth is a story-glue about artists that simplifies a man who planned his compositions carefully and made many drawings and repeated versions.
--- question 1
How far does the popular myth of his life shape how his paintings are read?

=== person/vitus-bering
title: Vitus Bering
type: person
date: 1681 – 1741
themes: science, trade
src: 90f9f096d4fb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Vitus Bering (1681 to 1741) was a Danish-born officer in the Russian navy who led two expeditions across Siberia to chart the waters between Asia and North America. The strait and sea between the continents carry his name.
--- fact 1
Bering was baptized on 5 August 1681 in Horsens, Denmark, and joined the Russian navy in 1704 under Peter the Great. He died on 19 December 1741 on the island now named for him.
--- fact 2
On the First Kamchatka Expedition (1725 to 1730) he sailed through the strait between Siberia and Alaska in 1728 without sighting the American coast, though some historians think it was visible.
--- fact 3
The Great Northern Expedition (1733 to 1743) grew to about 3,000 people, one of the largest scientific undertakings of its time.
--- fact 4
In 1741 his ship, the St. Peter, and a second ship under Aleksei Chirikov, the St. Paul, sailed separately toward America. Chirikov's crew sighted the coast first; Bering's crew sighted Mount Saint Elias on 16 July.
--- fact 5
On the return voyage the St. Peter wrecked on an uninhabited island. Bering died there of scurvy and exposure; of 77 men aboard, 46 survived to reach Kamchatka the next year.
--- fact 6
The Cossack Semyon Dezhnev had already passed through the strait in 1648, but Bering's voyages brought it to European attention, and James Cook named it for him in 1778.
--- context
Bering's expeditions extended Russian reach across [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], following the earlier [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] led by figures such as [[person/yermak-timofeyevich|Yermak Timofeyevich]]. See [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a strait already known to a Cossack in 1648 became attached to a Danish officer's name because his voyage reached the meaning-engine of European science and cartography. The expedition's scale, thousands crossing a continent to build ships on its far shore, shows a state's reach as much as one man's skill. A specialist could argue that Chirikov's crew, not Bering's, first reached America in 1741, which would weaken a reading that credits the discovery to Bering alone.
--- question 1
Did Bering's 1728 voyage bring the American coast into view, and did he recognize it?
--- question 2
How much credit for reaching America in 1741 belongs to Chirikov's separate ship?

=== person/wangari-maathai
title: Wangari Maathai
type: person
date: 1940 – 2011
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: 63a48926a5bf
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Wangari Maathai (1940 to 2011) was a Kenyan environmental and political activist who founded the Green Belt Movement, which organized women to plant trees, and in 2004 became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.
--- fact 1
Maathai was born on 1 April 1940 in Ihithe, near Nyeri, Kenya. She studied biology in the United States and at the University of Nairobi, where she received a doctorate in 1971; she is recorded as the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn one.
--- fact 2
In 1977 she founded the Green Belt Movement, which organized women to plant tree seedlings against deforestation, soil loss and fuelwood shortage.
--- fact 3
Her campaigns against building projects on public land in Nairobi and against forest clearing brought clashes with the government of President Daniel arap Moi; she was arrested and at times injured.
--- fact 4
She was elected to parliament in 2002 and served as an assistant minister for the environment from 2003.
--- fact 5
She received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004. In the same year a Kenyan newspaper reported that she had said HIV was deliberately created by scientists; she denied saying it and stated that she did not hold that belief.
--- fact 6
She died in Nairobi on 25 September 2011.
--- context
Maathai worked in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] in the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], in the decades after Kenya's independence during [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]]; compare [[person/chico-mendes|Chico Mendes]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Maathai joined a practical task, planting a tree, to an argument about who may decide the fate of land, and that the practice put women's labor at the center of an environmental story. The HIV dispute shows one public figure holding admired and contested positions at once. The reading of her as chiefly a tree planter would weaken if the movement's work on land rights and democracy were given equal weight.
--- question 1
How did the Green Belt Movement's political work shape its environmental results?

=== person/wassily-kandinsky
title: Wassily Kandinsky
type: person
date: 1866 – 1944
themes: art, religion
src: 4b59d2a01e82
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Wassily Kandinsky (16 December 1866 to 13 December 1944) was a Russian painter and theorist, one of the first to create purely abstract works in Europe. He believed colours and forms, freed from depicting objects, could move the viewer as directly as music.
--- fact 1
Kandinsky was born in Moscow and grew up in Odessa. He studied law and economics and began an academic career. In 1896, having seen one of Monet's Haystacks and heard Wagner's Lohengrin, he gave up law and moved to Munich to study painting.
--- fact 2
In 1911 he and Franz Marc founded Der Blaue Reiter (The Blue Rider) and he published On the Spiritual in Art. About then he painted his first fully abstract works.
--- fact 3
The First World War forced him back to Russia, where he took part in Soviet cultural institutions after the 1917 Revolution. He returned to Germany in 1921 and taught at the Bauhaus from 1922 to 1933.
--- fact 4
When the Nazis closed the Bauhaus he moved to Neuilly-sur-Seine near Paris, becoming a French citizen in 1939, and died there.
--- fact 5
He sorted his works into Impressions, Improvisations and Compositions, and made only ten Compositions over 30 years. He probably experienced synaesthesia and assigned musical qualities to colours: yellow like a trumpet, blue like a deep cello.
--- fact 6
Compositions I to III were destroyed in the Second World War. The Guggenheim Museum holds more than 150 of his works.
--- fact 7
His work influenced the Abstract Expressionists. In 2017 Murnau with Church II sold for £33 million.
--- context
Kandinsky lived in Russia, Germany and France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], through [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and the [[event/russian-revolution|Russian Revolution]]. He is usually credited with abstraction alongside [[person/hilma-af-klint|Hilma af Klint]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a lawyer who left his profession after a painting and a piece of music, and who then built a theory that colour and form carry spiritual meaning without pictured objects, a meaning-engine of his own made of pigment. Where af Klint kept comparable work hidden, he published a book, and that difference in visibility helped decide who was later credited with inventing abstraction, a story-glue of art history that is being revised.
--- question 1
Did synaesthesia shape his colour theory, and how much?

=== person/wilfrid-laurier
title: Wilfrid Laurier
type: person
date: 1841 – 1919
themes: kinship, trade
src: 6111d32ed4a5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Wilfrid Laurier (1841 to 1919) was the first French-Canadian Prime Minister of Canada, serving from 1896 to 1911 during a period of rapid western settlement and economic growth.
--- fact 1
A lawyer from Quebec, he became Liberal Party leader in 1887 and prime minister in 1896, holding the office for over fifteen years, then the longest unbroken term in Canadian history.
--- fact 2
His government promoted immigration and settlement of the Canadian prairies, a policy associated with his minister Clifford Sifton, that brought large numbers of European immigrant farmers to Western Canada.
--- fact 3
He worked to balance relations between English- and French-speaking Canada, though his support for Canadian participation in Britain's Boer War strained relations with Quebec nationalists who opposed it.
--- fact 4
He lost the 1911 election over a proposed reciprocity (free trade) agreement with the United States, which opponents attacked as a threat to Canada's ties with Britain.
--- context
Wilfrid Laurier led Canada, in [[region/north-america|North America]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], a period of large-scale immigration and westward settlement. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Laurier's career illustrates a recurring tension in settler societies built from more than one founding population: his efforts to hold English and French Canada together were tested repeatedly by exactly the kind of imperial military and economic questions, war participation and trade policy, that most directly divided them.
--- question 1
How much of the immigration boom under his government reflected deliberate policy versus favorable timing in global migration patterns?

=== person/william-morris
title: William Morris
type: person
date: 1834 – 1896
themes: art
src: 4bc43f9957ad
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
William Morris (1834 to 1896) was an English designer, writer and socialist, the central figure of the Arts and Crafts movement. He rejected cheap mechanised ornament in favour of patterns drawn from nature and made by traditional craft methods.
--- fact 1
Morris was born in Walthamstow in 1834 into a family that had grown wealthy through a Cornish copper mine. At Oxford he met the painter Edward Burne-Jones and, through him, the circle around Dante Gabriel Rossetti.
--- fact 2
He built Red House (1859 to 1860) and, unable to find furnishings he considered well made, founded a decorative arts firm in 1861 with Burne-Jones, Rossetti, Philip Webb and others. It was reorganised in 1875 as Morris & Co.
--- fact 3
He founded the Socialist League in 1884 and wrote extensively on politics and utopian fiction. In 1891 he founded the Kelmscott Press; its illustrated Chaucer was completed months before his death in 1896.
--- fact 4
He designed from real plants, disciplining them into flattened repeating patterns. He revived hand block-printing and indigo-discharge dyeing and letterpress printing from hand-cut type over faster industrial methods.
--- fact 5
His principle was "have nothing in your house that you do not know to be useful or believe to be beautiful". His goods were luxuries that generally only the wealthy could afford.
--- fact 6
Morris & Co. wallpapers and textiles, including Strawberry Thief (1883) and Willow Bough (1887), remain in production. The Kelmscott Chaucer launched the private-press movement.
--- context
Morris worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], in reaction to the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], and his machine-averse approach contrasts with that of [[person/christopher-dresser|Christopher Dresser]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a socialist who made luxury goods that mostly the wealthy could buy, an irony the record itself notes, so that an ethic of honest labour ran through a status-signal. His refusal of the machine was itself a claim about tool-lineage: that the hand-made line was the honest one. The patterns still in production after more than a century show a design outliving the arguments made for it.
--- question 1
How far did the movement he founded shape later industrial design, as opposed to reacting against it?

=== person/wu-zetian
title: Wu Zetian
type: person
date: 624 – 705 CE
themes: war, religion, kinship
src: 3a7ce9247b9b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Wu Zetian (624 to 705 CE) was the only woman to rule China in her own name as emperor, founding the short-lived Zhou dynasty (690 to 705).
--- fact 1
She entered the palace as a concubine of the Tang emperor and later became empress consort.
--- fact 2
She ruled effectively for decades before taking the throne herself in 690.
--- fact 3
She expanded the civil service examination system to draw in talent from beyond the aristocracy.
--- fact 4
She supported Buddhism.
--- context
Wu Zetian ruled in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], before the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]] made the examination system central.
--- observer
The Observer notes that the historical record about her was written largely by hostile male officials. How power appears in the record depends on who controls the writing.
--- question 1
How biased are the traditional histories of her?

=== person/xuanzang
title: Xuanzang
type: person
date: 602 – 664 CE
themes: religion, language
src: e85ca0a33c7b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Xuanzang (602 to 664) was a Chinese Buddhist monk who traveled overland to India from 629 to 645 in search of authentic scriptures, studied for years at the monastery of Nalanda, and returned to translate texts and record his journey.
--- fact 1
Xuanzang was born in 602 and ordained as a monk in his youth. In 629 he left China without imperial permission, traveling west through Central Asia toward India.
--- fact 2
He spent years studying at Nalanda, a major center of Buddhist learning, under the scholar Silabhadra, and visited Buddhist sites across the subcontinent.
--- fact 3
He returned to China in 645 with Buddhist texts, images and relics, and was welcomed by the Tang court despite his earlier unauthorized departure.
--- fact 4
With official support he led a translation effort that produced more than a thousand fascicles of Buddhist texts rendered from Sanskrit into Chinese.
--- fact 5
He also wrote the Great Tang Records on the Western Regions, a travel account whose distances and descriptions later helped archaeologists locate sites such as Nalanda itself.
--- fact 6
Centuries later his journey was reworked into the folk novel Journey to the West, adding a fantastical cast around a lightly fictionalized version of him.
--- context
Xuanzang traveled from [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], under the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], reaching [[place/nalanda|Nalanda]] in India. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one monk's disobedience of a travel ban produced, sixteen years later, a body of translated texts and a travelogue precise enough to guide excavations a thousand years afterward, memory-outsourcing at its most literal. His own record stands apart from the legendary version that grew around him in later folk fiction. A specialist could argue the fictionalized Journey to the West has shaped popular memory of him far more than his own writings, which would weaken a reading of the historical monk as what most people now picture.
--- question 1
How much of Silabhadra's teaching, as opposed to Xuanzang's own synthesis, shaped the texts he brought back?
--- question 2
How did the Tang court's welcome compare with its earlier ban on his departure?

=== person/yaa-asantewaa
title: Yaa Asantewaa
type: person
date: c. 1840 – 1921
themes: war, kinship, religion
src: 7de16c2f9b09
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Yaa Asantewaa (about 1840 to 1921) was the queen mother of Ejisu in the Asante kingdom, in present-day Ghana, who led an Asante uprising against British rule in 1900. She died in exile in the Seychelles.
--- fact 1
Yaa Asantewaa was born about 1840 and was queen mother (ohemaa) of Ejisu, a title held by a woman who advises and helps choose the ruler.
--- fact 2
The British exiled the Asante king Prempeh I in 1896. In March 1900 the governor, Frederick Hodgson, demanded the Golden Stool, the Asante symbol of national unity, so that he could sit on it.
--- fact 3
According to tradition, she then urged the Asante chiefs at a council to resist and became a leader of the uprising sometimes called the War of the Golden Stool.
--- fact 4
Asante forces besieged the British fort at Kumasi for several months in 1900. British reinforcements relieved it, and the fighting ended that year with a British victory.
--- fact 5
She was captured and exiled to the Seychelles in 1901, and died there on 17 October 1921.
--- fact 6
The British annexed Asante in 1902 but did not obtain the Golden Stool itself.
--- context
Yaa Asantewaa's revolt was part of resistance to colonial conquest during the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]], in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the war centered on a shared object, a stool in which Asante tradition holds the nation's spirit, and that the governor's demand to sit on it was heard as a demand on the story-glue of the whole people. The queen mother's office within the kin-lattice gave her standing to call men to fight. A specialist could argue that later nationalist memory has shaped the accounts of her council speech, which would weaken any reading that leans on the words she is said to have spoken.
--- question 1
How do Asante oral histories and British records differ on her role in the war?

=== person/yermak-timofeyevich
title: Yermak Timofeyevich
type: person
date: c. 1532 – 1585
themes: war, trade
src: 56809db1f35f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Yermak Timofeyevich (about 1532 to 1585) was a Cossack leader whose expedition across the Urals in 1581 and 1582 overthrew the Khanate of Sibir and began the Russian conquest of Siberia. His early life is poorly known.
--- fact 1
Yermak's birth year is uncertain; about 1532 is often given. Most accounts say he was a Volga Cossack, and some say he was wanted for piracy.
--- fact 2
In 1581 he set out from the Ural lands of the Stroganov merchant family with several hundred men armed with firearms. Accounts of the year and the numbers vary.
--- fact 3
In 1582 his force defeated the army of Khan Kuchum of Sibir and took the khanate's capital, Qashliq (Isker), on 26 October.
--- fact 4
He sent word of the conquest to Tsar Ivan IV, who reportedly pardoned him and sent gifts.
--- fact 5
Yermak died in August 1585 (some sources say 1584) in an ambush by Kuchum's forces. Tradition says he drowned in the Irtysh River, weighed down by chain mail sent by the tsar.
--- fact 6
Russian forts followed, including Tyumen in 1586 and Tobolsk in 1587.
--- context
Yermak's campaign opened the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] in [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a few hundred armed men working for a merchant family, not directly for the state, opened a path across a continent, and that the state adopted the result afterward. In Russian tradition he is a national hero, while for the peoples he conquered the campaign began tribute-taking and the loss of independence; both accounts can be accurate. The reading of the venture as private rather than state-directed would weaken if court records show it was planned in Moscow.
--- question 1
How much did Moscow direct the expedition, and how much did it adopt afterward?

=== person/yongle-emperor
title: Yongle Emperor
type: person
date: 1360 – 1424
themes: trade, technology, war
src: 0faeec5d52d2
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Yongle Emperor (Zhu Di; 1360 to 1424) was the third emperor of China's Ming dynasty, whose reign moved the capital to Beijing and sponsored the ocean voyages of Zheng He.
--- fact 1
He seized the throne from his nephew, the Jianwen Emperor, in a civil war from 1399 to 1402, after his father, the dynasty's founder, had passed over him in the succession.
--- fact 2
He moved the imperial capital from Nanjing to Beijing and began construction of the Forbidden City, completed in 1420.
--- fact 3
He commissioned [[person/zheng-he|Zheng He]]'s seven treasure-fleet voyages, the first of which departed in 1405, reaching as far as the coasts of East Africa and Arabia and projecting Ming naval power across the Indian Ocean.
--- fact 4
He sponsored the Yongle Encyclopedia, an enormous compilation of Chinese knowledge finished in 1408 that ran to over 11,000 volumes, most of which was later lost.
--- context
The Yongle Emperor ruled the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and his patronage of Zheng He's fleets connected Ming China directly with trade networks across the Indian Ocean. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the treasure fleets Yongle sponsored were abruptly ended by his successors within decades of his death, their records largely destroyed, while the European voyages that followed half a century later, on a fraction of the scale, are remembered as the start of an age of exploration. The difference lies less in capability than in which government chose to keep looking outward.
--- question 1
Why did the Ming court end the treasure fleet voyages so decisively after Yongle's death, rather than continuing them at reduced scale?

=== person/zenobia
title: Zenobia
type: person
date: c. 240 – 274 CE
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 035bbc4d6b4f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Zenobia (Septimia Zenobia; about 240 to after 272) was queen of the caravan city of Palmyra in present-day Syria. She ruled for her young son and briefly extended Palmyrene control over Roman Egypt, Syria and much of Anatolia before the emperor Aurelian defeated her in 272.
--- fact 1
Zenobia was the wife of Odaenathus, the ruler of Palmyra who had fought the Sasanian Persians for Rome in the 260s. He was killed about 267, and she took power as regent for their son Vaballathus.
--- fact 2
About 270 her forces took Roman Egypt and pushed into Anatolia. By 271 she and her son used the titles Augusta and Augustus.
--- fact 3
Some coins struck in 271 and 272 show Aurelian on one side and Vaballathus on the other, so her claim was for a time framed inside the Roman order.
--- fact 4
Aurelian defeated her armies in 272 at Immae and Emesa and captured her. Palmyra was sacked in 273 after a revolt.
--- fact 5
Her fate is unknown. One tradition says she was shown in Aurelian's triumph in 274; its source, the *Historia Augusta*, is often unreliable.
--- context
Zenobia's revolt against the [[culture/romans|Romans]] took place in [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and Palmyra's wealth rested on caravan trade, part of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Zenobia governed a trading city between two large powers, and that her claim-line, from Egypt to central Anatolia, grew while Rome was weak and shrank within a few years once it recovered. Most personal detail about her comes from Roman writers who defeated her and later writers who embellished her. The reading of her as a rebel would weaken if inscriptions and coins show she sought a place within the Roman order, as some historians argue.
--- question 1
Did she seek independence from Rome or a share of its power?

=== technology/airplane
title: Airplane
type: technology
date: 1903
themes: technology
src: bc5e3d2fdc75
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A powered fixed-wing aircraft achieved documented, sustained, controlled flight with a pilot aboard in 1903, through work by Orville and Wilbur Wright.
--- fact 1
On 17 December 1903 near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, the Wrights made four flights in the Wright Flyer.
--- fact 2
The first flight lasted 12 seconds; the fourth, flown by Wilbur, lasted 59 seconds and covered about 260 meters (852 feet).
--- fact 3
In 1901 they built a wind tunnel in Dayton and tested model wings, having found published lift tables unreliable.
--- fact 4
Their 1902 glider was controlled in pitch, roll, and yaw, using wing-warping for roll; a patent was granted in 1906.
--- fact 5
Santos-Dumont's 23 October 1906 flight near Paris was the first publicly witnessed, certified powered flight in Europe.
--- fact 6
Claims for Gustave Whitehead (Connecticut, 1901) and Richard Pearse (New Zealand, 1902-1903) rest on disputed press reports and later eyewitness accounts, and most historians do not accept them.
--- context
The Flyer relied on a light [[technology/internal-combustion-engine|Internal Combustion Engine]]. Aircraft entered military use in [[event/world-war-i|World War I]], first for reconnaissance and aerial photography, and civil aviation grew afterward. Aerospace engineering later fed the [[event/space-age|Space Age]]. Early flight work spanned [[region/north-america|North America]] and Europe in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Thread: [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The airplane reads as tool-lineage: the species joined a light engine, propellers, lift data, and control methods, some inherited and some new, and the Wrights' distinctive step was control. Within roughly a decade it also served sanctioned harm as well as scouting. This weakens if earlier controlled flights were documented, or if gradual accumulation (Lilienthal, Langley, and others) explains the result better than a single 1903 threshold.
--- question 1
How much did Whitehead and Pearse achieve, if anything?
--- question 2
How far did earlier glider work shape the Wrights' methods?

=== technology/alphabet
title: Alphabet
type: technology
date: c. 1800 BCE
themes: language, technology
src: 7214995af8c1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The alphabet is a writing system in which signs represent individual sounds. The first alphabet, Proto-Sinaitic, appeared around 1800 BCE and gave rise to most alphabets in use today.
--- fact 1
It was adapted by Semitic-speaking workers from Egyptian hieroglyphs.
--- fact 2
The Phoenician alphabet (about 1050 BCE) had about 22 letters, all consonants.
--- fact 3
The Greeks added vowels in the 8th century BCE, and the Latin alphabet descends from a Greek form.
--- fact 4
Hebrew, Arabic, Cyrillic, and many Indian scripts also descend from or are related to early alphabets.
--- context
The alphabet follows the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] and spread through the [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a system of about two dozen signs made literacy learnable in months, not years. Simplicity made writing far more widely available.
--- question 1
Who invented the first alphabet?

=== technology/anesthesia
title: Anesthesia
type: technology
date: 1846
themes: science
src: b92765c96f1a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anesthesia is the use of drugs or vapors to block pain and consciousness during surgery. General anesthesia spread rapidly worldwide after a public ether demonstration in 1846.
--- fact 1
William T. G. Morton publicly demonstrated ether anesthesia at Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston, on 16 October 1846, credited with launching its rapid worldwide adoption.
--- fact 2
Priority is contested: Georgia physician Crawford Long used ether in minor surgery on 30 March 1842, years earlier, but published the case only in 1849.
--- fact 3
Japanese surgeon Hanaoka Seishu performed surgery under a general anesthetic, an herbal compound called tsusensan, in 1804, decades earlier, though the work stayed isolated within Japan.
--- fact 4
Horace Wells demonstrated nitrous oxide's pain relief in December 1844; his public Harvard demonstration in January 1845 failed from an inadequate dose.
--- fact 5
Ether anesthesia reached Europe within weeks: Robert Liston performed the first such public operation in London on 21 December 1846.
--- context
Surgical anesthesia grew from the era's expanding [[theme/science|Science]], part of the technical change of the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Morton's 1846 demonstration in [[region/north-america|North America]] was adopted within weeks in Europe, and it saw its first mass use during the [[event/american-civil-war|American Civil War]], alongside contemporaries such as [[technology/vaccination|Vaccination]] in the century's medical advances.
--- observer
This looks like one tool-lineage that surfaced independently in more than one branch of the species' story, though only one branch fed the modern global chain: the species tends to credit whichever branch achieved widest publicity, not the earliest instance. This weakens if unrecorded contact between the branches is found; an alternative reading treats them as separate independent inventions.
--- question 1
Why did Hanaoka's method not spread beyond Japan despite treating many documented cases?
--- question 2
How much did Long's late publication cost him priority credit relative to Morton?
