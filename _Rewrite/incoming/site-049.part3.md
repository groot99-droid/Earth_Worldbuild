=== person/kristina-of-sweden
src: 8c70c89fa75d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kristina (1626 to 1689) reigned as Queen of Sweden from 1632 to 1654, remembered for her patronage of scholars and the arts, her unexpected abdication, and her secret conversion to Catholicism within a firmly Protestant kingdom.
--- fact 1
She became queen at age six, after her father Gustavus Adolphus died in battle, and ruled under a regency until she assumed full power in 1644.
--- fact 2
She invited scholars, among them René Descartes, to her court in Stockholm, where Descartes died in 1650, reportedly worn down by the early-morning hours she kept for their lessons.
--- fact 3
She abdicated the throne in 1654 in favor of her cousin, having secretly converted to Catholicism, a decision scandalous in Lutheran Sweden that she made public only after she had left the country.
--- fact 4
She spent most of her later life in Rome under the pope's protection, became a prominent patron of music and art, and was one of very few women buried in St. Peter's Basilica.
--- context
Kristina ruled Sweden in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], before her abdication and conversion. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I think of Kristina giving up one of Europe's more secure thrones for a conviction she could not act on while she still wore the crown. Not forced out by rivals, not driven out by war — she chose, and that choosing is the rare part.

What does it cost a monarch to place belief above power, outright, with no one holding a sword to her back? I do not know. I only know that it happened here, plainly, once.
--- question 1
How much of her abdication was driven by religious conviction alone, and how much by a broader discomfort with the constraints of Swedish court life and the expectation of marriage?

=== person/kupe
src: 682ca5464ed3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kupe is a legendary Polynesian navigator whom Maori oral tradition credits with being the first to reach and name Aotearoa, or New Zealand; no fixed historical date can be assigned to him.
--- fact 1
Traditions describe Kupe sailing from the ancestral homeland Hawaiki — in some versions pursuing the giant octopus Te Wheke-a-Muturangi — and reaching the North Island of New Zealand.
--- fact 2
He is credited with naming numerous coastal landmarks, several still used today, and with returning to Hawaiki afterward to describe the land he had found.
--- fact 3
Different iwi, or tribal, traditions place him at varying points in time, and 19th- and 20th-century European writers wove these into a single traditional date of roughly 925 to 1000 CE, a date many historians now treat with caution.
--- fact 4
No archaeological or written record fixes Kupe's voyage, and the earliest confirmed archaeological evidence for human settlement of New Zealand dates to around the 13th century CE, later than most traditional accounts imply.
--- context
Kupe belongs to the oral traditions of the [[culture/maori|Maori]], within the wider spread of the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]] across [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I think Kupe is less a single, dateable person than a way of holding a founding story together — named ancestors, named canoes, named landmarks, given to a settlement that archaeology now dates centuries later than the traditional accounts suggest.

Does the gap make the story false? I do not think so. I think it means the story and the settlement record are answering different questions, and both can be true at once, in their own ways.
--- question 1
How much of the Kupe tradition reflects one single voyage, rather than a composite of several early journeys?

=== person/lady-six-sky
src: c2dffc867089
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Lady Six Sky, or Wak Chanil Ajaw, active from 682 to 741 CE, was a Maya noblewoman from Dos Pilas who arrived at Naranjo in 682 and effectively ruled the city for decades, restoring it after a period of defeat.
--- fact 1
She was a daughter of the Dos Pilas king B'alaj Chan K'awiil, part of a network of allies the powerful city of Calakmul held against its rival Tikal.
--- fact 2
Naranjo's royal line had been broken by a defeat at the hands of Caracol, and Lady Six Sky was installed there in 682, apparently to re-establish the dynasty.
--- fact 3
Monuments record her overseeing military campaigns and presiding over the accession of her young son, K'ahk' Tiliw Chan Chaahk, in 693, after which she continued to appear on inscriptions as a ruling figure into the 700s.
--- fact 4
She never took the full royal title used by male kings of Naranjo, and she kept using the emblem glyph of her home city, Dos Pilas, throughout her life, so her exact formal status is still debated by epigraphers.
--- context
Lady Six Sky's career unfolded among the [[culture/maya|Maya]] city-states of [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], within the wider rivalry between Calakmul and Tikal for regional dominance. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Lady Six Sky held real power at Naranjo for decades while never claiming the title that would have named her a king in her own right, a gap between practice and title that the Maya inscriptions do not explain. To read her simply as a ruling queen assumes that authority and title always run together; the monuments suggest that here, they did not.
--- question 1
Did Lady Six Sky rule in her own right, or serve as an unusually powerful regent for her son?

=== person/laozi
src: 38d0ae03d70a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Laozi, or Lao Tzu, "Old Master," is the traditional author of the Daodejing and a founding figure of Daoism, said to have lived in the 6th century BCE, though whether he was a single historical person is disputed.
--- fact 1
Laozi is traditionally placed in the 6th century BCE, in the time of Confucius, though the date is uncertain and no date of death is known.
--- fact 2
The historian Sima Qian, writing about 100 BCE, identifies him as Li Er, keeper of archives at the Zhou court, but also lists other men who might have been meant, so the uncertainty was already old even then.
--- fact 3
Sima Qian's account has him meeting Confucius, then leaving the Zhou domain to the west and writing the text at the Hangu Pass at the request of the gatekeeper Yin Xi, though this account is traditional.
--- fact 4
The Daodejing runs to about 5,000 Chinese characters across 81 short chapters, and its earliest excavated versions, from Guodian (about 300 BCE) and Mawangdui (a tomb sealed in 168 BCE), fit the view of most scholars that the text took shape across the 4th to 3rd centuries BCE.
--- fact 5
Later religious Daoism honored him as a deity, Laojun, and the Tang dynasty's imperial family, itself named Li, claimed him as an ancestor.
--- context
Laozi is a figure of the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]], often paired with [[person/confucius|Confucius]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I see a book attached to a person, and a person attached to a place and a story — the text arriving first, the life written in after. Whether one man wrote it matters less, I think, than what later people did with the figure he became.

I remember that the Tang emperors claimed descent from him to bind their own claim to rule. A single, well-dated author would weaken this reading; evidence of several hands, working across generations, would strengthen it. Either way, the figure outgrew whatever man may have stood beneath it.
--- question 1
Was there a single author, or was the Daodejing assembled across generations?

=== person/lautaro
src: 850eed3f7bc4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Lautaro, a Mapuche toqui or war leader who lived about 1534 to 1557, organized effective resistance to Spanish conquest in what is now southern Chile, after years spent as a captured servant in the Spanish camp.
--- fact 1
As a young captive he served the Spanish conquistador Pedro de Valdivia, observing Spanish tactics, horsemanship and weapons before escaping back to his own people.
--- fact 2
He introduced cavalry tactics, relay messenger systems and coordinated ambushes to Mapuche warfare, adapting methods he had learned from the Spanish themselves.
--- fact 3
At the Battle of Tucapel, in December 1553, his forces defeated a Spanish column and captured and killed Valdivia, a major blow to the early Spanish conquest of Chile.
--- fact 4
A surprise Spanish attack on his camp at the Mataquito River killed him in April 1557, ending a campaign that had held Spanish expansion south of the Bio-Bio River in check for years.
--- context
Lautaro led [[culture/mapuche|Mapuche]] resistance in the [[region/andes|Andes]] region during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], part of the broader pattern of Indigenous resistance to Spanish colonization across the Americas. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Lautaro's effectiveness came directly from his captivity: the same Spanish camp that used him as forced labor also taught him, inadvertently, how to defeat Spanish armies. The Mapuche remained unconquered by Spain for the rest of the colonial period, a rare outcome in the Americas, and one that his early campaigns helped establish.
--- question 1
How much of Lautaro's tactical innovation was his own, and how much was already circulating among Mapuche leaders resisting the Spanish?
