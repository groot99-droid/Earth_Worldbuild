=== person/savitribai-phule
title: Savitribai Phule
src: edccd29bd521
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Savitribai Phule (1831 to 1897) was an Indian educator, poet and social reformer in Maharashtra who, with her husband Jyotirao Phule, opened one of the first schools for girls in Pune in 1848. She died of plague in 1897 after nursing patients.
--- fact 1
Savitribai Phule was born on 3 January 1831 in Naigaon, Maharashtra, and married Jyotirao Phule in 1840 as a child; he taught her to read.
--- fact 2
In 1848 the couple opened a school for girls in Pune. She is often called India's first woman teacher; earlier girls' schools existed, so the "first" claim is contested, and historians also credit Fatima Sheikh as a co-worker.
--- fact 3
Accounts say she was harassed with stones and dung on her way to teach.
--- fact 4
She and Jyotirao later opened further schools, including for children of lower castes, and in 1863 they founded a home for the prevention of infanticide.
--- fact 5
She published a book of poetry, *Kavya Phule*, in 1854.
--- fact 6
She died on 10 March 1897 in Pune after catching plague while nursing patients during an outbreak.
--- context
Savitribai Phule worked in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] under the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], when schooling and caste were contested subjects. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I think of literacy in her setting as a mark-code that was itself a marker of caste and gender privilege, and of how she crossed those lines at real personal cost. I remember that her poems survive as her own writing, unlike much of what is said about her, which comes from later admiring accounts. I wonder whether a specialist could argue that the popular "first female teacher" story hides other women who taught girls, which would weaken the reading that she acted alone.
--- question 1
How much of the harassment story comes from contemporary sources and how much from later tradition?

=== person/seondeok
title: Seondeok
src: 1e9259a0dd02
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Queen Seondeok (about 595 to 647 CE) ruled the Korean kingdom of Silla as its first reigning queen, a period marked by Buddhist temple building, early astronomical observation and continued warfare with the rival kingdoms of Baekje and Goguryeo.
--- fact 1
She succeeded her father, King Jinpyeong, in 632 after he left no surviving son, becoming Silla's first female monarch in her own right.
--- fact 2
Her reign saw the construction of Cheomseongdae, one of the oldest surviving astronomical observatories in East Asia, and the Hwangnyongsa nine-story pagoda, built to help protect the kingdom according to Buddhist belief.
--- fact 3
Silla faced repeated attacks from Baekje during her reign, including the loss of border fortresses, and she sent her general Kim Yushin and diplomat Kim Chunchu, later King Muyeol, to negotiate a military alliance with Tang China.
--- fact 4
A rebellion by the noble Bidam against her rule broke out in 647 and was suppressed by Kim Yushin shortly after her death that same year, which some accounts link to the unrest.
--- context
Seondeok ruled in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and the Tang alliance her court pursued eventually enabled Silla's unification of most of the Korean peninsula after her reign. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I notice that later chroniclers, writing under a dynasty uneasy about female rule, framed Seondeok's reign partly through omens and prophecy rather than policy, which can make her recorded wisdom look more like folklore than governance. I set that against the observatory and the pagoda, built objects rather than reported speech, harder to dismiss, pointing to a court capable of sustained, costly projects.
--- question 1
How much of the surviving account of her reign reflects later Silla or Goryeo-era attitudes toward female sovereignty rather than contemporary record?

=== person/sequoyah
title: Sequoyah
src: dcf2bdab7f2e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sequoyah (about 1770 to 1843) was a Cherokee silversmith who created the Cherokee syllabary, a writing system for the Cherokee language, completed by 1821. It is one of the few writing systems whose invention by a single known person is well documented.
--- fact 1
Sequoyah was born about 1770 in the Cherokee town of Tuskegee, in present-day Tennessee; the birth year is approximate.
--- fact 2
He did not read or write English, and began work on a writing system for Cherokee about 1809, completing it by 1821.
--- fact 3
The syllabary has 85 characters (86 in early versions), each standing for a syllable.
--- fact 4
The system spread rapidly among the Cherokee. The Cherokee Phoenix, a newspaper printed in Cherokee and English, began publication in 1828 and was the first Native newspaper in the United States.
--- fact 5
He later lived among Cherokee who had moved west of the Mississippi River, and died in August 1843 in Mexico while searching for a group of Cherokee said to have settled there.
--- context
Sequoyah's work belongs to the history of writing; see [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. The Cherokee were among the nations forced west by [[event/indian-removal-and-the-trail-of-tears|Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]]. He lived in [[region/north-america|North America]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I think of a man who could not read any writing, designing a mark-code for his own sound-code, and, according to the accounts, a whole people taking it up within a few years, a rare case where the inventor of a writing system is known by name. I see it spreading among a people under pressure on their land, memory-outsourcing put to work holding a community together and dealing with a state that ran on paper. This reading would weaken if evidence showed he drew more on existing writing systems than the accounts suggest.
--- question 1
How did he arrive at the syllable approach, and how much did he know of other writing systems?
