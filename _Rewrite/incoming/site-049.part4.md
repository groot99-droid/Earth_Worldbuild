=== person/leo-tolstoy
src: af4bf57df2fb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Leo Tolstoy (1828 to 1910) was a Russian novelist, author of the realist epics War and Peace and Anna Karenina, and a religious crisis in the second half of his life turned him toward shorter fiction and writing on faith, mortality and nonviolence that would influence Gandhi.
--- fact 1
Tolstoy was born in 1828 into an aristocratic family at Yasnaya Polyana, the estate south of Moscow that he inherited and managed for most of his life, and he served briefly as an army officer in the Crimean War.
--- fact 2
In the 1860s and 1870s he wrote War and Peace (1869) and Anna Karenina (1877), and he founded a school for the children on his estate.
--- fact 3
After Anna Karenina he underwent a spiritual crisis, described in A Confession; he rejected the Russian Orthodox Church, embraced an ascetic, pacifist reading of Christian ethics, and increasingly disowned his earlier fiction as morally frivolous.
--- fact 4
His later works, shorter, starker and more didactic than the novels, include The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886), The Kreutzer Sonata (1889) and Resurrection (1899).
--- fact 5
War and Peace argues, in long digressions, that the forces driving historical events lay far beyond the control of any single general or emperor.
--- fact 6
His writing on nonviolent resistance to evil directly influenced Mahatma Gandhi, who corresponded with him near the end of his life.
--- fact 7
He died in 1910 of pneumonia at a remote railway station, after collapsing during a flight from his estate, and Yasnaya Polyana is now preserved as a museum.
--- context
Tolstoy wrote in the Russian Empire, in [[region/europe|Europe]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and his ethics passed through [[person/mahatma-gandhi|Mahatma Gandhi]] into later traditions of nonviolence. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I read a writer who argued that history is driven by forces beyond any general or emperor, and then, in later life, disowned his own fiction and preached nonviolence instead. One person, testing two accounts of what moves people.

I remember the line running from Tolstoy to Gandhi: ethics passed from hand to hand, by letter, and from there into movements that outlived them both. The flight from the estate, the death at a railway station — that ending invites retelling, again and again, and so his last days deserve the same care I would give to any other late-life story.
--- question 1
How much of his later disavowal of his own novels was reasoned, and how much was crisis?

=== person/leonardo-da-vinci
src: ba103999b6c5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Leonardo da Vinci (15 April 1452 to 2 May 1519) was an Italian painter, engineer and anatomist who left few finished paintings — counts differ, but fewer than 25 attributed major works, some unfinished, including the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper — alongside thousands of notebook pages of studies.
--- fact 1
Leonardo was born on 15 April 1452 near Vinci in Tuscany, the illegitimate son of a notary, Ser Piero, and a young woman named Caterina, and he was apprenticed in Florence to Andrea del Verrocchio.
--- fact 2
About 1482 he moved to Milan, where, in a letter to Duke Ludovico Sforza, he stressed his skills as a military engineer before his art, and there he painted The Last Supper.
--- fact 3
When the French invaded Milan in 1499, he moved between Mantua, Venice and Florence, worked briefly as a military engineer for Cesare Borgia, and began the Mona Lisa in Florence.
--- fact 4
In 1516 King Francis I invited him to France and gave him the manor of Clos Lucé near Amboise, where he died on 2 May 1519; his pupil Francesco Melzi inherited his paintings, tools, library and personal effects, and, by tradition, his notebooks.
--- fact 5
His notebooks hold thousands of pages of drawings and mirror-writing, with studies of anatomy, flight, hydraulics and optics, and he dissected corpses to understand muscles and bones.
--- fact 6
He pioneered sfumato, a soft modelling without hard outlines, and his key works include the Virgin of the Rocks (about 1483 to 1493, sources differ), Lady with an Ermine (about 1489 to 1491), Vitruvian Man (about 1485 to 1490, sources differ), The Last Supper (about 1495 to 1498) and the Mona Lisa (about 1503 to 1519).
--- fact 7
Not all of his experiments with materials held up: The Last Supper began to decay within decades.
--- fact 8
The Codex Leicester was bought by Bill Gates in 1994, and in 2017 the Salvator Mundi, attributed to Leonardo, sold for $450 million, a record for a painting, though its attribution remains disputed.
--- context
Leonardo worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
I think of one person who made pictures, studied anatomy and flight, and set it all down in mirror-writing that mostly stayed shut inside notebooks. Because those notebooks scattered after his death, what he understood reached his own contemporaries through the paintings, not through publication, and only later did the notes reveal how far his understanding truly went.

I remember, too, that his fame today rests partly on status: a price of $450 million for a disputed attribution measures a market as much as it measures a picture. What is a name worth, when even doubt cannot lower it?
--- question 1
Is the Salvator Mundi truly by Leonardo, and if so, how much of it?

=== person/li-qingzhao
src: c6a5baab01e4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Li Qingzhao (1084 to about 1155) was a Chinese poet of the Song dynasty, widely regarded as the greatest woman writer in the classical Chinese tradition, and her ci, a lyric verse form, moved from a settled, cultured life to loss and exile after northern China fell to invaders.
--- fact 1
Born in 1084 in Jinan, Shandong, to a literary family, she is thought to have died about 1155.
--- fact 2
In 1101 she married Zhao Mingcheng, a scholar who shared her interest in antiquities, and together they compiled the Jinshi lu, a catalog of bronze and stone inscriptions.
--- fact 3
When the Jin dynasty invaded northern China in 1127, the couple fled south with part of their collection, much of which was lost along the way, and Zhao Mingcheng died in 1129.
--- fact 4
After his death she wrote an afterword to their Jinshi lu describing their marriage and their losses, and she briefly remarried around 1132, divorcing amid scandal soon after.
--- fact 5
Her poems, mostly short ci in personal, direct language, describe love, separation, and later grief and displacement, and fewer than 100 of her poems survive, out of a larger body assembled and praised by later editors.
--- context
Li Qingzhao lived in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], within the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]], whose north fell to Jin invasion in 1127. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I read her verse and find an ordinary domestic partnership — book-collecting, companionship — that a state's collapse turned into flight and loss. It was that turn, from ease into grief, that gave later readers a reason to keep her words alive.

But a specialist could argue that fewer than a hundred surviving poems, out of a much larger and partly lost body, cannot support firm claims about how her style changed across her life. If that is right, it would weaken any confident before-and-after reading — and I have to hold that caution alongside the story I want to tell.
--- question 1
Given how much was lost after 1127, how much of her attributed work is authentic?
--- question 2
What became of her after that brief second marriage?

=== person/liliuokalani
src: 35c626e2ec66
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Liliuokalani (1838 to 1917) was the last monarch of the Kingdom of Hawaii, reigning from 1891 until a group of residents, backed by United States troops, deposed her in January 1893.
--- fact 1
Born in Honolulu on 2 September 1838, she died there on 11 November 1917.
--- fact 2
On 29 January 1891 she succeeded her brother Kalākaua, becoming the kingdom's only reigning queen.
--- fact 3
In January 1893 she moved to issue a constitution that would restore royal powers reduced by the 1887 "Bayonet Constitution."
--- fact 4
On 17 January 1893 a Committee of Safety of mostly American and European residents overthrew her while United States Marines were ashore, and she yielded to the United States government to avoid bloodshed, asking it to reinstate her.
--- fact 5
After a failed royalist uprising, she was arrested and signed an abdication in January 1895.
--- fact 6
In 1897 Native Hawaiians submitted mass petitions against annexation, but the United States annexed Hawaii in 1898.
--- fact 7
She composed songs, including "Aloha ʻOe," and published a memoir, *Hawaii's Story by Hawaii's Queen*, in 1898.
--- fact 8
In 1993 the United States Congress apologized to Native Hawaiians for the overthrow, which had been carried out with the participation of agents and citizens of the United States.
--- context
Liliuokalani ruled a Native Hawaiian state in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Native Hawaiians descend from the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]]; see [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I watch Liliuokalani defend her kingdom's sovereignty with the same instruments her opponents used against her: a written constitution, diplomatic appeals, a published memoir. Her songs carried love of place and people in a form that outlasted the kingdom itself.

I remember the 1993 apology — a wrong named at last, without ever being undone. And I remember to hold this loosely: the reading of her as the main carrier of the resistance would weaken if the 1897 petitions show that it rested chiefly on Native Hawaiian organizations rather than on her alone.
--- question 1
How much of the resistance to annexation came from Native Hawaiian organizations, rather than from the queen herself?

=== person/louis-comfort-tiffany
src: db1fb0766d38
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Louis Comfort Tiffany (1848 to 1933) was an American designer whose iridescent Favrile glass, stained-glass windows and lamps became the signature look of American Art Nouveau, though recent scholarship credits many of his studio's best-known designs to staff designers, above all Clara Driscoll.
--- fact 1
Tiffany was born in New York in 1848, the son of the jeweller Charles Lewis Tiffany, and he trained as a painter under George Inness before turning to interior design and decorative arts in the 1870s.
--- fact 2
His firm redecorated state rooms in the White House in 1882 for President Chester Arthur, work that was later removed; in 1885 he founded what became Tiffany Studios, and in 1894 he patented Favrile glass.
--- fact 3
His Byzantine-style chapel for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago won international acclaim, and he built Laurelton Hall on Long Island as a showcase of his work.
--- fact 4
Favrile glass builds iridescent, metallic colour into molten glass through mineral oxides, and "drapery glass" was manipulated while hot to form folds like fabric.
--- fact 5
Financial losses during the Depression forced the studio's closure, and he died in New York in 1933.
--- fact 6
Staff designers did much of the detailed design of individual pieces, especially the lamps, above all Clara Driscoll, who led the women's glass-cutting department.
--- fact 7
For most of the 20th century Tiffany was credited as sole designer, until the mid-2000s rediscovery of Driscoll's letters credited her and the women of the "Tiffany Girls" department with several famous lamps, including the Dragonfly — a correction now generally accepted by museums and scholars.
--- context
Tiffany worked in [[region/north-america|North America]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. [[person/christopher-dresser|Christopher Dresser]] travelled to Japan in 1876 and 1877 partly on commission from Tiffany & Co., the jewellery firm founded by Tiffany's father. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I look at a name on a product and see it credit one man for the work of a whole studio, and I remember that the correction came only from letters found a century later. The name worked like a brand, a signal of status that gathered credit upward and away from the hands that made the thing.

The women who cut the glass, who designed much of the work — they were recorded only in a side channel, almost an afterthought. This pattern, a maker's name absorbing a workshop, is old, older than this one case. What makes this case different is only that the letters survived to tell us so.
--- question 1
Which other famous Tiffany Studios pieces were designed by staff, rather than by Tiffany himself?
