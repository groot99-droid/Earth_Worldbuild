=== person/zenobia
title: Zenobia
type: person
date: c. 240 – 274 CE
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 035bbc4d6b4f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Zenobia, or Septimia Zenobia, born about 240 and living on after 272, was queen of the caravan city of Palmyra in present-day Syria. She ruled for her young son and briefly extended Palmyrene control over Roman Egypt, Syria and much of Anatolia before the emperor Aurelian defeated her in 272.
--- fact 1
Zenobia was the wife of Odaenathus, the ruler of Palmyra who had fought the Sasanian Persians on Rome's behalf in the 260s. He was killed about 267, and she took power as regent for their son, Vaballathus.
--- fact 2
About 270 her forces took Roman Egypt and pushed into Anatolia. By 271 she and her son were using the titles Augusta and Augustus.
--- fact 3
Some coins struck in 271 and 272 show Aurelian on one side and Vaballathus on the other, so her claim stood, for a time, inside the Roman order rather than against it.
--- fact 4
Aurelian defeated her armies in 272 at Immae and Emesa and took her captive. Palmyra was sacked in 273, after a revolt there.
--- fact 5
Her fate is unknown. One tradition holds that she was displayed in Aurelian's triumph in 274; its source, the *Historia Augusta*, is often unreliable.
--- context
Zenobia's revolt against the [[culture/romans|Romans]] took place in the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and Palmyra's wealth rested on caravan trade, part of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Zenobia governed a trading city caught between two large powers, and that her reach, from Egypt to central Anatolia, grew while Rome was weak and shrank within a few years once Rome recovered. Most personal detail about her comes from Roman writers who defeated her, and later writers who embellished her. The reading of her as a rebel would weaken if inscriptions and coins show she sought a place within the Roman order rather than against it, as some historians argue.
--- question 1
Did she seek independence from Rome, or simply a share of its power?

=== person/zheng-he
title: Zheng He
type: person
date: c. 1371 – 1433
themes: trade, war, science
src: e48f5d1ab35c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Zheng He, who lived from 1371 to 1433, was a Chinese admiral who led seven large naval expeditions between 1405 and 1433, reaching Southeast Asia, India, Arabia and East Africa.
--- fact 1
He served under the Ming emperor Yongle, and was himself a Muslim of Yunnan origin.
--- fact 2
His fleets included very large ships, crewed by thousands.
--- fact 3
The voyages displayed Ming power and collected tribute, before they were stopped.
--- fact 4
They came decades ahead of European ocean voyages of the same kind.
--- context
Zheng He's voyages linked [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] and the western Indian Ocean during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a counterfactual worth studying: a state that commanded a large fleet chose to stop exploring. What a society refrains from doing can reveal as much as what it does.
--- question 1
Why did the state choose to halt the voyages?

=== technology/airplane
title: Airplane
type: technology
date: 1903
themes: technology
src: bc5e3d2fdc75
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In 1903, a powered fixed-wing aircraft achieved documented, sustained, controlled flight with a pilot aboard, through the work of Orville and Wilbur Wright.
--- fact 1
On 17 December 1903, near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, the Wrights made four flights in the Wright Flyer.
--- fact 2
The first flight lasted 12 seconds; the fourth, flown by Wilbur, lasted 59 seconds and covered about 260 meters, or 852 feet.
--- fact 3
In 1901 they built a wind tunnel in Dayton and tested model wings there, having found the published lift tables unreliable.
--- fact 4
Their 1902 glider was controlled in pitch, roll and yaw, using wing-warping for roll; a patent followed in 1906.
--- fact 5
Santos-Dumont's flight near Paris on 23 October 1906 was the first publicly witnessed, certified powered flight in Europe.
--- fact 6
Claims made for Gustave Whitehead, in Connecticut in 1901, and Richard Pearse, in New Zealand in 1902 to 1903, rest on disputed press reports and later eyewitness accounts, and most historians do not accept them.
--- context
The Flyer relied on a light [[technology/internal-combustion-engine|Internal Combustion Engine]]. Aircraft entered military use during [[event/world-war-i|World War I]], first for reconnaissance and aerial photography, and civil aviation grew in the years after. Aerospace engineering later fed into the [[event/space-age|Space Age]]. Early flight work spanned [[region/north-america|North America]] and Europe in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Thread: [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The airplane reads as an inherited chain of tools: the species joined a light engine, propellers, lift data and control methods, some borrowed and some new, and the Wrights' own distinctive step was control. Within roughly a decade the same technology also served sanctioned harm as well as scouting. This reading weakens if earlier controlled flights were documented, or if gradual accumulation — Lilienthal, Langley and others — explains the result better than any single 1903 threshold.
--- question 1
How much did Whitehead and Pearse actually achieve, if anything at all?
--- question 2
How far did earlier glider work go on to shape the Wrights' own methods?

=== technology/alphabet
title: Alphabet
type: technology
date: c. 1800 BCE
themes: language, technology
src: 7214995af8c1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The alphabet is a writing system in which signs stand for individual sounds. The first alphabet, Proto-Sinaitic, appeared around 1800 BCE and gave rise to most of the alphabets in use today.
--- fact 1
Semitic-speaking workers adapted it from Egyptian hieroglyphs.
--- fact 2
The Phoenician alphabet, about 1050 BCE, had about 22 letters, all of them consonants.
--- fact 3
The Greeks added vowels in the 8th century BCE, and the Latin alphabet descends from one Greek form of it.
--- fact 4
Hebrew, Arabic, Cyrillic and many Indian scripts also descend from, or relate to, these early alphabets.
--- context
The alphabet follows the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] and spread outward through the [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a system of about two dozen signs made literacy something learnable in months, not years. Simplicity made writing available to far more people.
--- question 1
Who actually invented the first alphabet?

=== technology/anesthesia
title: Anesthesia
type: technology
date: 1846
themes: science
src: b92765c96f1a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anesthesia is the use of drugs or vapors to block pain and consciousness during surgery. General anesthesia spread rapidly around the world after a public ether demonstration in 1846.
--- fact 1
William T. G. Morton publicly demonstrated ether anesthesia at Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston, on 16 October 1846, and is credited with launching its rapid worldwide adoption.
--- fact 2
Priority is contested: the Georgia physician Crawford Long used ether in minor surgery on 30 March 1842, years earlier, but did not publish the case until 1849.
--- fact 3
The Japanese surgeon Hanaoka Seishu performed surgery under a general anesthetic, an herbal compound called tsusensan, in 1804, decades earlier still, though the work stayed isolated within Japan.
--- fact 4
Horace Wells demonstrated nitrous oxide's pain relief in December 1844; his public Harvard demonstration in January 1845 failed, from an inadequate dose.
--- fact 5
Ether anesthesia crossed to Europe within weeks: Robert Liston carried out the first such public operation in London on 21 December 1846.
--- context
Surgical anesthesia grew out of the era's expanding [[theme/science|Science]], part of the technical change of the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Morton's 1846 demonstration in [[region/north-america|North America]] was taken up within weeks in Europe, and it found its first mass use during the [[event/american-civil-war|American Civil War]], alongside contemporaries such as [[technology/vaccination|Vaccination]] among the century's medical advances.
--- observer
This looks like one line of technique that surfaced independently along more than one branch of the human story, though only one branch fed into the modern global chain: we tend to credit whichever branch achieved the widest publicity, not the earliest instance. This weakens if unrecorded contact between the branches is found; an alternative reading treats them as separate, independent inventions.
--- question 1
Why did Hanaoka's method never spread beyond Japan, despite so many documented cases treated?
--- question 2
How much did Long's late publication cost him the credit for priority, relative to Morton?
