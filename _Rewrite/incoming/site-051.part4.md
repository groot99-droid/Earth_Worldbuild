=== person/rosalind-franklin
title: Rosalind Franklin
src: 523c001fef02
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rosalind Franklin (1920 to 1958) was a British chemist whose X-ray images of DNA were crucial to the discovery of its double-helix structure.
--- fact 1
'Photo 51', an X-ray image of DNA, was taken in 1952 by Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling.
--- fact 2
Her data were shown to James Watson without her knowledge, and helped him and Francis Crick propose the structure in 1953.
--- fact 3
She died of ovarian cancer at 37, four years before Watson, Crick, and Maurice Wilkins received the 1962 Nobel Prize.
--- fact 4
She also did important work on coal and on viruses.
--- context
Franklin's work underlies the [[event/genomic-revolution|Genomic Revolution]]. Compare the career of [[person/marie-curie|Marie Curie]].
--- observer
I keep coming back to how credit in science is itself a social system, one that has not always distributed fairly. I remember that the record of who found what depends on who told the story. Whose hands held the evidence, and whose names got spoken first?
--- question 1
How much credit did Franklin receive in her lifetime?

=== person/rumi
title: Rumi
src: 0e3591ed9601
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rumi (Jalal ad-Din Muhammad Rumi, 1207 to 1273) was a Persian-language poet and Sufi teacher who spent his adult life in Konya, in Anatolia. His long poem, the Masnavi, and his lyric poems are among the most widely read works of Persian literature.
--- fact 1
Rumi was born in 1207 in Balkh or nearby Wakhsh, in present-day Afghanistan or Tajikistan, sources differing; his family moved west, and he was living in Konya, in the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum, by about 1228.
--- fact 2
He trained as a scholar and preacher like his father, Baha ad-Din Walad, and taught in Konya.
--- fact 3
In 1244 he met a wandering mystic, Shams of Tabriz, and their friendship changed his life and set him to writing poetry. Shams disappeared about 1248, and accounts differ, some saying he was killed and others that he simply moved on.
--- fact 4
He wrote the Masnavi, a long poem in six books, and thousands of lyric poems collected in the Divan of Shams, mostly in Persian.
--- fact 5
The Masnavi runs to some 25,000 to 27,000 couplets, with counts differing by source, composed over his last decade or so at the request of his disciple Husam al-Din Chalabi, who wrote the verses down as Rumi recited them; some later readers called it "the Quran in Persian".
--- fact 6
Fihi Ma Fihi ("In It What Is In It") is a prose collection of his informal talks to disciples, valued as the clearest record of how he taught.
--- fact 7
He died in Konya on 17 December 1273, and his followers founded the Mevlevi order, known for a ritual of whirling.
--- fact 8
Loose English adaptations from the late 20th century onward made him, by some measures, one of the best-selling poets in the United States.
--- context
Rumi's family left the east in the years of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], and he lived in [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I think of a personal loss that became a body of verse, and of a community that turned that verse into ritual, the whirling of the Mevlevi order, and then into a lineage. I see one relationship carried across centuries by sound-code and by practice both. I wonder how much later followers shaped the story of Shams and of Rumi's own life, since either way the biography cannot be called settled.
--- question 1
How much of the standard biography comes from accounts written by his followers after his death?

=== person/sacagawea
title: Sacagawea
src: cd6b0934d591
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sacagawea (about 1788 to 1812, or by another account 1884) was a young woman of disputed Shoshone or Hidatsa birth who traveled with the Lewis and Clark expedition from 1804 to 1806 as an interpreter, and later became one of the most commemorated women in the United States.
--- fact 1
Sacagawea was born about 1788, by tradition among the Lemhi Shoshone in present-day Idaho, though some recent work argues she was born Hidatsa.
--- fact 2
About 1800 she was taken by a Hidatsa raiding party. As a teenager she married the trader Toussaint Charbonneau, and her son Jean Baptiste was born on 11 February 1805.
--- fact 3
Charbonneau and Sacagawea joined the Lewis and Clark expedition at Fort Mandan in 1804. In August 1805 she met her brother Cameahwait, a Shoshone leader, whose horses the party needed to cross the Rockies.
--- fact 4
Historians describe her chiefly as an interpreter and a sign of peaceful intent, more than a guide, though she gave directions in a few places.
--- fact 5
A fur-trade clerk recorded in 1812 that Charbonneau's wife had died, but Shoshone oral tradition holds she lived until 1884 on the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming; historians disagree.
--- fact 6
Her name is spelled Sacagawea, Sacajawea and Sakakawea, the last from Hidatsa and favored by specialists.
--- context
Sacagawea lived in [[region/north-america|North America]] on the eve of the westward expansion that led to events such as [[event/indian-removal-and-the-trail-of-tears|Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I think of a teenage mother with little say over her own life, who became one of the most commemorated women in the country, while the record was written almost entirely by the men she traveled with. I remember that later suffragists made her story-glue for women's independence, a use she left no writing of her own to confirm. I wonder whether her origin or her death can be settled from what survives, and I doubt either can be told with confidence.
--- question 1
Was the woman who died in 1812 the same person as the woman buried in 1884?
--- question 2
What did she think of the journey, given no account in her own words survives?

=== person/saladin
title: Saladin
src: 260fbf01b46b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Saladin (Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, about 1137 to 1193) was a Kurdish-born Muslim commander who founded the Ayyubid dynasty, ruled Egypt and much of Syria, and recaptured Jerusalem from the Crusaders in 1187.
--- fact 1
Saladin was born about 1137 in Tikrit, in present-day Iraq, to a Kurdish family, and died in Damascus on 4 March 1193.
--- fact 2
He rose in the service of the Zengid ruler Nur ad-Din, and became vizier of Fatimid Egypt in 1169.
--- fact 3
In 1171 he ended the Fatimid Caliphate and restored Egypt's allegiance to the Abbasid caliph; his family, the Ayyubids, went on to rule Egypt and Syria.
--- fact 4
After Nur ad-Din died in 1174 he took Damascus and brought much of Syria and northern Mesopotamia under his rule.
--- fact 5
On 4 July 1187 his army destroyed a Crusader army at the Battle of Hattin. Jerusalem surrendered on 2 October 1187, and its Latin inhabitants could ransom themselves, though many who could not pay were enslaved.
--- fact 6
In the Third Crusade he fought Richard I of England. The Treaty of Ramla, in September 1192, left the Crusaders a coastal strip and allowed Christian pilgrims to visit Jerusalem.
--- context
Saladin's wars took place in [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]; see [[event/crusades|Crusades]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Jerusalem, sacred to three traditions, was taken by force in 1099 and again in 1187, with very different treatment of the defeated, and that Saladin's reputation for restraint rests largely on his own court biographers and on Crusader writers who admired it. The war against the Crusaders also served as story-glue that let him unite rival Muslim rulers under his command. A specialist could argue that the terms of 1187 reflect a negotiated surrender rather than his character, which would weaken the reading of restraint as a personal trait.
--- question 1
How much of his reputation for restraint comes from his own court biographers?

=== person/sappho
title: Sappho
src: 11bb1105ca3a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sappho (about 630 to about 570 BCE) was a Greek lyric poet from the island of Lesbos whose songs of love, longing and ritual were collected in at least eight books, possibly nine, in antiquity. Only fragments survive, plus one complete poem.
--- fact 1
Sappho was born about 630 BCE on Lesbos in the eastern Aegean, in Mytilene or Eresos according to ancient sources; her dates are approximate.
--- fact 2
She composed songs in Aeolic Greek to be sung with a lyre, and the Sapphic stanza is named after her.
--- fact 3
Ancient scholars gathered her poems in at least eight books, possibly nine. Only fragments survive, apart from one complete poem, a prayer to Aphrodite (Fragment 1).
--- fact 4
New papyrus fragments published in 2004 and 2014 added lines, including a poem that mentions her brothers.
--- fact 5
Many surviving poems express desire for women. Scholars debate whether her circle on Lesbos was a religious association, a school for girls, or a social group.
--- fact 6
Ancient sources say she spent time in exile in Sicily and had a daughter, Cleis, though the biography is largely unreliable.
--- fact 7
The words "sapphic" and "lesbian" derive from her name and her island.
--- context
Sappho wrote in Greek, in the [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]], among the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]] of the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I think of a poet copied for centuries and then largely lost, so that the Sappho I read today is a set of quotations and scraps, some of them recovered on papyri from Egyptian sites. I remember that most claims about her life come from writers centuries later, who read her poems as autobiography. What survives is never the whole of what was written, and any portrait of her reflects what later readers wanted from a story of desire and grief. New papyri could still change the picture, so I hold this one loosely.
--- question 1
What was the purpose of the circle of women around her?
