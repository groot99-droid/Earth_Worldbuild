=== people--murasaki-shikibu--1
src: 5c9936c783c6
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Murasaki Shikibu (about 973 to about 1014) was a lady-in-waiting at the Japanese imperial court in the Heian period. She wrote The Tale of Genji, a 54-chapter novel of court life that is widely called the world's first novel.

"Murasaki Shikibu" is a later nickname. Her personal name is unknown, lost like a petal on a stream; the nickname joins a character from her novel to her father's court rank.

She was born about 973 into a minor branch of the Fujiwara clan, and she learned the Chinese classics, normally reserved for men, by taking in the lessons given to her brother.

About 998 she married a much older provincial governor and was widowed within a few years. About 1005 or 1006 she entered court service as a lady-in-waiting to Empress Shōshi.

The Tale of Genji follows a fictional prince and, in its later chapters, his descendants. It may have been begun before she came to court, though sources suggest she likely composed it while serving as a lady-in-waiting, possibly chapter by chapter.

She wrote almost entirely in hiragana, the script tied to women's writing at court, and set many waka poems into the story as the characters' own compositions.

=== people--ibn-sina--1
src: c46a819d05fb
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980 to 1037) was a Persian physician and philosopher whose 'Canon of Medicine' stood for centuries as a standard medical text in the Islamic world and in Europe.

He was born near Bukhara in Central Asia.

The Canon of Medicine (completed about 1025) set medical knowledge in systematic order, like stars charted into constellations.

Translated into Latin, it was used in European universities into the 17th century.

His philosophical 'Book of Healing' also shaped medieval Europe.

=== people--harald-hardrada--1
src: 7d51c19f8306
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Harald Hardrada (about 1015 to 1066) was King of Norway from 1046 to 1066, a warrior-king whose road ran from exile through mercenary service in Byzantium to the throne, and whose death at Stamford Bridge is often used to mark the end of the Viking Age.

After his half-brother King Olaf II fell in battle in 1030, the teenage Harald fled Norway and in time served as a commander in the Byzantine Empire's elite Varangian Guard, campaigning in the Mediterranean and Middle East.

He came back to Scandinavia around 1045 with substantial wealth, won a share of the Norwegian throne in 1046, and became sole king when his co-ruler died in 1047.

For years he waged inconclusive war on Denmark over rival claims, making peace at last in 1064 (or 1065 according to Morkinskinna) without the Danish throne he sought.

In September 1066 he invaded England to press a rival claim to its throne, and was defeated and killed at the Battle of Stamford Bridge by King Harold Godwinson, days before Godwinson had to march south to face William of Normandy's invasion at Hastings, one storm breaking just before the next.

=== people--li-qingzhao--2
src: 090a1a5a8c1d
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Li Qingzhao (1084 to about 1155) was a Chinese poet of the Song dynasty, widely regarded as the greatest woman writer in the classical Chinese tradition. Her ci, a lyric verse form, passed from a settled, cultured life into loss and exile after northern China fell to invaders, as a river leaves the garden for the gorge.

She was born in 1084 in Qi Prefecture, today's Zhangqiu in Shandong, to a literary family, and is thought to have died about 1155.

In 1101 she married Zhao Mingcheng, a scholar who shared her love of antiquities. The Jinshi lu, a catalog of bronze and stone inscriptions, is described as her husband's work and was published after his death.

When the Jin dynasty invaded northern China in 1127, the couple fled south with part of their collection, much of which is said to have been lost along the way. Zhao Mingcheng died in 1129.

After his death she wrote an afterword to the Jinshi lu telling of their marriage and their losses. She briefly remarried, the year traditionally given as about 1132, and divorced amid scandal soon after.

Her poems, mostly short ci in personal, direct language, tell of love, separation, and later grief and displacement. Only around a hundred are known to survive, out of a larger body gathered and praised by later editors.
