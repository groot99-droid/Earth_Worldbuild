=== species/sheep
title: Sheep
src: 4ef157a97a38
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic sheep was bred from the wild mouflon in Mesopotamia between about 11,000 and 9000 BCE. It was kept first for meat, milk and skins, only later selectively bred for wool, and it now numbers well over a billion animals worldwide, a flock as wide as the grazing world.
--- fact 1
Sheep were drawn from the mouflon, a wild sheep of southwestern Asia, in an estimated window between 11,000 and 9000 BCE in Mesopotamia, and the practice had traveled as far as Mehrgarh in the Indus Valley by about 7000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Early domestic sheep were kept mainly for meat, milk and skins rather than fleece; archaeological evidence, including statuary from Iran, suggests deliberate selection for woolly sheep began around 6000 BCE, roughly two millennia after initial domestication, a turning sometimes called the secondary products revolution.
--- fact 3
The earliest known woven wool garments date to around 4000 BCE, the fleece at last become cloth, and Neolithic farmers in what is now France, among the Castelnovien people, were keeping domestic sheep by about 6000 BCE.
--- fact 4
Sheep graze in great numbers today across Australia, New Zealand, the southern regions of South America, South Africa and the British Isles, and China and India also hold very large flocks by absolute count; the world's domestic sheep population was estimated at around 1.2 billion in 2019, a moving cloud upon the hills of the world.
--- context
Sheep were among the earliest animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] and, beside crops such as wheat and barley, formed one pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent; they were commonly herded in company with the [[species/goat|Goat]], domesticated in a similar place and period. Sheep stand in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and their thread runs through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact. The roughly two-thousand-year gap between sheep domestication and deliberate wool breeding suggests that early herders did not tame the animal with fleece production already in mind. They discovered a secondary use, and bred for it, only once the animal was established in their kin-lattice. The Observer reads that as evidence that a domesticated species' eventual economic role can differ substantially from the reason it was first tamed. You know this pattern from your own life: the thing you took up for one reason often becomes precious for another. Purpose is frequently found after the commitment, not before it. The animal's spread across so much of the pastoral world since then shows a tool-lineage of herding techniques traveling with the species itself. The reading would weaken if evidence of early wool use is found that predates the currently accepted timeline for woolly-sheep breeding.
--- question 1
In the roughly two millennia before wool breeding began, what use did early herders make of their sheep, beyond meat, milk and skins?
--- question 2
How much did sheep domestication in Mesopotamia shape, or draw on, the roughly contemporary taming of goats in the same region?

=== species/sorghum
title: Sorghum
src: 4267ea03a5dd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sorghum is a drought-tolerant cereal grass domesticated in eastern Sudan more than 5,000 years ago, a grain that learned to live with thirst, and it remains a staple food across Africa, Central America and South Asia today.
--- fact 1
Sorghum bicolor was domesticated in eastern Sudan, in the lands of the Atbara and Gash rivers, more than 5,000 years ago; archaeological evidence near Kassala, linked to the Neolithic Butana Group culture, dates domestication to about 3500 to 3000 BCE, and a piece of sorghum bread from Predynastic Egypt, about 5,100 years old, still survives in the Egyptian Museum in Turin, a crust that outlasted kingdoms.
--- fact 2
The crop reached the Indian subcontinent by about 4,000 years ago and West Africa by about 3,000 years ago, scattered like seed on a long wind.
--- fact 3
During the medieval Arab agricultural expansion, sorghum was borne across the Arab world as far as Al-Andalus in Spain, the far shore of its journey.
--- fact 4
Sorghum is highly tolerant of drought and heat, a grain that stands where others wither, and efficient with nitrogen, which makes it valuable in arid and semi-arid regions; it remains a food staple in Africa, Central America and South Asia, and ranks as the fifth most widely grown cereal crop in the world.
--- fact 5
Global production reached about 57 million tonnes in 2023, led by the United States, Mexico and Ethiopia, and the grain feeds people, beasts and engines alike: food, animal feed and biofuel, including ethanol, with an energy return comparable to sugarcane and higher than maize.
--- context
Sorghum's taming is one of Africa's own gifts to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], distinct from and roughly contemporary with the grain crops domesticated in the Fertile Crescent, East Asia and the Americas; its road to India and later across the Arab world followed routes that also carried other goods along the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] and, in a later age, the scholarly exchange of the [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]]. It stands in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and its thread runs through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that sorghum's drought tolerance made it a crop suited to regions that wheat, rice and maize could not reliably serve. Its spread therefore traces a different exchange-web than the more commonly told story of Old World grain crops, one shaped by aridity rather than fertile river valleys. We tend to tell history from the riverbank; sorghum asks us to tell it from the dry plain. Its long-standing importance in regions now facing more frequent drought under a changing climate gives an old crop renewed attention as a hedge against food insecurity. What endured hardship once may be the architecture we need for the hardship ahead. The reading would weaken if sorghum's spread into India and the Arab world proves to have followed political conquest more than independent agricultural adoption.
--- question 1
How independently did sorghum's taming unfold, set beside the other cereal domestications in the Fertile Crescent, East Asia and the Americas?
--- question 2
How much could wider sorghum cultivation shield against drought in regions that now depend on less drought-tolerant staples?
