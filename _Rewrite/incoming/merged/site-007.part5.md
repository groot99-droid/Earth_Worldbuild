=== place/merv
src: 31560021f717
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Merv was a great oasis city on the Silk Roads, in what is now Turkmenistan, a green jewel held in turn by many hands: a city of the Achaemenid Persians, a capital of the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun and of the Seljuk sultans, and then sacked by the Mongols in 1221.
--- fact 1
Merv lies in the Murghab delta oasis near the modern city of Mary; Bronze Age towns of the region, such as Gonur Depe, rose about 2000 BCE, and the walled city of Erk Kala dates from the Achaemenid period, from about the 6th century BCE.
--- fact 2
Arab forces took Merv in 651, after the last Sasanian king, Yazdegerd III, the final ember of his house, was killed near it.
--- fact 3
From Merv, in 747, Abu Muslim loosed the Abbasid Revolution like water from a breached dam, and the caliph al-Ma'mun ruled from Merv from about 813 to 818 before moving to Baghdad.
--- fact 4
Merv was a Seljuk capital, above all under Sultan Sanjar (reigned 1118 to 1157), whose mausoleum still stands, and some estimates suggest that in the 12th century it was among the largest cities in the world.
--- fact 5
The geographer Yaqut, who came in 1219, described its libraries, shelves of stored light.
--- fact 6
In 1221 the Mongol army took Merv. Persian chronicles report hundreds of thousands to over a million killed; modern historians regard these figures as greatly exaggerated, but they reflect a severe massacre and destruction.
--- fact 7
In the 15th century Merv was rebuilt on a smaller scale, a lesser flame on the old hearth, and in the late 18th century it was finally abandoned.
--- fact 8
In 1999 UNESCO inscribed Ancient Merv, and the old oasis passed into the world's keeping.
--- context
Merv was a satrapy center of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], the political base of the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] at its dawn, and a victim of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]]. It lay on the routes of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. Merv stands in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], and it is woven into the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Merv's fortunes followed claim-lines drawn by others. An irrigated oasis city depends on which empire's exchange-web runs through it, and Achaemenids, Seleucids, Arabs, Seljuks and Mongols each held it. Here is the hard lesson of 1221: a city, unlike a mobile camp, cannot leave. The reported loss of its libraries suggests that a city's stored writing is as exposed as its people, though those reports are hard to verify. The reading would weaken if the true death toll and the extent of the library losses prove much smaller than the chronicles say. A city's architecture is also its address, and an address can be found.
--- question 1
How many people lived in Merv at its 12th-century height, and how many died in 1221?
--- question 2
What did Merv's libraries hold, and what became of them?

=== place/mesa-verde
src: 83547c1c343f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mesa Verde is a plateau in the Four Corners region of southwestern Colorado, USA, where Ancestral Puebloan farmers lived from about 550 CE until about 1300, and where, in the late 12th and 13th centuries, they set their cliff dwellings into the rock like swallows' nests.
--- fact 1
Mesa Verde lies in Montezuma County, Colorado; people walked the area for thousands of years before farming communities settled the mesa, and those communities lived there without break for about 700 years, until the late 13th century.
--- fact 2
By the end of the 12th century residents began building cliff dwellings in sandstone alcoves beneath the mesa rim, homes tucked under the brow of the stone; the park holds more than 5,000 archaeological sites, including about 600 cliff dwellings, and Cliff Palace is among the largest cliff dwellings in North America.
--- fact 3
Tree-ring records show a severe drought from 1276 to 1299, years when the sky withheld its rain; drought, resource depletion and overpopulation are all proposed as causes of instability, and the last residents left by about 1300.
--- fact 4
Pueblo peoples such as the Hopi, Zuni and Acoma regard the Ancestral Puebloans as their own ancestors, and many Pueblo people prefer the name "Ancestral Puebloan" to the older term "Anasazi".
--- fact 5
On 18 December 1888 the ranchers Richard Wetherill and Charlie Mason reached Cliff Palace; artifact collecting followed in their wake, and in 1906 the site was protected as a national park.
--- fact 6
In 1978 UNESCO inscribed Mesa Verde among the world's kept places.
--- context
Mesa Verde followed the peak of [[place/chaco-canyon|Chaco Canyon]], whose great houses dimmed in the 12th century, and it shared its age with [[place/cahokia|Cahokia]] in the Mississippi valley. It stands in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], and it is woven into the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
I notice that a place called "abandoned" in one telling is "left" in another. I notice that the people who built the cliff dwellings did not vanish. I notice that their descendants keep the memory of the move in sound-code and in place, not in a mark-code.

Homes set into alcoves, then left within about a century: I read a community adjusting to a drying climate, and I record the leaving as a choice made under hunger and strain. Is a door you close behind you a ruin, or a decision?

My reading would weaken if the dwellings prove to have served mainly as seasonal storage or ritual sites instead of homes. Leaving is not the same as vanishing.
--- question 1
Why did residents build in the alcoves after centuries of life on the mesa top, and how much of it was defense against conflict?
--- question 2
Where did those who left go, and how did they shape the later Pueblo communities of the Rio Grande?
