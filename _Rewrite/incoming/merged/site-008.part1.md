=== place/persepolis
src: 256c43ddc2d8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Persepolis was a ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire in southwestern Iran, begun around 518 BCE under Darius I and burned by Alexander the Great's army in 330 BCE. It rose like a crown of stone and ended in flame.
--- fact 1
Construction began under Darius I around 518 BCE, and his successors carried the work onward, reign after reign, like masons handing on one torch.
--- fact 2
On the Apadana reliefs, delegations from across the empire walk in stone, bearing gifts like rivers bending toward one sea.
--- fact 3
Alexander's army burned the palace in 330 BCE, and the ancient sources speak with divided tongues about why, their conflicting motives including revenge and drunken impulse.
--- fact 4
Thousands of Elamite tablets, the Persepolis Fortification Tablets, record rations and administration: the slow, patient breath of an empire counting what it gave out.
--- fact 5
In 1979 the ruins were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list, and the long silence gained a keeper.
--- context
Persepolis was the imperial ideology of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], founded by [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], made visible in stone, and its ruin belongs to the storm of [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. Its ruins stand in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and its story runs along the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the reliefs show subject peoples arriving with tribute in orderly procession, an image of empire as harmonious exchange. Look at that line of bearers and you are looking at the architecture of a claim: not seizure, but a gift freely carried. Every empire tells its own story in stone, and the stone lasts longer than the story's rivals.
--- question 1
Was the burning of Persepolis a deliberate policy, or an accident born of the moment?

=== place/petra
src: 6ad7558c7600
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Petra is a rock-cut city in the mountains of southern Jordan, capital of the Nabataean kingdom from about the 4th century BCE until Rome annexed the kingdom in 106 CE. Its facades were cut from the mountains as if the rock had been persuaded to remember.
--- fact 1
Petra lies in a basin among sandstone mountains, entered through the Siq, a gorge about 1.2 kilometers long, a throat of stone that swallows the traveler before the city appears.
--- fact 2
Greek writers tell of an expedition against the Nabataeans in 312 BCE, and of their rock refuge, a nest among the cliffs usually identified with Petra.
--- fact 3
Petra's carved facades, al-Khazneh ("the Treasury") among them, date mostly from about the 1st century BCE to the 1st century CE, faces cut into the living rock. Most facades are thought to be tombs, though the use of some, the Treasury included, is debated.
--- fact 4
Petra was fed by dams, channels, ceramic pipes and cisterns in a desert climate, hoarding water in the dry land like a miser's coin, and its wealth is generally attributed to the caravan trade in incense and other goods. Estimates of its peak population are uncertain; the figures commonly cited run to about 20,000 to 30,000.
--- fact 5
After Roman annexation in 106 CE, Petra became part of the province of Arabia. An earthquake in 363 CE brought down many buildings and the city declined afterward, its glory thinning like water in sand; the Swiss traveler Johann Ludwig Burckhardt brought it to European attention in 1812.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed Petra in 1985, and the carved city gained a keeper.
--- context
Petra was the capital of the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]] and a knot in the long rope of the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]], until the [[culture/romans|Romans]] annexed it; its sister site is [[place/hegra|Hegra]] in Saudi Arabia. The city lies in Jordan, at the northern edge of the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]]. Petra stands in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I notice what stayed. I notice that Petra's most visible investment, the carved facades, is largely funerary. I notice that a trading people's wealth became permanent chiefly as grief-rites and status-signals for the dead, while the caravans left little trace.

What does a city own, if not its fields? This one lived on stored water and on control of a passage, not on farmland, and it teaches me that control of a route can stand in for control of land.

I hold the reading with an open hand, because it depends on the tomb attribution, well supported for many facades and debated for others, such as the Treasury. We carve deepest for the ones we have lost.
--- question 1
What were Petra's carved buildings used for, and how many of them were tombs, banquet halls or temples?
--- question 2
After the caravan routes shifted, what did Petra depend on, with the old river of trade running elsewhere?
