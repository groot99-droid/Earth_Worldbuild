=== person/murasaki-shikibu
title: Murasaki Shikibu
src: 99f86c262d51
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Murasaki Shikibu (about 973 to about 1014) served as a lady-in-waiting at the Japanese imperial court of the Heian period, and wrote The Tale of Genji, a 54-chapter novel of court life widely called the world's first novel.
--- fact 1
"Murasaki Shikibu" is itself a later nickname; her personal name is unknown, and the nickname combines a character from her own novel with her father's court rank.
--- fact 2
She was born about 973 into a minor branch of the Fujiwara clan, and learned the Chinese classics, normally reserved for men, by absorbing the lessons given to her brother.
--- fact 3
She married a much older provincial governor about 998 and was widowed within a few years; about 1005 or 1006 she entered court service as a lady-in-waiting to Empress Shōshi.
--- fact 4
The Tale of Genji follows a fictional prince and, in its later chapters, his descendants; it may have been begun before she came to court, though sources suggest she likely composed it while serving as a lady-in-waiting, possibly chapter by chapter.
--- fact 5
She wrote almost entirely in hiragana, the script associated with women's writing at court, and set many waka poems into the story as compositions belonging to her own characters.
--- fact 6
She also kept The Diary of Lady Murasaki, which records court life and its rivalries. She is thought to have died about 1014, though the date, like most of her biography, remains uncertain.
--- fact 7
The earliest surviving illustrated version, the 12th-century Genji Monogatari Emaki, is a Japanese National Treasure, and she appears on the 2000 yen banknote.
--- context
Murasaki wrote at the Heian court within [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I think of the earliest long prose fiction on record, and I think of a woman writing it in the very script her society had assigned to women, while the mark-code of official record, Chinese characters, belonged only to men. The formal script carried authority; the informal one carried private feeling. I remember that the novel circulated as copied and illustrated scrolls, memory-outsourcing put to the work of interior life rather than law or scripture. Whether it counts as the first novel depends on how the term itself is defined, and her biography rests mostly on her own diary and scattered court records — so I hold this reading loosely. A script built to exclude a woman became the one she used to outlast her century.
--- question 1
How much of The Tale of Genji, if any, was written before she entered court service?

=== person/nefertiti
title: Nefertiti
src: 03caaf3b749e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nefertiti (about 1370 to about 1330 BCE) was the Great Royal Wife of the Egyptian king Akhenaten, and is shown with unusual prominence in the art of his reign; her exact birth and death dates and her burial place remain uncertain, and the dates given here are approximate.
--- fact 1
Nefertiti married Akhenaten, then still called Amenhotep IV, early in his reign; the exact dates of that marriage are uncertain.
--- fact 2
The couple promoted worship of the sun disk Aten, and moved the royal capital to a new city, Akhetaten, now Amarna.
--- fact 3
Reliefs and statues depict Nefertiti at a scale, and in roles, usually reserved for the king, including a part in rites for the Aten.
--- fact 4
They had six daughters, among them Meritaten and Ankhesenamun, who later married Tutankhamun.
--- fact 5
Scholarship before 2012 assumed she vanished from the record around year 12 of the reign, but an inscription dated to year 16 names Neferneferuaten-Nefertiti alongside Akhenaten; whether she died, changed title, or ruled as co-regent under the name Neferneferuaten is debated.
--- fact 6
A painted limestone bust found in 1912, in the workshop of the sculptor Thutmose at Amarna, is now held in Berlin; Egypt has requested its return.
--- fact 7
Later king lists left out Akhenaten and his immediate successors.
--- context
Nefertiti's life is bound to [[person/akhenaten|Akhenaten]], among the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] of the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I look at one sculpture, and I see how it made her one of the best-known faces of antiquity, though the record beneath it covers only a short span that later rulers tried to scrub from the king lists. Her size in the reliefs reads as a status-signal, and I do not know whether it records real power or the couple's use of the royal family as a pair of divine images. This reading would weaken if evidence showed that such scale was simply the convention for queens of the period. A single face can outlive the whole reign that made it, and still tell us less than it seems to.
--- question 1
Did she rule after Akhenaten's death, and under what name? Egyptologists still debate this, and a year 16 inscription shows she was still recorded late in his reign.

=== person/nelson-mandela
title: Nelson Mandela
src: 41073a78d637
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Nelson Mandela (1918 to 2013), a South African anti-apartheid leader, spent 27 years in prison before becoming his country's first democratically elected president in 1994.
--- fact 1
He co-founded the African National Congress Youth League, and helped lead resistance to apartheid.
--- fact 2
From 1962 to 1990 he was held in prison.
--- fact 3
After his release he negotiated apartheid's end.
--- fact 4
He served as president from 1994 to 1999.
--- context
Mandela stands as a figure of the [[era/information-age|Information Age]] in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], his approach shaped by the example of [[person/mahatma-gandhi|Mahatma Gandhi]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a rare pattern: a leader who, once he held power, chose reconciliation over retribution. The species is capable, it turns out, of institutionalizing forgiveness as a political tool.
--- question 1
Which factors made South Africa's transition comparatively peaceful?

=== person/nezahualcoyotl
title: Nezahualcoyotl
src: 01e4dce3621d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Nezahualcoyotl (1402 to 1472) was the tlatoani, or ruler, of the Acolhua city-state of Texcoco in the Valley of Mexico, and a founder of the Triple Alliance with Tenochtitlan and Tlacopan; he is remembered as a lawgiver, builder and poet, though much of what is said about him comes from later writers.
--- fact 1
Nezahualcoyotl was born in 1402 in Texcoco, the son of the ruler Ixtlilxochitl I and a Mexica princess; after the Tepanec state of Azcapotzalco conquered Texcoco and killed his father, he lived in exile.
--- fact 2
With Tenochtitlan and Tlacopan he fought Azcapotzalco; its defeat, traditionally dated about 1428, created the Triple Alliance. He ruled Texcoco from about 1431 until 1472, when his son Nezahualpilli succeeded him.
--- fact 3
Later sources credit him with a law code and public works, among them a dike that protected Tenochtitlan from flooding and the gardens at Tetzcotzingo.
--- fact 4
About 34 poems are attributed to him in sixteenth-century manuscripts; whether he composed them is debated, since "his song" can equally mean a song about him.
--- fact 5
Accounts that he opposed human sacrifice and favored a single creator deity derive largely from his own descendant, Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxochitl, writing in the early seventeenth century, and are treated with caution.
--- context
Nezahualcoyotl ruled within [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. The Triple Alliance he helped found grew into the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]], centered on [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler whose rise rested on a military alliance is remembered chiefly through poems on the brevity of life. This verse, made in sound-code, survived the Conquest only because writers copied it into mark-code in the sixteenth century. A specialist could argue that both the poems and the portrait of him as a tolerant sage reflect the aims of those later writers, which would weaken any reading of him as an exceptional thinker.
--- question 1
Which of the poems attributed to him, if any, did he compose himself?
--- question 2
How much of the later picture of his laws and beliefs comes from his own descendants?

=== person/nur-jahan
title: Nur Jahan
src: 9ae4b28d7aec
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nur Jahan (1577 to 1645), a Mughal empress and wife of the emperor Jahangir, held great political power at court from about 1611 to 1627.
--- fact 1
Born Mehr-un-Nisa in 1577, Nur Jahan was the daughter of Mirza Ghiyas Beg, a Persian who served the Mughal court.
--- fact 2
Her first husband, Sher Afghan, died in 1607. She married Jahangir in 1611 and received the title Nur Mahal, then in 1616 the title Nur Jahan, "Light of the World."
--- fact 3
As Jahangir's health declined, she took an increasing share of government; coins were struck in her name, and imperial orders carried her seal.
--- fact 4
Her father became chief minister, and her brother Asaf Khan a leading noble, so her family held key posts.
--- fact 5
She commissioned her father's tomb at Agra, built between 1622 and 1628, an early Mughal building faced in marble with inlaid stone.
--- fact 6
In 1626 the general Mahabat Khan seized Jahangir, and she took a part in securing his release.
--- fact 7
After Jahangir died in 1627, her brother backed Shah Jahan, who became emperor. She was then placed under house arrest by her own brother, on Shah Jahan's orders, and lived in Lahore on an annual allowance until her death in 1645.
--- context
Nur Jahan's court belonged to the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. Her niece Mumtaz Mahal is commemorated by the [[place/taj-mahal|Taj Mahal]]. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I see a woman who held power through the kin-lattice — through marriage into the ruling house, and posts found for her father and her brother — rather than through any office carrying her own name. I remember coins struck with her name and a seal bearing her mark, the state itself turning her into a status-signal, while the chroniclers who wrote of her wrote from within that same court. This reading would weaken if evidence showed her reported power was overstated by writers looking for a woman to explain Jahangir's rule. Power borrowed through kinship can still be spent as though it were one's own.
--- question 1
How far did her real authority extend beyond what the court chronicles report?
