=== culture/jomon-culture
title: Jomon Culture
src: e0497cf9b502
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Jomon were hunter-gatherer-fishers of the Japanese archipelago who shaped some of the world's earliest pottery and lived in settled villages for about 14,000 years before rice farming arrived, a long and patient season among the islands.
--- fact 1
The name Jomon means cord-marked, after the patterns pressed into their pottery like the tracks of rain on soft earth.
--- fact 2
Some of the earliest Jomon pottery is dated to around 16,000 years ago, fired clay reaching across an ocean of years.
--- fact 3
They shaped clay figurines called dogu, small watchers made of earth.
--- fact 4
The site of Sannai-Maruyama in Aomori was occupied for about 1,700 years, from around 3900 BCE, its hearths kept across the generations.
--- fact 5
Rice-farming migrants, the Yayoi, arrived from the continent beginning around the first millennium BCE, like a new tide on an old shore, and the dates are debated.
--- context
The Jomon show that [[technology/pottery|Pottery]] and sedentary life came before [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in this region: the pot before the plough. Rice farming, whose tale is told in [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]], entered later. In the codex the Jomon stand in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region of [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and their threads are [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I was taught a ladder: fire, then farming, then settlement. I look at the Jomon and the ladder bends. I see permanent villages. I see elaborate pots. I see no farming. What else did I learn in the wrong order? The sequence of fire, farming and settlement is not universal. Settling does not require agriculture.
--- question 1
When the Jomon and the Yayoi met, how did their populations interact?

=== culture/kanem-bornu-empire
title: Kanem-Bornu Empire
src: 2dc5307667d2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kanem-Bornu was a long-lived Muslim state around Lake Chad, governed by the Sayfawa dynasty, that survived by shifting its centre from Kanem to Bornu, as a river shifts its bed and flows on, and lasted until 1893.
--- fact 1
Around 1396 the Bulala forced Mai Umar Idrismi to abandon Njimi and lead the Kanembu people to Bornu, on the western side of Lake Chad. Over time the Kanembu and the peoples of Bornu intermarried like two rivers meeting, and from them came a new people and language, the Kanuri.
--- fact 2
Holding both capitals, the Sayfawa dynasty grew more powerful than before, and political authority came to rest in Bornu like a bird settling on a new branch.
--- fact 3
The empire reached its height under Mai Idris Alooma (about 1571 to 1603), remembered for military skill, administrative reforms and Islamic piety. He brought in legal and administrative reforms based on Islamic law, sponsored mosques, and made a pilgrimage to Mecca, where he set up a hostel for pilgrims from his empire.
--- fact 4
His reforms and campaigns held the empire aloft until the mid-17th century, when its power began to fade like evening light. In 1808 Fulani warriors conquered the capital, Ngazargamu. The Sayfawa dynasty ended in 1846, and the empire itself fell in 1893.
--- context
Kanem-Bornu had the [[culture/songhai-empire|Songhai Empire]] as its neighbour to the west, and it was overtaken in part by the Fulani movement associated with [[person/usman-dan-fodio|Usman dan Fodio]] and the [[culture/sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]], a rising wind over the old realm.
--- observer
What do you do when you lose the ground you stand on? The Observer notes a state that survived an exile by moving its centre of gravity, and whose people fused with those who had displaced them into a new people. Notice which architecture carried the weight. Where the political story-glue failed at one capital, the kin-lattice held it together long enough to build another. Kinship can outlast a capital.
--- question 1
Given that the sources consulted here say little about it, how is the earliest period of Kanem known?
--- question 2
How did Kanem-Bornu's rulers handle their dealings with the Fulani states that arose around 1808?

=== culture/khmer-empire
title: Khmer Empire
src: a456045b49b7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Khmer Empire ruled much of mainland Southeast Asia from about 802 to 1431 CE, its capital at Angkor rising like a lotus from the waters.
--- fact 1
It was founded by Jayavarman II, the first stone laid.
--- fact 2
Angkor Wat rose in the early 12th century under Suryavarman II, a mountain shaped into prayer.
--- fact 3
The empire wove a large water-management network of reservoirs and canals, holding the rains as a cupped hand holds water.
--- fact 4
Its state religion turned between Hindu and Buddhist, as the moon turns between phases. The traditional end date of 1431 (the sack of Angkor) is debated, and Angkor Wat itself was never abandoned.
--- context
Its tale lives on in [[place/angkor|Angkor]] and across the lands of [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]].
--- observer
I stand between the temple and the reservoir and cannot tell where one ends. I see temples raised toward heaven. I see water led to the fields. I see one project, not two. Was the king's power shown here, or made here? Both: temples and irrigation both displayed and produced the king's power. What shows power can also make it.
--- question 1
What brought Angkor to its decline?

=== culture/kingdom-of-aksum
title: Kingdom of Aksum
src: 2942fb3c221e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Kingdom of Aksum was a trading empire in the highlands of present-day Ethiopia and Eritrea, a crown set on high ground, from about the 1st to the 10th century CE.
--- fact 1
It controlled the Red Sea trade between Rome, Arabia and India, a gatekeeper standing where the waters met.
--- fact 2
It minted its own coins and raised tall carved stone obelisks (stelae), fingers of stone pointing at the sky.
--- fact 3
In the 4th century King Ezana adopted Christianity, a new faith taken into the royal hand.
--- fact 4
The Persian prophet Mani listed Aksum as one of the four great powers of his time, beside Rome, Persia and China, four pillars holding up the known world.
--- context
Aksum was a bridge joining [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] to the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean worlds, and it stands as a counterexample to narratives that leave Africa out of the ancient record.
--- observer
I remember that its own contemporaries counted it among the four great powers. I remember that later retellings often forgot that. I remember how easily a name slips from the list. Who decides which greatness is remembered? Forgetting is also a kind of telling.
--- question 1
What made Aksum fall into decline?
