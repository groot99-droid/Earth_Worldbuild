=== observer-note/why-languages-die-and-why-some-spread
src: 99baa1b68981
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Of the thousands of languages humans have spoken, a handful now dominate global communication while most others have shrunk or vanished entirely. What decides which languages survive?
--- observer
I read a language's survival as resting less on anything in the language itself, its grammar, its words, than on the size, the power, and the staying power of the community of speakers and writers who keep it alive.

A language works as the shared story of its speakers, and when the structure holding that community together falls apart, scatters, or is forced into another people's mold, the language it carried can vanish within a few generations, with no single moment marking its end.

This reading would weaken if close study shows that a language's own features, how easy it is to learn, how its script is built, matter as much as outside political and demographic force in deciding whether it survives.
--- extra 1: Question
Of the thousands of languages humans have spoken, a handful now dominate global communication while most others have shrunk or vanished entirely. What decides which languages survive?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- The Mogao Caves preserved manuscripts in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Sogdian and other languages once spoken along Silk Road trade routes, several of which, including Sogdian, have no native speakers left today (East Asia, Medieval Period).
- Ancient Egyptian, once the administrative language of a major empire, fell out of everyday use so completely that its hieroglyphic script could only be recovered once the [[technology/rosetta-stone|Rosetta Stone]] supplied a bilingual key in the 19th century (Nile Valley, Classical Antiquity).
- Sequoyah created a complete syllabary for the Cherokee language in the early 19th century, a rare case of one person giving a previously unwritten language its own writing system, partly as a defense against cultural erasure (North America).
- The [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] carried Spanish and Portuguese across the Americas alongside crops, animals and diseases, and the resulting demographic collapse of Indigenous populations sharply cut the number of speakers of many American languages within a few generations.
- Languages backed by large populations, strong states, trade networks or colonial administration have tended to expand at the expense of languages spoken by smaller or politically weaker communities, though the historical record also holds exceptions where prestige languages later receded, as classical Latin did after the fall of Rome.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
This account stresses political and demographic power at the expense of the deliberate, sometimes successful efforts by small communities to preserve or revive their languages, as Sequoyah's syllabary itself shows. It also does not fully explain why some numerically small language communities have persisted for centuries under pressure while demographically larger ones have not.

=== observer-note/why-prophets-appear-in-clusters
src: 27c835d14ba2
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Between roughly 800 and 200 BCE, several unconnected regions of Eurasia each produced influential religious and ethical teachers within a few centuries of one another. Why would prophets and philosophers cluster in time, rather than appear at a steady, even rate across history?
--- observer
I read this clustering as a sign that growing trade and city life, by bringing people again and again into contact with strangers outside their own kin, put pressure on several regions at once for a belief that could speak to duty beyond one's own tribe or village, a need older, narrower views of the world answered less well.

That the same kind of movement came again later, with Muhammad centuries afterward, suggests to me that this pressure could return wherever conditions of contact and upheaval appeared again, not only once, by accident.

This reading would weaken if the timing of each tradition's rise turns out to match political crises particular to each region more closely than any shared condition running across them all.
--- extra 1: Question
Between roughly 800 and 200 BCE, several unconnected regions of Eurasia each produced influential religious and ethical teachers within a few centuries of one another. Why would prophets and philosophers cluster in time, rather than appear at a steady, even rate across history?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- In East Asia, Confucius developed an ethical system addressing how strangers, not only kin, should treat one another, during the disorder of late Zhou-era China.
- In South Asia, Siddhartha Gautama taught a path to liberation from suffering that addressed people regardless of caste or origin, in a period of growing cities and expanding trade.
- On the Iranian Plateau, the teachings gathered under [[culture/zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]] proposed a cosmic struggle between good and evil with implications for how any person, not only members of one tribe, should act.
- In the Levant and Anatolia, the prophetic tradition within [[culture/judaism|Judaism]] developed an ethical monotheism addressing a whole people's relationship to a single god and to justice among strangers.
- Centuries later, in the Arabian Peninsula, Muhammad's teachings addressed a similarly wide, cross-tribal audience, showing that the pattern of prophetic movements did not end with this one cluster (Medieval Period).
- Historians grouping these Iron Age developments together often use the term Axial Age to describe the cluster, noting that each tradition emerged independently amid growing cities, expanding trade networks and, in several cases, political instability.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
This account treats trade and city growth as the common cause but cannot fully explain why these particular individuals, rather than others facing similar conditions, became the ones remembered as founders. It also risks overstating how "independent" these traditions truly were, since some contact and exchange of ideas across Eurasia during this period cannot be ruled out even where direct evidence is lacking.
