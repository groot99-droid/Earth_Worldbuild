=== technology/digital-computer
title: Digital Computer
src: 28d3093d800f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The digital computer is a programmable machine that processes information as binary numbers. The ENIAC was completed in 1945, following earlier machines such as Zuse's Z3 (1941) and Colossus (1943).
--- fact 1
The transistor (1947) and the integrated circuit (about 1958) let computers shrink and grow cheaper.
--- fact 2
The microprocessor (1971) placed a whole computer on a single chip.
--- fact 3
Computing power has roughly doubled every two years, in the pattern known as Moore's law.
--- fact 4
Computers now run inside phones, cars, appliances, and networks.
--- context
The computer carries forward ideas from [[person/alan-turing|Alan Turing]] and [[person/ada-lovelace|Ada Lovelace]], and it drives the [[event/digital-revolution|Digital Revolution]].
--- observer
The Observer sees a general-purpose tool for manipulating symbols: the first machine able to imitate many other machines. It turns memory-outsourcing dynamic instead of static.
--- question 1
Who, in the end, counts as the inventor of the computer?

=== technology/double-entry-bookkeeping
title: Double-Entry Bookkeeping
src: db8db81ee7bf
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Double-entry bookkeeping records each transaction as a matched debit in one account and a credit in another, keeping the books balanced and letting merchants track assets, liabilities, and profit. It appears in Italian trade cities by the 14th century, and it was first set down in print in 1494.
--- fact 1
As early as 1327, Genoese legislation required treasury accounts to follow what it called the method of the bankers.
--- fact 2
The 1340 ledgers of the Genoese massari, treasury officials, show a developed double-entry form; historians debate whether they fully qualify as such.
--- fact 3
A Florentine merchant ledger from 1299 to 1300 is sometimes cited as an even earlier example, though this too is contested.
--- fact 4
Luca Pacioli's Summa de arithmetica, printed in [[place/venice|Venice]] in 1494, gave the first known printed and systematic description of the method.
--- fact 5
Pacioli organized and publicized an existing merchant practice, rather than inventing the technique himself.
--- context
The method grew from the commercial needs of merchants and city governments in [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] [[region/europe|Europe]], centered on Italian trade hubs such as Venice, Genoa, and Florence. Pacioli's 1494 book made it widely teachable, spreading it through the thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
Seen from outside, double-entry bookkeeping reads as tool-lineage for taming risk within an exchange-web: a notation that lets strangers trust a ledger instead of only a face. It is also memory-outsourcing, since matched debit-credit pairs let later readers catch errors the original scribe missed. This reading weakens if the 1340 Genoa ledgers are not true double-entry, which would push the documented lineage later. An alternative reading treats Pacioli's printed description, not any surviving ledger, as the real turning point, since print gave the method a wide, checkable audience.
--- question 1
Do the 1340 Genoa massari ledgers meet the full technical definition of double-entry, or only approximate it?
--- question 2
Did the technique originate earlier, perhaps in now-lost Florentine or other Italian merchant ledgers?

=== technology/electric-lighting
title: Electric Lighting
src: 45de274a4398
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Practical incandescent lighting emerged from parallel work in Britain and the United States between 1878 and 1880, and it spread from there into homes and workplaces.
--- fact 1
Joseph Swan showed a carbon-filament lamp at Newcastle on 18 December 1878, and again on 3 February 1879.
--- fact 2
Thomas Edison's team made a lamp with a carbonized thread filament in October 1879; US patent 223,898 followed on 27 January 1880.
--- fact 3
Humphry Davy had demonstrated a carbon arc back in 1809, and Yablochkov candles lit Paris streets from 1878, but arcs remained too intense for homes.
--- fact 4
In New York, Edison's Pearl Street Station began supplying current on 4 September 1882.
--- fact 5
Priority is debated: Swan's public demonstrations came earlier, and the two inventors eventually merged their British interests as Ediswan in 1883.
--- context
Lamps needed generation and distribution, covered in [[technology/electrical-power-grid|Electrical Power Grid]]. Davy's arc drew on a large [[technology/battery|Battery]] of voltaic cells, and later dynamos shared principles with the [[technology/electric-motor|Electric Motor]]. Both grew from the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] in [[region/europe|Europe]] and the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Thread: [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
In the Observer's terms, the lamp is a tool-lineage product: glassworking, vacuum pumps, and dynamos matured together, so the near-simultaneous lamps of Swan and Edison fit inheritance more than any lone inventor. Light after dark extended the species' working and social hours, though gas lighting had already begun that shift. This reading would weaken if the two lamps were shown to rest on unrelated techniques; a rival reading stresses capital and patent strategy over technique itself.
--- question 1
How much did Swan's earlier work shape Edison's design choices?
--- question 2
How quickly did electric light displace gas in ordinary homes?

=== technology/electric-motor
title: Electric Motor
src: 37dab4e0d5a1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The electric motor turns electrical current into continuous mechanical rotation by way of electromagnetism. It developed in stages from 1821 to the late 1830s, moving from a bare demonstration of motion to machines producing usable work.
--- fact 1
On 3 September 1821, Michael Faraday demonstrated electromagnetic rotation -- a wire circling a magnet -- the first conversion of electricity into continuous motion, though not yet into usable torque.
--- fact 2
William Sturgeon built a commutator-equipped motor in 1832, a device some historians treat as the first practical rotating motor.
--- fact 3
In May 1834, Moritz von Jacobi built a rotating motor that delivered continuous mechanical power, often cited among the first usable for real work.
--- fact 4
In 1837, Thomas Davenport received the first US patent for an electric motor, a design partly adapted from Sturgeon's own.
--- fact 5
On 13 September 1838, an improved motor of Jacobi's powered a passenger-carrying paddle-wheel boat on the Neva River.
--- fact 6
Priority for the first electric motor among Faraday, Sturgeon, Jacobi, and Davenport is disputed, depending on whether rotation, torque, or patent is taken to define "first."
--- context
The motor followed Oersted's 1820 discovery that current produces magnetism, part of broader [[theme/technology|Technology]] currents in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], centered on [[region/europe|Europe]] with parallel US work. Unlike steam power, it saw little industrial use until the [[technology/electrical-power-grid|Electrical Power Grid]] existed, decades later.
--- observer
The tool-lineage around the electric motor ran through several groups working in parallel over two decades, not through one inventor: rotation, torque, commutation, and usable output arrived as separate contributions. Later retellings often compress this into a single named inventor, which this record resists by naming the contested claimants themselves. This reading would weaken if evidence showed one device clearly predating and outperforming the rest.
--- question 1
Which criterion -- rotation, torque, or patent -- should decide priority for the first electric motor?

=== technology/electrical-power-grid
title: Electrical Power Grid
src: d9b5940715be
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Electrical power grids distribute electricity from central generators out to homes and factories. Thomas Edison opened the first commercial power station in New York in 1882.
--- fact 1
Alternating current, promoted by Westinghouse and Tesla, won out because it could be transmitted over long distances.
--- fact 2
By 1896, Niagara Falls was delivering power to Buffalo.
--- fact 3
Electricity now powers lighting, motors, communication, and computers.
--- fact 4
Grids today connect most of the world's population.
--- context
The grid followed after the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]], and it became the base for the [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a species' night became optional: light at any hour changed sleep, work, and city life alike. The grid stands, too, as one of the largest machines ever built.
--- question 1
How will grids themselves change as power sources change?
