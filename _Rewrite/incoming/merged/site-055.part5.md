=== technology/railways
title: Railways
src: 386303f8f372
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Railways run by steam locomotives began with the Stockton and Darlington Railway in England in 1825, and went on to reshape transport, trade, and the experience of time across the 19th century.
--- fact 1
In 1825, the Stockton and Darlington Railway opened as the first public railway to use steam locomotives.
--- fact 2
The Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened in 1830, was the first intercity line run entirely by steam and to a timetable.
--- fact 3
Robert Stephenson's locomotive Rocket won the Rainhill Trials in 1829.
--- fact 4
In the United States, the first transcontinental railroad was completed at Promontory Summit, Utah, on 10 May 1869.
--- fact 5
Railway timetables led in turn to standardized time zones, as in Britain from the 1840s and in North America by 1883.
--- context
Railways applied the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]] to land transport and drove the growth of the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]]. They worked alongside the [[technology/telegraph-and-submarine-cables|Telegraph and Submarine Cables]] to coordinate movement. Railways belong to the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region of [[region/europe|Europe]], within the theme threads of [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a machine built for moving coal ended up changing how the species measured the hour. Local noon gave way to a shared schedule, because the trains needed one, and clocks across a whole country were made to agree with each other.

=== technology/refrigeration
title: Refrigeration
src: 9d2ac3e96a59
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Refrigeration is engineered cooling used to preserve food and other perishables. It moved from an 1834 lab-scale prototype to reliable industrial compression systems, taken up by brewers, meatpackers, and shippers between the 1850s and the 1880s.
--- fact 1
In 1834, Jacob Perkins — US-born, working in Britain — patented a closed-cycle vapor-compression refrigerator using ether: a proof of concept, not commercially developed at the time.
--- fact 2
Between 1850 and 1853, Alexander Twining patented ether vapor-compression ice-making, and is credited with early US commercial refrigeration by the mid-1850s.
--- fact 3
James Harrison built an experimental ice machine at Geelong, Australia, in 1851, and a commercial one in 1854; a dozen such systems served breweries and meatworks by 1861.
--- fact 4
In 1882 the ship Dunedin, fitted with a compressor, completed the first successful frozen-meat shipment from New Zealand to Britain, after an earlier attempt in 1876 had failed.
--- fact 5
Carl von Linde patented an improved ammonia compression process in Germany in the mid-1870s, and founded a refrigeration firm in 1879 that had sold about 747 machines by 1890.
--- context
Refrigeration exemplifies [[theme/technology|Technology]] taking decades to move from principle to industrial reliability, part of the wider [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]]. Uptake tracked [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] [[region/europe|Europe]]'s brewing and meatpacking industries from the 1850s, and 1880s refrigerated shipping extended [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] to long-distance perishable goods.
--- observer
Seen as a single tool-lineage, refrigeration looks assembled by many hands over five decades: Perkins's 1834 device supplied a working principle that later engineers independently re-derived, once materials and refrigerants had matured. Its larger effect was to widen the exchange-web, letting perishable food cross oceans and loosening diets from their local seasons. This reading weakens if earlier, comparably reliable compression devices ever turn up.
--- question 1

=== technology/rosetta-stone
title: Rosetta Stone
src: b109c1375898
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Rosetta Stone is a stele inscribed in 196 BCE with a decree written in Egyptian hieroglyphs, Demotic, and Greek. Its discovery in 1799 is what allowed Egyptian hieroglyphs to finally be deciphered.
--- fact 1
It records a decree issued by the priests of Memphis, honoring Ptolemy V.
--- fact 2
French soldiers found it in 1799 near Rashid, or Rosetta, during Napoleon's Egyptian campaign.
--- fact 3
Britain took it after the French defeat in 1801, and it has remained in the British Museum since 1802.
--- fact 4
Thomas Young made the early progress on its Demotic text.
--- fact 5
Jean-Francois Champollion then announced his decipherment of the hieroglyphs in 1822.
--- context
The stone unlocked the writing of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] and stands as a landmark in the history of the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. Its Greek text allowed comparison with a known language, unlike the more distant [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]]. The Rosetta Stone sits within [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region of the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], within the theme thread of [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a script left unread for about fourteen centuries was recovered only because a single administrative decree happened to be repeated across three writing systems. A record survives to be read again only if someone, somewhere, still keeps the key.
