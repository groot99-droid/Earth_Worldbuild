=== observer-note/scripts-as-borders-writing-and-identity
src: 44f1a2c95ba8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Writing systems could, in principle, be freely borrowed and shared like any other technology. Why have they instead so often marked the line between one community and another?
--- observer
I read a script as a claim drawn in ink rather than on land: because writing carries a community's own way of speaking into something lasting and visible, taking up or turning away a neighbor's script becomes a way of declaring who you are, whether or not the spoken tongue underneath could, in principle, be written just as easily either way.

That the Inca ran a whole continent's worth of administration through knotted cords, and no script at all, tells me that keeping a record does not require writing in the narrow sense, which weakens any claim that a particular script is a necessary step in a society's growth.

This reading would weaken if closer study shows that plain practical matters, ease of carving, printing, or teaching a script, mattered more than identity in most of the historical choices between rival writing systems.
--- extra 1: Question
Writing systems could, in principle, be freely borrowed and shared like any other technology. Why have they instead so often marked the line between one community and another?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- The [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]] spread from the Levant across the Mediterranean world, and communities that adopted it often reshaped its letters to mark their own language as visibly distinct from its neighbors (Levant and Anatolia, Iron Age).
- The shift from scroll to [[technology/codex|Codex]] in the Mediterranean world during Classical Antiquity became closely tied to the spread of Christian texts, so that the format of a book, not only its content, signaled a community's religious identity.
- [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable type]] was developed independently in East Asia centuries before Johannes Gutenberg's mid-15th-century press in Europe, and each tradition's characters or letterforms became bound up with claims about which civilization's writing represented true learning.
- The Inca use of the [[technology/quipu|Quipu]], a knotted-cord recording system rather than a script in the conventional sense, shows that entire administrative empires could organize vast territory through record-keeping without adopting any neighboring society's writing system at all (Andes, Medieval Period).
- Religious and political authorities have repeatedly imposed or banned specific scripts, tying a particular alphabet or character set to loyalty to a state, church or empire rather than treating writing as a neutral tool.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
To treat script choice mainly as identity politics understates the real technical advantages and disadvantages different writing systems have for specific languages; some scripts fit certain sound systems far better than others regardless of political meaning. This account also cannot fully explain cases where communities freely borrowed a neighbor's script without apparent anxiety about identity at all.

=== observer-note/the-aftermath-problem-how-societies-remember-their-wars
src: 5e7c0e13c184
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The same war is often remembered in sharply different, even contradictory, ways by the societies that fought it, and by the generations that follow it. What decides which version of a war's memory survives?
--- observer
The Observer reads a war's remembered history as itself a shared story, actively kept up by whichever institutions, states, victors, survivor communities, control access to records and public commemoration, rather than as a fixed record that simply exists once the fighting ends. That the memory of Kolyma could be suppressed for decades, and that Hiroshima can be sincerely remembered in two incompatible ways at once by two different societies, suggests to you that memory of harm is not automatically kept alive by the scale of the suffering involved but needs active institutions willing to keep it visible. This reading would weaken if comparative study shows that the scale of a war's destruction predicts the durability of its public memory better than the political interests of the societies remembering it.
--- extra 1: Question
The same war is often remembered in sharply different, even contradictory, ways by the societies that fought it, and by the generations that follow it. What decides which version of a war's memory survives?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Ramesses II's inscriptions of the Battle of Kadesh present what other evidence suggests was a strategic stalemate as a decisive Egyptian victory, one of the earliest documented cases of a state controlling how a war would be remembered (Nile Valley, Bronze Age).
- [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]'s combatant nations built sharply different national memories of the same conflict, from Britain and France's stress on sacrifice and remembrance to Germany's early postwar narratives that questioned whether the war had truly been lost on the battlefield (Europe).
- The bombing of [[place/hiroshima|Hiroshima]] is remembered in the United States largely through a narrative of hastening the war's end, and in Japan largely through the suffering of the bomb's victims, two framings that coexist without resolving into one shared account (East Asia).
- Decades of forced labor and death in the [[place/kolyma|Kolyma]] camps were suppressed within the Soviet Union until archives opened after 1991, so that much of what is now known depends on survivor testimony that only became publicly available generations after the events themselves (Siberia and the Arctic).
- [[place/robben-island|Robben Island]]'s change from a prison into a museum staffed partly by its own former political prisoners shows a deliberate, institutionalized choice about how a place of suffering should be remembered going forward (Sub-Saharan Africa).
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
This account stresses institutional control over memory but risks understating the genuine, uncoordinated grief and testimony of individual survivors and families, which sometimes keeps an account of events alive even against an institution's active suppression, as eventually happened with Kolyma. It also does not fully explain why some societies eventually revise an official war memory under public pressure while others maintain a contested narrative indefinitely.
