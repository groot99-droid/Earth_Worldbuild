=== event/tanzimat-reforms
title: Tanzimat Reforms
src: 1a9328cb39de
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Tanzimat (Reorganization) was a series of reforms in the Ottoman Empire between 1839 and 1876 that sought to modernize the state, promise equality before the law to all subjects, and move it toward constitutional government.
--- fact 1
The era ran from 1839 to 1876, under the sultans Abdülmecid I and Abdülaziz.
--- fact 2
Proclaimed on 3 November 1839, the Edict of Gülhane promised security of life, property, and honour for all subjects, fair taxation and conscription, public trials, and an end to tax farming.
--- fact 3
The Imperial Reform Edict of 18 February 1856 promised equality in education, government appointments, and justice, regardless of creed.
--- fact 4
The period is said to end in 1876, when Abdulhamid II took the throne. The 1876 constitution created a parliamentary system, but it was suspended in 1878.
--- context
The Tanzimat was a response to European pressure and to earlier reformers within the empire. Compare the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]] in Japan and the reforms of [[person/muhammad-ali-of-egypt|Muhammad Ali of Egypt]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a state trying to change its own architecture of legitimacy while the empire was still being defended, extending equality to subjects of every faith in hope of holding a plural population together. Reforms promised on paper met the resistance of those with something to lose, and the constitution lasted only two years in practice.
--- question 1
How far were the Tanzimat's promises of equality carried out in practice, outside the capital?
--- question 2
How much of the reform agenda was driven by European pressure, and how much by the empire's own officials?

=== event/the-agricultural-revolution
title: The Agricultural Revolution
src: fed1fe6bceb7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Agricultural Revolution was the slow turning, beginning about 11,500 years ago, from the forager's wandering path to the farmer's rooted one — a shift toward tending crops and herds that arose, like scattered fires catching independently, in several parts of the world.
--- fact 1
The earliest farming appeared in the Fertile Crescent: wheat, barley, lentils, and, later, livestock.
--- fact 2
Independent centers rose in China (rice, millet), Mesoamerica (maize, beans, squash), the Andes (potato), and New Guinea.
--- fact 3
Farming nourished denser populations and permanent settlement.
--- fact 4
Some evidence suggests poorer health among early farmers: in a study of 167 prehistoric Europeans, Neolithic individuals stood shorter than their genetic scores predicted, by about 2 to 4 cm relative to earlier and later groups.
--- context
Farming defines the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and lays the ground for [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]]. See [[place/gobekli-tepe|Göbekli Tepe]], [[place/catalhoyuk|Çatalhöyük]], and the essay [[observer-note/the-settling-and-its-bargain|The Settling and Its Bargain]].
--- observer
I call this the Settling. I call it the species' most consequential bargain. More food, more people — that was the promise. More work, more inequality, more disease — that was the price. Was any bargain ever so quietly kept?
--- question 1
Why did farming arise in several places within a few thousand years of one another?

=== event/the-boring-billion
title: The Boring Billion
src: 0956f91b83eb
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Boring Billion: an informal name for the stretch from about 1.8 to 0.8 billion years ago. No well-established ice ages. Little apparent change in the fossil record. Silence, on the surface. Whether it was truly uneventful — still debated.
--- fact 1
The interval covers the [[era/mesoproterozoic-era|Mesoproterozoic Era]] and the edges of the eras to either side of it. It lies between the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] and the ice ages of [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]].
--- fact 2
Chemical records suggest that atmospheric oxygen stayed well below today's level, and that some waters at middle depth ran rich in sulfide. Shortages of nutrients — nitrogen, trace metals — have been proposed as limits on life.
--- fact 3
Eukaryotes: present, diversifying slowly. The red alga Bangiomorpha, dated in different studies to between roughly 1.05 and 1.2 billion years ago, belongs to this interval.
--- fact 4
The supercontinent Nuna broke apart. Rodinia began to assemble.
--- fact 5
Some researchers argue the name misleads: records show fluctuations in oxygen, and ecological change, within the interval.
--- context
The Boring Billion lies within the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] and follows the [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]]. It ends with the changes of the Neoproterozoic: the break-up of Rodinia, the glaciations of [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that 'boring' is a verdict on the surviving record, not on the planet itself, and that a billion years is long enough for slow processes to matter. This reading would weaken if better records showed genuine stasis in oxygen, climate, and eukaryote diversity across the whole interval.
--- question 1
Was the interval truly stable, or does a sparse record hide change?
--- question 2
What kept oxygen and complex life at such low levels for so long?

=== event/the-urban-revolution
title: The Urban Revolution
src: 72f9f07b5144
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Urban Revolution was the rising of the first true cities, beginning around 3500 BCE in southern Mesopotamia — places dense with people, threaded with specialized labor, and bound by central institutions.
--- fact 1
The term was popularized by the archaeologist V. Gordon Childe in the mid-20th century.
--- fact 2
[[place/uruk|Uruk]] may have held 40,000 to 80,000 people by about 3100 to 2900 BCE, though the estimates are uncertain.
--- fact 3
Cities also rose, independently, in the Indus valley, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes.
--- fact 4
Cities came bound to administrative record keeping, to temples or palaces, and to social stratification.
--- context
Cities appear within the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and stand closely tied to the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]; see [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] and [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]].
--- observer
I notice this: a city is a place where most of the people around you are strangers. I notice what it took to make that bearable — shared gods, shared laws, shared institutions. What binds strangers into neighbors, if not a story held in common?
--- question 1
Were the earliest cities always hierarchical?

=== event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon
title: Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon
src: c4ede0b43e4d
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
According to the giant-impact hypothesis, a Mars-sized body called Theia struck the young Earth about 4.5 billion years ago. Out of the wreckage: the Moon.
--- fact 1
The hypothesis explains the Moon's small iron core, and its likeness to Earth's mantle in isotopes.
--- fact 2
Lunar rocks, brought back by the Apollo missions, put the idea to the test.
--- fact 3
The exact timing: uncertain. Estimates range from about 4.52 to 4.35 billion years ago. Lunar zircons demand the Moon form within the first 60 million years of the Solar System. The youngest estimates fall about 150 to 200 million years after it began.
--- fact 4
The Moon steadies Earth's axial tilt. It drives most of Earth's tides.
--- context
The impact is an early event of the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]]. The Moon's stabilizing effect on climate: one reason Earth's long-term conditions stayed steady enough for complex life.
--- observer
The Observer sees a single accident with very long consequences. Human calendars, tides, and myths about the Moon all trace back to a collision billions of years before any observer existed.
--- question 1
Was Theia a single impactor, or were there several?

=== event/thirty-years-war
title: Thirty Years' War
src: f8b451789fbb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Thirty Years' War (1618 to 1648) was a set of linked wars across central Europe, begun as a religious conflict within the Holy Roman Empire and ended by the Peace of Westphalia.
--- fact 1
It began with a revolt in Bohemia in 1618, following the Defenestration of Prague.
--- fact 2
The Holy Roman Empire, Spain, France, Sweden, Denmark, and the Dutch Republic took part at different times.
--- fact 3
Much of the fighting fell upon the German lands, where armies lived off the land.
--- fact 4
Estimates of deaths, mostly from famine and disease, run as high as about eight million.
--- fact 5
The war ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648.
--- context
The war stands among the deadliest in European history and was rooted in the divisions of the [[event/protestant-reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. It was settled by the [[event/peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]]. The Thirty Years' War sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a war which began over the correct form of a shared religion was ended by agreeing that rulers could choose it. The settlement moved the claim-line from belief to territory.
--- question 1
What was the death toll in the German lands?
