=== event/age-of-revolutions
title: Age of Revolutions
src: 9f88bb3200e9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Historians call the decades of political upheaval that swept around the Atlantic world, from the 1770s to the revolutions of 1848, the Age of Revolutions — though where its dates begin and end remains a matter of historians' choice; this note sets them at 1775 to 1848.
--- fact 1
Periodization itself is contested ground: Eric Hobsbawm's *The Age of Revolution* covers 1789 to 1848, Robert Palmer's *The Age of the Democratic Revolution* begins about 1760, and some accounts reach back as early as 1765.
--- fact 2
Its major chapters run: the American Revolution, ending in 1783; the French Revolution, 1789 to 1799; the Haitian Revolution, 1791 to 1804; the Spanish American wars of independence, beginning about 1808 to 1810; and the revolutions of 1848.
--- fact 3
The Haitian Revolution opened with a mass uprising of enslaved people in northern Saint-Domingue on 22 August 1791. Independence followed on 1 January 1804, and Haiti is widely described as the first state founded by people who had been enslaved — the second independent country in the Americas, after the United States.
--- fact 4
Ideas of popular sovereignty, of citizenship, of nationalism itself, spread widely in their wake.
--- fact 5
Slavery and the rights of women, by contrast, stayed contested ground, largely excluded from the new settlements at first.
--- context
These revolutions overlap the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and reshape the very architecture of legitimacy and claim-lines; see [[observer-note/the-fiction-of-claim-lines|The Fiction of Claim-Lines]].
--- observer
The Observer marks a shift in the story-glue of legitimacy itself: authority increasingly claimed to spring from 'the people,' rather than from tradition or the divine.
--- question 1
How connected were the revolutions to one another? Older histories treated them as separate episodes; more recent scholarship increasingly reads them as links in one chain, though the question of causation is still very much argued.

=== event/alexander-s-conquests
title: Alexander's Conquests
src: e877ba50128b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between 336 and 323 BCE, Alexander III of Macedon overthrew the Achaemenid Persian Empire and stretched his rule from Greece and Egypt to the edge of India.
--- fact 1
Alexander took the throne at about twenty, and died in Babylon at thirty-two.
--- fact 2
His campaigns brought the empire of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] to its end.
--- fact 3
After his death, his own generals carved the empire into separate Hellenistic kingdoms.
--- fact 4
Greek language and culture spread widely in their wake, and blended with the traditions already in place.
--- context
See [[person/alexander-the-great|Alexander the Great]]. His conquests reshaped [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and helped open the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]].
--- observer
The Observer weighs the outsized role of one man's ambition against its longer legacy: a region-wide Greek-speaking elite whose common tongue let ideas travel more freely than before.
--- question 1
Did Alexander intend a lasting empire, or only the conquest itself?

=== event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths
title: Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths
src: 518efe246c14
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Evidence gathers that the Amazon basin once held great, planned settlements — bound by roads and earthworks from about 500 BCE to the 1500s CE — and that its Indigenous peoples enriched the very soil and shape of the land.
--- fact 1
Laser eyes cast down from the air have found whole networks of settlement: the [[place/upano-valley-settlements|Upano Valley Settlements]] in Ecuador, the sites of the [[culture/casarabe-culture|Casarabe Culture]] in Bolivia, and the [[place/xingu-garden-cities|Xingu Garden Cities]] of Brazil.
--- fact 2
At the river's mouth, the [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]] raised great mounds of earth.
--- fact 3
Across the basin lie dark, fertile soils — terra preta, the Amazonian Dark Earths — thick with charcoal and often scattered with pre-Columbian pottery, wherever settlement once stood. Whether human hands made this soil, or only found and used it, remains disputed; one 2021 study of a single site in the Brazilian Amazon argued the earth there rose largely from natural, alluvial cause.
--- fact 4
These were 'low-density' settlements beside cities elsewhere: households scattered wide, with plazas, roads, and gardens between them, and no walled town drawn tight.
--- context
This evidence redraws the [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]] as it stood before the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], and it reaches toward wider questions raised in [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]].
--- observer
I look for a city and expect stone piled tight, walls drawn close, streets like veins. I find gardens instead, and roads that wander, and households spread wide across the green. Must a city look like the cities I was taught? I let go of that assumption here: a scattered, garden-grown settlement can carry the same complexity as any walled town.
--- question 1
How great were the peoples of the Amazon before 1500, and how much of that forest was theirs to shape and tend?

=== event/american-civil-war
title: American Civil War
src: ee96cbd5bb42
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
From 1861 to 1865, the United States fought a civil war against eleven Southern states that seceded to form the Confederacy. Historians overwhelmingly agree that slavery stood at the center of secession itself. The war ended in Confederate defeat, and the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery later that same year.
--- fact 1
It began on 12 April 1861, when Confederate guns opened fire on Fort Sumter. General Robert E. Lee surrendered the main Confederate army at Appomattox on 9 April 1865, though scattered fighting and further surrenders continued into the weeks that followed.
--- fact 2
Eleven states made up the Confederacy in the end: seven seceded first, and four more joined only after the war had already begun.
--- fact 3
How many died depends on the method used to count. The traditional figure stands at about 620,000. A 2011 census-based study by J. David Hacker raised that to about 750,000, within a range of 650,000 to 850,000. A 2024 study drawing on full-count census records estimated about 698,000 deaths, with a bounding interval of roughly 50,000 either way. That study found a far higher death rate among Confederate states than among Union ones: about 13 percent of military-age native-born white men in the core Confederate states, against about 5 percent in the core Union states — though the Union lost more men in absolute numbers, more than 400,000 against about 300,000. All of these remain estimates of excess mortality; no side kept standardized records.
--- fact 4
The Emancipation Proclamation took effect on 1 January 1863, a wartime measure freeing those held in states then in rebellion. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified on 6 December 1865, abolished slavery across the whole of the United States.
--- fact 5
Railways, telegraphs, and industrial production together shaped how the war itself was fought.
--- fact 6
It remains the deadliest conflict in American history.
--- context
The war sits at the center of the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]], and shows plainly what the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] could do to the conduct of war. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a modern state turning its own weapons upon its own citizens, over the question of whether one person could be owned by another. The war also displays a technology-driven scale of killing that stood as a warning of [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] to come.
--- question 1
How reliable are these casualty estimates, in the end? The figures range from about 620,000 to 750,000 or more depending on method used, and civilian deaths are counted only inconsistently across the sources.

=== event/american-revolution
title: American Revolution
src: 7ad488a1623d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The American Revolution, fought from 1775 to 1783, was the war by which thirteen British colonies in North America became the independent United States.
--- fact 1
Fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord in April 1775.
--- fact 2
On 4 July 1776, the Declaration of Independence was formally adopted.
--- fact 3
France joined the war on the American side in 1778, and the British army surrendered at Yorktown in 1781.
--- fact 4
The Treaty of Paris, signed in 1783, recognized American independence outright.
--- fact 5
The Haudenosaunee Confederacy split over the war itself: most of its nations sided with Britain, and some with the Americans.
--- context
The revolution grew from the debts left by the [[event/seven-years-war|Seven Years' War]], and stands among the first events of the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]]. It split the [[culture/haudenosaunee-confederacy|Haudenosaunee Confederacy]] in two. It sits within the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region of [[region/north-america|North America]], within the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a new state that justified its own founding by universal rights, even as it retained slavery and denied the claims of Indigenous peoples. Its founding documents made promises that later generations would turn back against the state's own conduct.
--- question 1
Just how divided was the colonial population itself?
