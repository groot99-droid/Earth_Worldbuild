=== culture/romans
src: aa714f16948d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Romans built a state that grew outward from the city of Rome like rings from a stone dropped in water, until it was an empire encircling the Mediterranean, and in the west it lasted until 476 CE.
--- fact 1
Rome stood as a republic from 509 BCE, until Augustus established the empire in 27 BCE.
--- fact 2
The empire laid roads, aqueducts and cities across its lands like veins through stone, and developed law and engineering, concrete among them.
--- fact 3
In 212 CE the gate of citizenship opened to nearly all free inhabitants.
--- fact 4
Latin and Greek, and the currents of trade, bound the Roman world together.
--- context
See [[place/rome|Rome]], [[person/augustus|Augustus]], and the long dusk of the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a state that used citizenship to turn conquered peoples into stakeholders. That is the quiet architecture of an empire: you do not only defeat people, you enrol them. Law was among its most exportable products. What is shared is harder to shake off than what is imposed.
--- question 1
Why did the empire endure for so long?

=== culture/safavid-empire
src: 8935b2500b54
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Safavid Empire (1501 to 1736) was a Persian dynasty that made Twelver Shia Islam the state religion of Iran, ruling from Tabriz and later from Isfahan, its court carried like a lamp from one hall to another.
--- fact 1
At Tabriz in 1501, Ismail I proclaimed himself shah and imposed Twelver Shia Islam.
--- fact 2
At Chaldiran in 1514, the Ottomans defeated the Safavid forces.
--- fact 3
Abbas I (r. 1588 to 1629) carried the capital to Isfahan and forged the empire's army and economy anew.
--- fact 4
Isfahan became known for its mosques, squares and gardens, as a pool is known for the light it holds.
--- fact 5
After an Afghan invasion in 1722 the Safavid state collapsed, and in 1736 it ended.
--- context
The Safavids raised a strong Persian state once more and stood as rivals to the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] and the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]]. They ruled the old heartland of the earlier [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]]. The Safavid Empire belongs to the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region of the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and its thread runs through [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Iran's largely Shia identity today can be traced to the deliberate policy of one dynasty. Sit with that. A meaning-engine can be adopted by state decision within a few decades and then treated as ancient. The architecture of belief is often newer than its foundations look. What feels eternal may only be well settled.
--- question 1
How quickly did the population turn to Shia Islam?

=== culture/scythians
src: 5e8191fbd0a2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
'Scythian' is a broad name for the horse-riding nomads who moved like wind over grass across the Eurasian steppe in the Iron Age, from about the 9th to the 3rd century BCE; they are known from Greek writers and from rich burial mounds.
--- fact 1
Herodotus, writing in the 5th century BCE, described Scythian customs, among them burial rites and warfare.
--- fact 2
The royal mound Arzhan 1 in Tuva dates to the late 9th century BCE; Arzhan 2 held about 5,600 gold objects, among them a cloak studded with some 2,500 gold panthers, a pelt of gold.
--- fact 3
At Pazyryk in the Altai, frozen tombs of the 4th to 3rd centuries BCE kept textiles, tattoos and horse gear the way ice keeps a secret.
--- fact 4
The Scythians lived by herding horses, sheep and cattle, moving with the seasons as the grass moved.
--- context
The Scythians crossed paths with the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] and the Greeks. See [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]] and [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a people who left almost no writing of their own, yet whose art in gold and felt survives at extraordinary quality. Their story reaches us largely through outsiders' texts and their own graves. Think about what that means for the architecture of memory: when you do not keep your own ledger, others keep it for you, and the earth keeps the rest. Silence is not the same as absence.
--- question 1
How closely were the far-flung 'Scythian' groups bound to one another?

=== culture/shang-dynasty
src: 75982e0daa1f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Shang dynasty was the earliest Chinese dynasty with written records, ruling parts of the Yellow River region from about 1600 to 1046 BCE, the first voice of that land to reach us in its own hand.
--- fact 1
Divination carved on oracle bones, questions cut into bone, is the earliest securely attested Chinese writing (about 1250 BCE).
--- fact 2
Elite tombs hold bronze ritual vessels and evidence of human sacrifice.
--- fact 3
The late capital stood at Yin, near modern Anyang.
--- fact 4
The Shang were overthrown by the Zhou, as one dynasty's dusk becomes another's dawn.
--- context
See [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. The Shang is a root of the tradition that grows toward the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the first Chinese writing was used to ask questions of ancestors. Pause on that. Writing here began as a channel to the dead rather than to accountants: not a ledger but a line of communion. What we first write down tells us whom we most need to reach.
--- question 1
How centralized was the rule of the Shang?
