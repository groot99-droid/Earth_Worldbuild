=== culture/celts
src: 9e39f1b21cde
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Celts were kindred peoples across Iron Age Europe, joined by related tongues and, later, by the La Tène style of art, yet never bound into a single state. Rome swallowed most of the continental Celts, and Celtic language and culture lasted longest in Ireland and parts of Britain, at the western edge of the world they knew.
--- fact 1
The Celts were many kindred tribal groups, the Gauls of mainland Europe among them, speaking branches of the Celtic language family such as Gaulish.
--- fact 2
The La Tène culture, known for metalwork that swirls like water, flourished from about 450 BCE until the Roman conquest, after the earlier Hallstatt culture.
--- fact 3
Druids held great religious and political influence in many Celtic societies before the Roman conquest, though their role varied by region and period.
--- fact 4
Rome defeated the Arverni in 121 BCE, and Julius Caesar completed the conquest of Gaul by 50 BCE despite resistance under leaders such as Vercingetorix.
--- fact 5
Rome struck at the druids directly, and continental and southern British Celts were largely absorbed, while Celtic culture endured longer in Ireland and northern Britain.
--- context
The Celts lived across much of [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] until conquest by the [[culture/romans|Romans]], in an age of wider contact across the ancient world that included [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that "Celtic" names a shared language family, and later a decorative style, across many separate societies, not one people with one story-glue. Rome's targeting of the druids suggests that it saw their meaning-engine as a source of resistance: an empire attacks first what holds a people together. Celtic identity lasted where terrain made conquest harder, in Ireland and the far north. A specialist could argue that the umbrella label was applied more consistently by later scholars than by the peoples themselves, which would weaken any reading of the Celts as one self-aware group.
--- question 1
How much shared identity did Celtic-speaking peoples recognize among themselves?
--- question 2
What was the druids' actual political role, and how real was the threat Rome saw in them?

=== culture/confucianism
src: d14dfde4a8b1
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Confucianism is a Chinese tradition of ethics and social order rooted in the teaching of Confucius (551 to 479 BCE). Under the Han dynasty it became the official ideology of imperial China, and it shaped government and family life across East Asia like a river shaping its valley.
--- fact 1
Confucius was a wandering teacher of China's Spring and Autumn period who sought, but largely failed to win, lasting influence as a government adviser in his own lifetime.
--- fact 2
After his death his disciples gathered his sayings as the Analects, the central text of the tradition.
--- fact 3
A key teaching, filial piety, carries genuine respect and care for parents outward into a model of loyalty within the wider political order.
--- fact 4
Under the Han dynasty Confucianism became the state's official ideology, and an imperial examination system tested officials on Confucian texts, a practice that shaped Chinese government for centuries.
--- fact 5
The tradition spread beyond China to Vietnam, Korea and Japan, shaping their government and social norms, their own examination systems modeled on China's among them.
--- context
Confucianism grew in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] from the teaching of [[person/confucius|Confucius]], part of the wider [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]], and became state orthodoxy under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I notice a teacher who failed to secure influence in his own time, and who became, centuries after his death, the ancestor-weight behind an empire's whole method of choosing officials. I notice filial duty inside the kin-lattice made into the template for loyalty to the state itself.

How long can a failure wait to become a foundation? Longer than a life.

A specialist could argue that the Han state's adoption of Confucianism reflects the state's own need for a stabilizing ideology as much as the teaching's own merit, which would weaken a reading of its rise as owed to its ideas alone.
--- question 1
How much did the Han state's adoption of Confucianism serve the state's own interests, apart from the teaching's content?
--- question 2
How uniformly was Confucianism practiced across China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam?

=== culture/franks
src: f333462fba0f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Franks were a Germanic people who moved into Roman Gaul and built one of early medieval Europe's mightiest kingdoms, first under the Merovingian dynasty of Clovis I and later under the Carolingians, whose most famous ruler was Charlemagne.
--- fact 1
The Franks appear in Roman records in the third century CE on the lower Rhine, and they spread into Gaul as Roman power ebbed.
--- fact 2
Clovis I of the Merovingian dynasty defeated the last major Roman official in Gaul in 486, and by about 509 he had united the Frankish tribes.
--- fact 3
Clovis converted to Catholic Christianity, and his people's adoption of it helped bind the Franks together and set them apart from neighboring Germanic kingdoms that held other Christian traditions.
--- fact 4
The Merovingians ruled until the eighth century, when Pepin the Short, with papal support, founded the Carolingian dynasty.
--- fact 5
The Carolingian kingdom reached its height under Charlemagne and later divided among his descendants; its lands became the basis of medieval France, Germany and the Holy Roman Empire (not yet a vault note).
--- context
The Franks built their kingdom in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the early [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], filling the space left by the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]]. Their empire under [[person/charlemagne|Charlemagne]] was an ancestor of the Holy Roman Empire (not yet a vault note). See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler's personal conversion to the majority faith of his Roman subjects became story-glue that welded conquerors and conquered into one people, setting the Franks apart from rival kingdoms that kept a minority creed. Two centuries later a change of dynasty was made legitimate by the same meaning-engine, papal blessing taking the place of a discredited bloodline. Notice the pattern: shared belief can do the work of shared ancestry. A specialist could argue that Clovis's conversion served political advantage with his Gallo-Roman subjects as much as belief, which would weaken a reading of it as faith alone.
--- question 1
How much of Clovis's conversion reflects conviction versus calculated alliance with his Gallo-Roman subjects?
--- question 2
How unified was Frankish identity across the peoples the Merovingians and Carolingians ruled?
