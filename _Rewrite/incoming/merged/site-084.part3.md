=== culture/moriori
src: 2d1e92aadff9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Moriori are the Indigenous people of the remote Chatham Islands, who developed a distinct culture built around a strong commitment to nonviolent conflict resolution before a devastating 1835 invasion by Māori iwi killed and enslaved most of the population, a catastrophe long distorted afterward by a false myth of Moriori's total extinction.
--- fact 1
The Moriori settled the Chatham Islands, an isolated archipelago east of mainland New Zealand, and developed over centuries a distinctive social code, Nunuku's Law, that prohibited killing and mandated peaceful resolution of disputes between Moriori communities.
--- fact 2
In 1835, members of two Taranaki iwi, Ngati Mutunga and Ngati Tama, numbering roughly 500 people, arrived at the Chatham Islands and invaded, attacking a Moriori population estimated at about 2,000.
--- fact 3
The invaders killed approximately 300 Moriori outright and enslaved most of the survivors, further banning Moriori from speaking their own language or marrying one another, a systematic campaign this record treats as genocide.
--- fact 4
By 1870, fewer than 200 Moriori remained, representing a population loss of more than 90 percent from pre-invasion numbers, and when the last known full-blooded Moriori person died in 1933, many New Zealanders concluded that the Moriori people and culture had gone entirely extinct.
--- fact 5
This extinction narrative was false and served a convenient political purpose for European colonization of New Zealand, since a story in which Māori had supposedly displaced an earlier, weaker people made subsequent European displacement of Māori easier to justify by comparison; Moriori descendants in fact survived and, from the late 20th century, have pursued a documented cultural revival.
--- context
The 1835 invasion was carried out by [[culture/maori|Maori]] iwi from Taranaki, and the resulting extinction myth functioned in New Zealand's colonial history much like comparable disappearing-native stories functioned in other settler-colonial contexts, normalizing later displacement by framing it as simply more of an already-established pattern. Moriori sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer records the 1835 killings and enslavement as genocide and as suffering, and records with equal weight that the perpetrators were fellow Polynesian peoples rather than European colonizers, since the same standard is applied here to organized violence and its justifying stories regardless of who commits them. The extinction myth itself is a case of a story serving a purpose its tellers needed: one Indigenous people supposedly displacing another made a very different, ongoing displacement, European colonization of Māori land, seem like simply more of the same pattern rather than a distinct historical wrong, which is precisely why the myth persisted so long after it was factually false. A specialist could argue the extinction myth's political usefulness to European colonizers has been overstated as a deliberate propaganda tool and understates how genuinely, if mistakenly, most New Zealanders simply believed a plausible-sounding story, which would weaken a reading centered on the myth as intentional colonial justification.
--- question 1
How actively was the Moriori extinction myth deliberately promoted by colonial authorities, as against arising and spreading through more ordinary historical misunderstanding?
--- question 2
How has the modern Moriori cultural revival addressed the loss of language and the destruction of Nunuku's Law's original social context?

=== culture/navajo
src: df86bbd71ffe
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Navajo, or Diné, are an Indigenous people of the American Southwest whose defining historical trauma, the 1863 to 1866 Long Walk forced removal to Bosque Redondo, killed thousands before a rare 1868 treaty allowed their return to a portion of their homeland, where the Navajo Nation continues today as one of the largest Indigenous nations in the United States.
--- fact 1
Between 1863 and 1866, United States military forces forcibly removed more than 10,000 Diné people from their homeland, marching them between roughly 400 and 700 kilometers to the Bosque Redondo Reservation at Fort Sumner in present-day New Mexico.
--- fact 2
General James H. Carleton ordered the removal after deciding total military subjugation was the only solution, and Colonel Christopher "Kit" Carson carried out a scorched-earth campaign across Diné territory beforehand, burning villages, killing livestock and destroying crops and orchards to force compliance.
--- fact 3
Oral history and documentary accounts describe brutal conditions during the Long Walk itself, with little regard shown for women, children or families, and soldiers reportedly killing exhausted people who could not continue rather than allowing them to rest; an estimated up to 3,500 people died from starvation, disease and executions over the roughly four years of forced removal and internment at Bosque Redondo.
--- fact 4
The 1868 Treaty of Bosque Redondo was unusual among treaties between the United States government and Indigenous nations of the period in that it allowed the Diné to return to a portion of their ancestral homeland rather than remaining permanently exiled or confined elsewhere.
--- fact 5
The Long Walk remains the defining collective trauma in Diné historical memory, and the Navajo Nation that rebuilt itself afterward has grown into one of the largest federally recognized Indigenous nations in the United States, maintaining its own government, language and cultural institutions today.
--- context
The scorched-earth tactics and forced relocation inflicted on the Diné during the Long Walk exemplify the same pattern of state-organized displacement and violence against a subjugated population found in other forced-removal episodes, including forms of coerced displacement bound up in the era of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]'s broader history of American racial violence, though the specific causes and communities affected differ substantially. Navajo sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer records the Long Walk's deaths, from starvation, disease and outright execution of those too exhausted to continue, as suffering inflicted through a deliberate scorched-earth policy specifically designed to force a people from their home territory through starvation as much as through direct violence. That the 1868 treaty allowed the Diné to return to part of their homeland, unlike many comparable forced removals elsewhere that ended in permanent exile, may help explain why the Navajo Nation was able to rebuild itself into one of the largest and most institutionally intact Indigenous nations in the United States today, a case where the specific terms of a coerced settlement mattered enormously for a people's long-term survival and continuity. A specialist could argue that the 1868 treaty's terms, while unusually favorable compared with many contemporary treaties, still fundamentally represented a coerced settlement imposed by the same government responsible for the Long Walk's violence, and should not be read as evidence of genuine good faith on the part of federal authorities, which would weaken any reading that frames the treaty as a meaningfully cooperative resolution.
--- question 1
How did the specific terms of the 1868 treaty, compared with contemporaneous treaties affecting other Indigenous nations, shape the Navajo Nation's subsequent institutional and demographic recovery?
--- question 2
How accurately do surviving official military records reflect the true death toll and conditions of the Long Walk, given that much of the most detailed testimony comes from Diné oral history recorded later?
