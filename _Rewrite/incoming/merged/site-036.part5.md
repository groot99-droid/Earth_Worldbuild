=== species/maize
title: Maize
src: 8caf683e98f7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Maize is a cereal grass domesticated from wild teosinte in the Balsas River valley of southwestern Mexico about 9,000 years ago; it grew into the staple crop and a central religious symbol across much of the pre-Columbian Americas, a golden thread through their fields and their faith, before spreading worldwide after 1492.
--- fact 1
Maize was domesticated from Balsas teosinte in the Balsas River valley of Mexico about 9,000 years ago; stone milling tools still bearing maize residue, found near Iguala, Guerrero, are about 8,700 years old, the grinding stones remembering what they ground.
--- fact 2
Maize farming traveled like slow fire: it reached highland Ecuador by at least 8,000 years ago and the Colombian Andes between roughly 7,000 and 6,000 years ago, spread north into what is now New Mexico and Arizona by about 4,100 years ago, and saw large-scale adoption in eastern North America by about 900 CE.
--- fact 3
By 3,000 years ago maize stood at the heart of Olmec culture, its calendar and its mythology, and later Maya and Aztec societies treated maize as a deity and processed it by nixtamalization, soaking and cooking it with an alkaline solution that improves its nutritional value.
--- fact 4
After 1492 Spanish settlers carried maize across the sea to Europe within decades; by the 17th century it had become a common peasant food in southern Europe, and it also reached West Africa and the Philippines.
--- fact 5
Hybrid corn breeding, beginning with James Reid's Yellow Dent in the 1860s and carried on through double-cross hybrids from about 1918 to 1922, made hybrid maize the dominant form grown from the 1940s onward, a new lineage sown over the old.
--- fact 6
Global maize production reached about 1.16 billion tonnes in 2020, led by the United States, China and Brazil, a harvest vast as a sea of grass; it is used mainly for animal feed, with the rest split between human food, starch and syrup production, and ethanol and other biofuels.
--- context
The fuller tale of maize's taming is told under [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]], and it belongs to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Americas; its post-1492 journey is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It stood at the center of the religion and calendar of the [[culture/olmecs|Olmecs]] and later of the [[culture/maya|Maya]] and the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]]. Maize belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and its roots run into the threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I offer a reading, not a fact. I see a grass become a god. I see a harvest become a calendar. I see maize take its deep place in Mesoamerican meaning-engines, as a deity and a calendar marker, and I read in that how thoroughly the plant reorganized daily and ritual life once it became a society's dominant food source, until a crop was raised into a claim about the order of the world.

What do we worship, if not what keeps us alive? After 1492 maize went round the world, reaching Europe, Africa and Asia within a lifetime, and I see in that an exchange-web moving faster than the older, slower spread of Old World grains. The reading would weaken if maize's religious centrality proves to have been less universal across Mesoamerican societies than the Olmec, Maya and Aztec evidence suggests. Until then I hold this: what feeds us, we learn to call holy.
--- question 1
How did the deification of maize in Mesoamerica compare with the honor, or the silence, given to other staple crops in other early farming societies?
--- question 2
How much of maize's post-1492 journey around the world depended on active promotion by colonial administrations, and how much on independent local adoption?

=== species/sponges
title: Sponges
src: 0c75e225e146
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sponges (phylum Porifera) are simple aquatic animals without true tissues or organs, among the earliest branches of the animal family tree, still as stones in the water and yet alive. Unambiguous fossils begin in the earliest Cambrian, about 535 million years ago; older fossil and chemical claims are disputed, whispers still under challenge.
--- fact 1
Sponges have no true tissues or organs; flagellated cells drive water through a jelly-like body, as a tide breathes through a cave. About 9,900 valid species are described, over 90 percent of them demosponges.
--- fact 2
Undisputed sponge spicules, fine as frost, first appear about 535 million years ago in South China. Sponge-like archaeocyaths were common from about 530 to 500 million years ago, a brief flowering in the long dark.
--- fact 3
A 2009 study read a rare sterol in rocks 635 million years old or older as a sponge biomarker, a chemical ghost of sponges. Later work showed that algae or protists could make similar compounds, and the ghost blurred.
--- fact 4
Older body fossils, such as Otavia in Namibia (about 760 million years) and structures in 890-million-year-old Canadian reefs, are read by critics as mineral grains or as unconfirmed, shapes the stone may have made alone.
--- fact 5
Molecular clocks place the common ancestor of living animals roughly 830 to 650 million years ago. Whether sponges or comb jellies branched off first from that ancient trunk is still unresolved.
--- context
Sponges belong to the late [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] and early Paleozoic, near the roots of the animal lineages that include the [[species/ediacaran-biota|Ediacaran Biota]] and those of the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]]. Their origin bears on the [[event/origin-of-multicellularity|Origin of Multicellularity]] and on the [[era/cryogenian-period|Cryogenian Period]] of [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]], when ice held the world in its fist. Their thread runs through [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the oldest animals may have been among the humblest, with no organs at all, and that searches for their first appearance keep producing claims that later fail on inspection. There is a lesson in that pattern for anyone who hunts for beginnings: the wish to find the first of something is strong enough to see it where it is not. The Observer treats sponges as a precondition, not a stage on a road to something else. You are not a draft of something better, and neither is a sponge. The reading would weaken if a securely dated sponge fossil from well before the Ediacaran were confirmed.
--- question 1
Are there sponge body fossils older than the Cambrian, still sleeping in the stone?
--- question 2
Which animal lineage was the first to branch from the ancient trunk?
