=== place/mausoleum-of-qin-shi-huang
src: e0b917530c16
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang is the burial complex of the first emperor of unified China, laid near Xi'an in Shaanxi and begun in 246 BCE; east of its tomb mound, in buried pits, stands the Terracotta Army, a host of clay soldiers keeping watch in the dark.
--- fact 1
The first earth was turned for the Mausoleum in 246 BCE, when Ying Zheng became king of Qin; after he bound China into one in 221 BCE he took the title Qin Shi Huang ("First Emperor"), and he died in 210 BCE.
--- fact 2
Sima Qian, writing about a century later, reports that 700,000 laborers worked on the tomb and palace; the figure echoes down the ages, but it cannot be verified.
--- fact 3
In 1974 farmers digging a well broke into the long sleep of the Terracotta Army. The pits hold life-size clay soldiers, horses, chariots and bronze weapons, estimated at about 8,000 figures in total, of which more than 2,000 have been raised into the light of excavation.
--- fact 4
The figures were assembled from molded parts, finished by hand and originally painted in bright colors, which mostly faded like morning mist once the air touched them.
--- fact 5
Other pits keep a quieter court: figures of officials, acrobats and musicians, and bronze waterbirds.
--- fact 6
The burial mound itself remains unexcavated, and the soil around it carries elevated mercury levels, consistent with Sima Qian's account of mercury in the tomb.
--- fact 7
Sima Qian also reports a darker ending: that childless women of the palace, and workers who knew the tomb's secrets, were sealed inside after the burial; archaeology has not confirmed this account.
--- fact 8
In 1987 UNESCO inscribed the Mausoleum on its roll of the world's heritage.
--- context
The Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang belongs to the emperor who forged the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]] and who linked walls that became part of the [[place/great-wall-of-china|Great Wall of China]]; after the Qin came the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]]. It rests in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and it is threaded through the themes of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a pattern worth holding onto. A state that standardized script, weights and measures also built its afterlife army from standardized parts, so a grief-rite and a status-signal were raised with the same logic as the state's administration. That is the architecture of the whole site: the ledger and the tomb speak one grammar. The labor behind it, reported by Sima Qian in the hundreds of thousands, is a cost we must record as suffering, though the figure is uncertain. The reading rests on the modular construction that excavators have documented for the warriors, and it would weaken if the unopened tomb proves to differ sharply from the pits. How a state counts is how it mourns.
--- question 1

--- question 2
How many hands built the mausoleum, and under what conditions did they labor?

=== place/monte-alban
src: f65c98c1f019
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Monte Alban was the hilltop capital of the Zapotec people, crowning the Oaxaca Valley of Mexico; it was founded around 500 BCE and abandoned by about 800 CE, when the wind had its summit back.
--- fact 1
The city sits on a mountaintop leveled flat as a table, gazing out over three valleys.
--- fact 2
Carved stones known as Danzantes, dated to the city's early centuries, show figures bearing glyphs, voices in an early Mesoamerican writing tradition.
--- fact 3
In its Classic period the city swelled like a river in spring, to a population of well over ten thousand.
--- fact 4
It kept ties with Teotihuacan, and depictions at Teotihuacan show Zapotec visitors, travelers remembered in a stranger's city.
--- fact 5
In 1987 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list, its summit named among the world's treasures.
--- context
Monte Alban was a great seat of power in Mesoamerica in the age between the [[culture/olmecs|Olmecs]] and [[place/teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]], and one of the cradles of the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] in the region. It stands in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and it is woven into the theme threads of [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I think of the hands that leveled a mountaintop. I think of the water brought up from far below. I think of a capital placed there anyway, for defense and for display, and not for ease. What do we value, if not what we are willing to haul uphill? Where we build tells something about what we treasure.
