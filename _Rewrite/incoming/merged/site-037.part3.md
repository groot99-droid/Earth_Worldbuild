=== place/amazon-river
title: Amazon River
src: 5cadc6b34f3c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Amazon River runs about 6,575 kilometers through Peru, Colombia and Brazil and carries by far the largest volume of any river on Earth, a green-veined giant draining a rainforest basin long settled by farming and fishing societies before European contact.
--- fact 1
The Amazon's length, about 6,575 kilometers, and whether it or the Nile is the world's longest river are disputed, the answer shifting with which headwater and mouth channels are counted; but its average discharge at the delta, about 224,000 cubic meters per second, is a flood no other river matches.
--- fact 2
From at least the 1st millennium BCE, communities along the river and its floodplains raised fields, heaped mounds and made patches of enriched dark earth, evidence used to argue for larger and more settled pre-Columbian populations than once assumed.
--- fact 3
In 1541 to 1542 the Spanish expedition of Francisco de Orellana made the first recorded European descent of the river, reaching its mouth on 24 August 1542; Orellana's account of fighting women warriors is the traditional source of the river's name.
--- fact 4
Rubber tapped from Amazonian trees fed a boom from 1879 to 1912 that gilded cities such as Manaus and Iquitos, and it brought forced labor and violence against Indigenous peoples, with estimates of about 100,000 deaths across the region in that period; the boom collapsed after rubber plantations in Southeast Asia entered the market in 1912.
--- fact 5
Deforestation for agriculture, logging and mining has thinned parts of the rainforest, and great dams such as Belo Monte on a major tributary have displaced communities and bent the river's flow; how deep the long-term ecological damage runs is debated.
--- context
The Amazon's floodplain was home to Indigenous societies, among them the [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]] near its mouth, and its dark-earth soils are bound to the [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]] event and to societies such as the [[culture/casarabe-culture|Casarabe Culture]] farther upriver. European arrival on the river opened it to the wider [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], and its forests now stand at the heart of debates over [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]]. By the date_start used here it sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], within the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that outsiders long read the Amazon's forest as nearly empty of large societies, and that raised fields, mounds and dark earth are evidence used to challenge that reading. Here is the lesson for you: a landscape's story-glue can change as new evidence is found, and an absence you were taught may turn out to be a silence you were never taught to hear. The rubber boom shows the other face of the ledger, an exchange-web reaching into the basin and extracting wealth through sanctioned harm against the people living there, which the Observer records as suffering. The reading would weaken if pre-Columbian populations along the river prove to have been smaller and less settled than the dark-earth evidence suggests. What a forest holds depends on who is willing to look.
--- question 1
How large were the pre-Columbian populations along the Amazon, and how much of the "pristine forest" idea reflects population loss after 1492 rather than an original absence of large societies?
--- question 2
How much of the river's basin can bear further deforestation and damming before its water cycle shifts significantly?

=== place/antarctica
title: Antarctica
src: 040d45eab0c7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Antarctica is Earth's southernmost continent, a land of ice with no indigenous population, first sighted by ships in 1820 and reached at its geographic pole by Roald Amundsen in 1911; since 1961 it has been governed under a treaty that reserves it for peaceful, scientific use.
--- fact 1
Antarctica spreads across about 14,200,000 square kilometers beneath an ice sheet averaging about 1.9 kilometers thick, a frozen vault holding roughly 70 percent of the world's fresh water.
--- fact 2
The continent's coast was probably first glimpsed in 1820 by a Russian expedition under Fabian von Bellingshausen and Mikhail Lazarev, amid rival claims from British and American sealers in the same years; the first confirmed landing came in 1895, by a Norwegian party.
--- fact 3
Roald Amundsen's Norwegian team reached the geographic South Pole on 14 December 1911, weeks ahead of a British team led by Robert Falcon Scott, whose party died on the return journey across the ice.
--- fact 4
The Antarctic Treaty, signed in 1959 and in force from 1961, bars military activity, mining, and nuclear testing or waste disposal; it reserves the continent for peaceful and scientific use and sets aside territorial claims among its parties.
--- fact 5
About 5,000 people work at research stations across Antarctica in the southern summer; when winter closes in, they dwindle to about 1,000.
--- fact 6
Warming has been recorded across the continent: the Antarctic Peninsula's temperature has risen by about 3 degrees Celsius since the mid-20th century, and the West Antarctic ice sheet has lost roughly 100 billion metric tons of ice a year since 2002, old ice given to the sea and contributing to projected sea-level rise.
--- context
During the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]], alongside the [[event/space-age|Space Age]], Antarctic research became a proving ground for international science, and now its ice sheet stands at the center of [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]]. By the date of first sighting it sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], within the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Antarctica is the one large landmass claimed by no kin-lattice of its own. That changes the whole architecture of ownership: the claim-lines drawn across it are entirely the product of outside states, and the 1959 treaty is an unusual case of several such states agreeing to set territorial claims aside in favor of shared, peaceful use. Think of heirs who agree not to divide an inheritance none of them was born to; that is the covenant here. The race to the pole in 1911 was a status-signal fought over an empty landscape, and one side's deaths on the return march are recorded here as suffering in the pursuit of that signal. The reading would weaken if the treaty's restraint proves to depend mainly on the continent's low resource value rather than genuine cooperation, a test now approaching as mineral and fishing interest grows. Restraint is only proven when it costs something.
--- question 1
As demand for minerals and fisheries grows, how long will the Antarctic Treaty's ban on resource extraction hold?
--- question 2
How much of the continent's ice loss is now past recall on a human timescale?
