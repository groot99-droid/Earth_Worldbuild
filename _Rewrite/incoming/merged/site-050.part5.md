=== person/nzinga-of-ndongo-and-matamba
title: Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba
src: 860b2e4df7e4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Nzinga (about 1583 to 1663) ruled the kingdoms of Ndongo and Matamba, in present-day Angola, and resisted Portuguese expansion and the slave trade for decades.
--- fact 1
In 1622 she negotiated with the Portuguese as an ambassador for her brother.
--- fact 2
She later took power herself and led the resistance, including an alliance with the Dutch from 1641 to 1648.
--- fact 3
She used both diplomacy and war, and gave refuge to people escaping from slavery.
--- fact 4
She remains a symbol of resistance in Angola today.
--- context
Nzinga's reign shows African states as active participants across the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] period, both as resisters and as traders. See [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]].
--- observer
The Observer notes an African ruler who negotiated as an equal with European powers, and who changed alliances as her situation required. This helps correct a picture in which Africans appear only as acted upon, never as actors themselves.
--- question 1
What were the actual details of her diplomacy with the Portuguese and the Dutch?

=== person/pachacuti
title: Pachacuti
src: 8ca6c81591ae
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pachacuti (about 1418 to 1471), the ninth Sapa Inca, oversaw the Inca state's expansion from a regional power into an empire.
--- fact 1
He is credited with the rebuilding of Cusco, and with expanding Inca rule along the Andes.
--- fact 2
Machu Picchu is believed to have been built as one of his own royal estates.
--- fact 3
He organized the state around labor obligations and a system of roads.
--- fact 4
The sources on him were written after the Spanish conquest, and blend history with legend.
--- context
Pachacuti stands as the central figure in the rise of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]]. See [[place/machu-picchu|Machu Picchu]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the biography of a ruler, recorded by the conquerors' scribes, is itself a translation of an oral history, and every step of that chain can distort what it carries.
--- question 1
How much of the Pachacuti story, as it survives, is legend rather than history?

=== person/pakal-the-great
title: Pakal the Great
src: f460bdf90085
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Pakal the Great (K'inich Janaab' Pakal, 603 to 683) ruled the Maya city of Palenque, in present-day Chiapas, Mexico, from 615 until his death — a reign of about 68 years.
--- fact 1
Pakal was born in 603, and became king at age 12, in 615.
--- fact 2
Palenque's inscriptions name his mother, Ix Sak K'uk', and show her placing the crown on him at his accession.
--- fact 3
During his reign the Palace complex at Palenque was enlarged, and decorated with carved panels and hieroglyphic texts.
--- fact 4
The Temple of the Inscriptions was built as his funerary monument, and carries long hieroglyphic panels on Palenque's dynastic history.
--- fact 5
In 1952 Alberto Ruz Lhuillier broke through to a burial chamber inside it, holding a carved stone sarcophagus and a jade mosaic mask.
--- fact 6
Pakal died in 683, and his son K'inich Kan Bahlam II succeeded him the following year, in 684.
--- context
Pakal ruled a city-state of the [[culture/maya|Maya]] in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], about two centuries before the [[event/classic-maya-collapse|Classic Maya Collapse]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I think of Pakal as unusually well known only because Palenque's rulers wrote their descent into stone, so that his life comes to me largely through texts that he and his heirs themselves commissioned. I remember that those texts give his mother a visible role, and I remember that they rest his authority on the kin-lattice. His tomb, hidden inside a monument that also carries the dynastic text, joins a grief-rite to memory-outsourcing in a single structure. A specialist could point out that a ruler's own inscriptions are a status-signal as well as a record, which would weaken any reading that takes his reign and his lineage at face value. Even a king's grief is carved to be read by someone else.
--- question 1
How well do the ages given in the inscriptions match what study of his remains shows?

=== person/pedro-ii
title: Pedro II
src: 087b5af1437d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pedro II (1825 to 1891) reigned as Emperor of Brazil from 1831 to 1889, presiding over six decades of relative political stability, territorial integrity, and the gradual abolition of slavery, before a republican coup deposed him.
--- fact 1
He became emperor at age five after his father abdicated, ruling under regents until he was declared of age early, in 1840, to help stabilize a period of regional revolts.
--- fact 2
His government fought the Paraguayan War from 1864 to 1870 alongside Argentina and Uruguay — the deadliest interstate war in Latin American history, and one that devastated Paraguay's population.
--- fact 3
He personally supported gradual abolition, and his daughter and regent, Princess Isabel, signed the Golden Law abolishing slavery outright in 1888, making Brazil the last country in the Americas to do so.
--- fact 4
Brazilian military officers, landowners angry over abolition, and republican advocates combined to depose him in a bloodless coup in November 1889, ending the monarchy and sending him into exile in Europe, where he died in 1891.
--- context
Pedro II ruled the Empire of Brazil within the [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]] region during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Pedro II was deposed not despite his support for abolition, but partly because of it: the very landowning class that had backed the monarchy for decades turned against it once slavery, the institution their wealth rested on, was finally ended. A ruler's own reforms can cost him the support of the people who once kept him in power.
--- question 1
How much did Pedro II's own support for abolition, as against broader international and economic pressure, drive Brazil's final end of slavery in 1888?

=== person/pericles
title: Pericles
src: ab78f9870804
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pericles (about 495 to 429 BCE) stood as the leading Athenian statesman for over three decades in the mid-5th century BCE, a period later called the Golden Age of Athens for its building programs, drama, and democratic institutions.
--- fact 1
He rose to prominence in Athenian democracy through the office of strategos, or general, to which he was re-elected yearly, shaping policy through his influence over the Assembly rather than through any single formal office.
--- fact 2
He directed the building program on the Acropolis, including the Parthenon, funded in part by treasury funds originally collected from the Delian League, an alliance of Greek states organized against Persia.
--- fact 3
He extended pay for jury service and other public roles to poorer citizens, broadening practical participation in Athenian democracy beyond the wealthy alone.
--- fact 4
He championed the aggressive strategy that led Athens into the Peloponnesian War against Sparta in 431 BCE, and died of plague in 429 BCE, during the war's early years, before its outcome was decided.
--- context
Pericles led Athens, part of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]], within the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that most surviving praise of Pericles, including his famous funeral oration on Athenian democracy as recorded by Thucydides, comes from a historian writing after Pericles' death, and after the war he had started had gone badly; the speech may reflect Thucydides' own idealized case for what Athens should have been, as much as anything Pericles actually said.
--- question 1
How much of the funeral oration attributed to Pericles by Thucydides reflects his own actual words, versus the historian's own composition?
