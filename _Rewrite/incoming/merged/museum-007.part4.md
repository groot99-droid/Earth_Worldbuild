=== people--khadija-bint-khuwaylid--1
src: 1cda327bd0d9
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Khadija bint Khuwaylid (about 555 to 619) was a merchant of Mecca and the first wife of Muhammad. Her dates and many details of her life come from Muslim biographies written more than a century after her death, light reaching us from a distant lamp.

She belonged to the Quraysh tribe of Mecca and, according to tradition, financed trading caravans to Syria and hired agents to lead them across the desert roads.

Tradition says Muhammad led one of her caravans and that she proposed marriage, about 595. Accounts differ on her age then; a common figure is 40.

Muslim tradition holds that she was the first person to accept Muhammad's message after his first revelation, about 610.

Tradition names four daughters, Fatima among them, and sons who died young.

She died about 619, shortly before Muhammad's move to Medina in 622, and that year is remembered as the Year of Sorrow.

=== people--muhammad--1
src: eb0020c6ac92
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Muhammad (about 570 to 632 CE) was an Arabian religious and political leader, regarded by Muslims as the last prophet, who founded the community that grew into the Islamic world.

He was born in Mecca, and according to Islamic tradition he began receiving revelations around 610, later gathered as the Quran.

In 622 he and his followers migrated to Medina (the Hijra), and from that journey the Islamic calendar takes its start.

By the time of his death, most of the Arabian Peninsula had joined the community.

Islam is now followed by roughly a quarter of the world's population.

=== people--xuanzang--1
src: 9e3a9bb64fa2
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Xuanzang (602 to 664) was a Chinese Buddhist monk who journeyed overland to India from 629 to 645 in search of authentic scriptures, studied for years at the monastery of Nalanda, and came home to translate texts and set down the story of his road.

He was born in 602 and ordained as a monk in his youth. In 629 he left China without imperial permission, travelling west through Central Asia toward India, like a single traveller walking against the wind.

For years he studied at Nalanda, a great center of Buddhist learning, under the scholar Silabhadra, and he visited Buddhist sites across the subcontinent.

In 645 he returned to China bearing Buddhist texts, images and relics, and the Tang court welcomed him despite his earlier unauthorized departure.

With official support he led a translation effort that carried more than a thousand fascicles of Buddhist texts from Sanskrit into Chinese.

He also wrote the Great Tang Records on the Western Regions, a travel account whose distances and descriptions later helped archaeologists find sites such as Nalanda itself.
