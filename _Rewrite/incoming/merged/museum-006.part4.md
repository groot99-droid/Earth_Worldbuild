=== people--sappho--1
src: 1aa9ba1eb4a8
mode: confessional-poetry
--- description
I came for Sappho (about 630 to about 570 BCE), the Greek lyric poet from the island of Lesbos, whose songs of love, longing and ritual were collected in antiquity in at least eight books, possibly nine. What reaches me is fragments, and one complete poem.

I learn that she was born about 630 BCE on Lesbos in the eastern Aegean, in Mytilene or Eresos according to ancient sources, and that her dates are approximate. I learn that she made songs in Aeolic Greek to be sung with a lyre, and that the Sapphic stanza carries her name.

Ancient scholars gathered her poems in at least eight books, possibly nine. Only fragments survive, apart from one whole poem, a prayer to Aphrodite (Fragment 1). New papyrus fragments published in 2004 and 2014 added lines, among them a poem that mentions her brothers.

Many of the surviving poems speak desire for women. Scholars debate whether her circle on Lesbos was a religious association, a school for girls, or a social group.

How much of a voice is enough to love it? I remember that a fragment is still a voice.

=== people--confucius--1
src: 6da1b21abcb3
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Confucius (Kong Fuzi, 551 to 479 BCE) was a Chinese teacher and thinker whose ideas about ethics, family and government ran through East Asian societies for over two thousand years, like a river through many valleys.

His disciples gathered his sayings into the Analects.

At the heart of his teaching stand ren (humaneness), li (ritual propriety), and filial piety.

In his own lifetime he was not widely influential; his ideas rose to prominence under the Han dynasty.

Later, Confucian learning became the foundation of the imperial civil service examinations.

=== people--darius-i--1
src: 6cd9740af6bf
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Darius I (about 550 to 486 BCE) was king of the Achaemenid Persian Empire from 522 BCE. He set its provinces in order, began the building of Persepolis, and carved the tale of his rise in the Behistun Inscription.

Born about 550 BCE, he became king in 522 BCE after the death of Cambyses II, and in his first years he put down revolts that flared across the empire like grass fires.

The Behistun Inscription, cut into a cliff in western Iran, tells of his rise in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian. Scholars debate whether its account of a usurper, Gaumata, is accurate, and Aramaic copies of the text have been found in Egypt.

He divided the empire into provinces (satrapies) under governors and issued a standard gold coin, the daric.

He is credited with the Royal Road from Sardis to Susa, and he began the terrace at Persepolis about 518 BCE, though sources differ, and the earliest surviving remains there date to about 515 BCE.

His inscriptions honor the god Ahura Mazda.

=== people--sun-tzu--1
src: af781b6d1bcd
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Sun Tzu (traditionally about 544 to 496 BCE) is the name bound to The Art of War, the most influential text on military strategy from ancient China, and he is said to have served the state of Wu. Whether he was one historical person is disputed.

Tradition names him Sun Wu, a general who advised King Helü of Wu in the late 6th century BCE. The Art of War is ordered in about 13 short chapters.

The historian Sima Qian, writing centuries later, told how Sun Tzu proved his discipline by drilling the king's concubines and executing two who disobeyed. Scholars treat the tale as legend.

His name appears in no text earlier than Sima Qian's history, and one theory holds that "Sun Wu" was a made-up title, the Art of War truly the work of his supposed descendant Sun Bin.

In 1972 bamboo strips rose from a Han tomb at Yinqueshan bearing versions of both the Art of War and a separate text by Sun Bin, confirming that the Art of War circulated early and that Sun Bin was a distinct author.

For over two thousand years the text has shaped military and business thought, and it has travelled the world in translation, like seed carried on wind.
