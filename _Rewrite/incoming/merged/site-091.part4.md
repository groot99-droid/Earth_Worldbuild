=== species/sheep
src: 93eef9de62c2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic sheep was bred from the wild mouflon in Mesopotamia between about 11,000 and 9000 BCE, kept first for meat, milk and skins and only later bred for wool, and today its flocks number well over a billion animals across the world.
--- fact 1
Sheep were tamed from the mouflon, a wild sheep of southwestern Asia, in an estimated window between 11,000 and 9000 BCE in Mesopotamia, and the practice had reached Mehrgarh in the Indus Valley by about 7000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Early domestic sheep were kept mainly for meat, milk and skins, not fleece; archaeological evidence, statuary from Iran among it, suggests deliberate breeding for woolly sheep began around 6000 BCE, several thousand years after the first taming, a shift sometimes called the secondary products revolution.
--- fact 3
The earliest known woven wool garments date to around 4000 BCE, and Neolithic farmers in what is now France, among the Castelnovien people, were keeping domestic sheep by about 6000 BCE.
--- fact 4
Sheep are raised in great numbers today across Australia, New Zealand, the southern regions of South America, South Africa and the British Isles, and China and India also hold very large flocks by absolute count; the world's domestic sheep were estimated at around 1.2 billion in 2019.
--- context
Sheep were among the first animals drawn into [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] and, beside crops such as wheat and barley, formed one pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent; they were often herded with the [[species/goat|Goat]], tamed in a similar place and age. Sheep belong to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact. The gap of several thousand years between sheep domestication and deliberate wool breeding suggests that early herders did not tame the animal with fleece already in mind; they discovered a secondary use and bred for it only once the animal was settled in their kin-lattice. The Observer reads this as evidence that a domesticated species' eventual economic role can differ substantially from the reason it was first tamed. You rarely know, at the start, what a partnership will become. The animal's spread across so much of the pastoral world since then shows a tool-lineage of herding techniques travelling with the species itself. The reading would weaken if evidence of early wool use is found that predates the accepted timeline for woolly-sheep breeding.
--- question 1
What use did early herders make of sheep in the several thousand years before wool breeding began, beyond meat, milk and skins?
--- question 2
How far did sheep domestication in Mesopotamia shape, or draw on, the roughly contemporary domestication of goats in the same region?

=== species/soybean
src: 1dc1e287ecdf
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The soybean is a legume domesticated between the Yellow and Huai rivers in China between roughly 9,000 and 6,000 years ago, long at the heart of East Asian diets through foods such as tofu and soy sauce, and now grown across the world mainly as animal feed and oil.
--- fact 1
The soybean was tamed in north-central China, between the Yellow River and the Huai River, and its earliest evidence comes from the site of Jiahu in Henan province, dated to between about 9,000 and 7,800 years ago.
--- fact 2
Chinese tradition credits the legendary Emperor Shennong, placed in myth at 2853 BCE, with naming the soybean one of five sacred grains beside rice, wheat, barley and millet.
--- fact 3
Fermented soy foods such as soy sauce, fermented bean paste, natto and tempeh, with tofu made from soy milk, grew up over many centuries in East Asia, all but tempeh, which comes from Java in Southeast Asia; the earliest Japanese written mention of soybeans is in the Kojiki, compiled in 712 CE.
--- fact 4
The soybean came to the Americas and Europe in the 18th century, first recorded in North American fields in 1765 on Skidaway Island, Georgia, and in French fields in 1779.
--- fact 5
Genetically modified, herbicide-tolerant soybean varieties entered commercial use in the United States in 1996, covered about 17 percent of United States soybean acreage by 1997, and rose to about 93 percent by 2010.
--- fact 6
In 2019 Brazil, the United States and Argentina were the largest producers, together roughly four-fifths of world output, and about 80 percent of the global soybean crop goes to feed livestock.
--- context
The soybean's domestication is one of East Asia's founding gifts to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], grown beside rice and other regional crops; its turn to genetically modified varieties from the 1990s parallels the yield-focused breeding of the 20th-century [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]] in other crops. It belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the soybean has passed through two very different uses in its long relationship with humans. For millennia it was mainly a food, transformed by fermentation and pressing into tofu, sauce and other dishes across East Asia. In the last century it became mainly an input, grown at enormous scale chiefly to feed livestock and make oil, in a system resting on genetically modified varieties barely three decades old. This shift shows how a crop's tool-lineage of use can change faster than the plant itself: the bean stayed the same while its purpose was rewritten. The reading would weaken if the rapid adoption of genetically modified soy proves to have depended more on the sequence of regulatory approvals than on farmer preference.
--- question 1
How much of the soybean's shift toward livestock feed and oil, rather than direct human food, reflects changing global diets versus agricultural economics?
--- question 2
How did the arrival of genetically modified soybeans affect farming communities differently across producing countries?
