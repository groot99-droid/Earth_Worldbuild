=== species/potato
title: Potato
src: b95a5b5343d6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The potato is a tuber crop domesticated in the highlands of southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia, near Lake Titicaca, roughly 7,000 to 10,000 years ago, a treasure first hidden in mountain soil. After its introduction to Europe in the 16th century, it reshaped diet and population growth across that continent.
--- fact 1
The potato was drawn from wild relatives in the S. brevicaule species complex, in the lands around Lake Titicaca in southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia, an estimated 7,000 to 10,000 years ago; the earliest verified tuber remains, from Ancón on the central Peruvian coast, date to about 2500 BCE.
--- fact 2
Andean farmers raised thousands of potato varieties, fitted to different altitudes like stones to a terraced wall, and Quechua and Aymara communities traditionally made chuño, a freeze-dried potato product, to keep the crop through the region's cold nights.
--- fact 3
In Inca religious tradition the deity Axomama was bound to the fertility of potatoes and of the soil that cradled them.
--- fact 4
Spanish colonizers carried the potato to Europe in the second half of the 16th century; European farmers were slow at first to take it up, but after about 1750 it rose into an important staple, and the crop is estimated to have driven roughly a quarter of Old World population and urban growth between 1700 and 1900.
--- fact 5
Potato blight, caused by the water mold Phytophthora infestans, destroyed crops across Ireland and parts of Scotland from 1845, contributing to the Great Famine. Estimates of Irish deaths run from about 700,000 to over 1 million people, and roughly 1 million more emigrated; Ireland's population fell from about 8.4 million in 1844 to about 6.6 million by 1851.
--- fact 6
Global potato production reached about 390 million tonnes in 2024, a buried wealth led by China, India and Ukraine.
--- context
The potato was first tamed in the highlands around [[place/lake-titicaca|Lake Titicaca]] and grew into a staple of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]]; its voyage to Europe after 1492 is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] and, beside grains domesticated elsewhere, belongs to the wider saga of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]]. It stands in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and its roots run through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the potato's introduction to Europe shows a single crop reshaping a continent's demography over roughly two centuries, and that its dependence there on a small number of varieties left the food supply vulnerable to a single pathogen. The Andean growers had reduced that risk by keeping thousands of varieties in cultivation. Think of the architecture of a household budget: put everything into one account and a single failure empties it; spread it across many and no one loss is total. Diversity is a form of insurance. The Irish famine is recorded here as mass suffering caused jointly by a crop disease and by the political and economic arrangements, including food exports during the crisis, that shaped who could obtain food. Blight and policy share that ledger; neither can be read alone. The reading would weaken if the population and urbanization gains credited to the potato in Europe prove to owe more to other agricultural and economic changes of the period.
--- question 1
How much did the Andes' wide web of crop diversity reduce the risk of famine there, compared with Europe's narrower range of potato varieties?
--- question 2
How much of the Irish famine's death toll came from the blight itself, and how much from the political and economic decisions made during the crisis?

=== species/rice
title: Rice
src: 5962c231faed
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Rice is a cereal grass domesticated from a wild ancestor in the Yangtze valley of China from about 9,000 years ago. It is now the staple food of more than half the world's population, as if a river's patience had been poured into a single grain.
--- fact 1
Rice was tamed from wild *Oryza rufipogon* by Neolithic communities along the Upper and Lower Yangtze valley, in what is now China, about 9,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
Genetic evidence shows the gene that stops rice grains from shattering is identical in the two main cultivated groups, japonica and indica, which points to a single original domestication event rather than two separate ones; indica rice is thought to have arisen later, when japonica reached India about 4,500 years ago and hybridized with a local wild or semi-wild rice, two streams joining.
--- fact 3
Rice farming spread like water finding its level: it reached Korea and Japan between about 5,500 and 3,200 years ago, island Southeast Asia and Taiwan between about 5,500 and 4,000 years ago, carried in part by Austronesian-speaking migrations, and mainland Southeast Asia by about 5,000 years ago.
--- fact 4
Rice is the staple food of more than half the world's population, the grain in countless bowls, and China and India together consumed several hundred million tonnes of it in the early 2020s.
--- fact 5
The Green Revolution rice variety IR8 was bred in 1966 at the International Rice Research Institute by crossing an Indonesian and a Chinese variety, yielding a short, strong-stemmed plant that stood firm and did not fall under heavy fertilizer use.
--- fact 6
Global rice production reached about 800 million tonnes in 2023, with about 90 percent grown in Asia, and less than 8 percent of the crop crossing a border in trade.
--- context
The fuller tale of rice's taming is told under [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]], and it belongs to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] beside cereals domesticated elsewhere; its 20th-century breeding is part of the [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]]. Rice farming shaped the population and economy of the [[place/yellow-river|Yellow River]] region and other East Asian river valleys, as floodwater shapes a plain. It stands in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and its thread runs through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that rice farming, especially the flooded-field methods used across much of Asia, ties a community to sustained cooperative labor. Building and maintaining paddies, canals and terraces is a task larger than any one household. Imagine trying to hold back water alone: a breach in your neighbor's bank floods your field too. The Observer reads that as a kin-lattice pressure toward dense, cooperative settlement, an architecture of obligation poured into the land itself. That most of the world's rice is grown and eaten within Asia, with little traded across borders, shows a staple that remains tied to the regions that farm it rather than becoming a fully global exchange-good in the way wheat has. What we must tend together, we tend to stay beside. The reading would weaken if paddy-field cooperation proves to have mattered less to social organization than dry-field cereal farming elsewhere.
--- question 1
Was there truly a single domestication event for rice, or did separate lineages feed into the stream more than current genetic evidence suggests?
--- question 2
How did paddy irrigation systems shape the social order of the communities that built them and kept them whole?
