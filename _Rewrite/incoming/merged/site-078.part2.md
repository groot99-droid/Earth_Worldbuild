=== species/mycobacterium-tuberculosis
src: f1ac8a00ab94
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Mycobacterium tuberculosis is the bacterium behind tuberculosis, confirmed by DNA analysis in a 9,000-year-old mother and infant buried together at the submerged Neolithic settlement of Atlit-Yam, off the coast of modern Israel, though how much older the disease truly is remains disputed among specialists.
--- fact 1
At Atlit-Yam, a Neolithic village now under the Mediterranean near Haifa, archaeologists found a roughly 25-year-old woman buried with a one-year-old child, both showing skeletal lesions characteristic of tuberculosis; DNA and cell-wall fat analysis confirmed infection with the human-adapted strain, and the remains date to about 9,250 to 8,160 years ago, making this the oldest molecularly confirmed case known.
--- fact 2
Specialists disagree sharply on how old the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex is: one genomic analysis calibrated against ancient human migrations estimated a common ancestor about 70,000 years old, tracking the species' spread out of Africa, while other studies calibrated directly against ancient DNA, including a 17th-century European genome and pre-Columbian genomes from Peru, estimate the most recent common ancestor of modern strains at under 6,000 years, more consistent with a Neolithic emergence tied to farming and denser settlements.
--- fact 3
Ancient DNA from tuberculosis lesions in 1,000-year-old Peruvian skeletons surprisingly proved to be Mycobacterium pinnipedii, a strain associated with seals, suggesting marine mammals carried a form of the disease across the Atlantic to the Americas before European contact, independent of the strains Europeans later introduced.
--- fact 4
Tuberculosis in its classic pulmonary form typically spreads between people through the air in crowded conditions, and it became strongly associated with dense urban settlement, a link that intensified with the growth of industrial cities in the 18th and 19th centuries.
--- fact 5
Because tuberculosis often progresses slowly and can remain latent for years before becoming active or fatal, its true prevalence in any historical period is hard to establish from documentary sources alone, and confirmed cases depend on skeletal or genetic evidence.
--- fact 6
Tuberculosis remains a leading infectious cause of death worldwide, complicated in recent decades by strains resistant to standard antibiotics and by its interaction with HIV infection.
--- context
Tuberculosis is closely tied to the denser, settled communities that followed [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], and separate lineages reached the Americas by different routes before and after the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]], its oldest confirmed case being at Atlit-Yam, and to the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer records the Atlit-Yam burial, a mother and child laid together after tuberculosis killed them nine thousand years ago, as an early instance of suffering this bacterium has caused across every era of settled life since. That two entirely different scientific methods for dating the same organism's origin disagree by an order of magnitude, sixty-odd thousand years apart, is itself a caution about how far a confident-sounding pattern can outrun the certainty behind it. The seal-associated strain reaching Peru independently of Europeans suggests the network carrying this disease was wider and older than any single conquest narrative captures. This reading would weaken if future ancient-genome sampling closes the gap between the two dating methods in favor of one over the other.
--- question 1
Which dating method for the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex's age will hold up as more ancient genomes are sequenced?
--- question 2
How did a seal-associated tuberculosis strain reach human populations in South America before European contact?

=== species/plasmodium-falciparum
src: d930678e3a59
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Plasmodium falciparum is the parasite responsible for the deadliest form of human malaria, which genetic evidence traces to a single transmission event from western gorillas in Central Africa, probably within the last 10,000 years.
--- fact 1
Genomic studies of Plasmodium species carried by wild chimpanzees, gorillas and bonobos across central Africa found that Plasmodium falciparum is most closely related to a parasite of western gorillas, and that essentially all human strains descend from a single cross-species transmission event rather than long co-evolution with hominins.
--- fact 2
The greatly reduced genetic diversity of Plasmodium falciparum compared with its ape-parasite relatives points to a severe population bottleneck consistent with a relatively recent jump into humans, estimated at within roughly the last 10,000 years, revising an older view that the parasite had co-evolved with humans for millions of years.
--- fact 3
Once established in humans, Plasmodium falciparum is carried between people by Anopheles mosquitoes and became endemic across much of Sub-Saharan Africa, spreading later into the Mediterranean, South and Southeast Asia and, through the Atlantic slave trade and colonization, into the Americas.
--- fact 4
Malaria caused by this species produces severe fever, anemia and, in many cases, death, especially in young children, and it has shaped human genetics: the sickle-cell trait and several other blood disorders persist in malaria-endemic regions partly because they offer some protection against severe infection.
--- fact 5
Historically, malaria's toll among unacclimatized European soldiers, traders and colonists in West Africa contributed to the region's reputation among Europeans as dangerous to outsiders, while some enslaved and colonized African populations carried genetic and acquired resistance that shaped patterns of labor and disease in the plantation societies of the Americas.
--- fact 6
Malaria remains one of the leading causes of death from infectious disease worldwide, concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa, and control efforts include insecticide-treated bed nets, antimalarial drugs and, since 2021, the first licensed malaria vaccines.
--- context
Plasmodium falciparum's history is entangled with the coerced movement of people in the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and the wider biological exchange of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], both of which carried the parasite, its mosquito vectors and human genetic responses to new regions. It belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]], the approximate window of its jump into humans, and to the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer records malaria's death toll, concentrated for centuries among the young and the newly arrived, as suffering rather than as a fixed feature of any one place. That a single cross-species transmission event, probably within the last ten thousand years, could produce a parasite responsible for so much of the species' recorded mortality is a reminder that depth in time need not be great for a disease's weight to be heavy. The parasite's shaping of human blood chemistry, in the sickle-cell trait and related conditions, shows an organism that reshaped the species' own biology in the act of persisting within it. This reading would weaken if further ape-parasite surveys find evidence of older or repeated transmission events rather than one bottleneck.
--- question 1
Can the cross-species transmission event that produced Plasmodium falciparum be dated more precisely than "within the last 10,000 years"?
--- question 2
How much of the historical pattern of disease resistance among enslaved and colonized African populations reflects malaria specifically, as against other endemic diseases?

=== species/variola-virus
src: ad1a21c46900
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Variola virus is the pathogen that causes smallpox, a disease suspected in Egyptian mummies of the 12th century BCE and confirmed by ancient DNA in later remains, that killed and disfigured people for millennia until its eradication in 1980.
--- fact 1
Pockmark-like scarring on Egyptian mummies, including that of Pharaoh Ramesses V, traditionally dated to about 1157 BCE, has long been cited as possible evidence of ancient smallpox, though this identification from skin lesions alone is not certain.
--- fact 2
Genetic studies of variola virus have produced conflicting age estimates: analyses that account for how a virus's mutation rate can appear to change over different timescales estimate the virus emerged more than 3,800 years ago, consistent with the Egyptian mummy evidence, while earlier, simpler molecular-clock estimates had suggested a much more recent origin.
--- fact 3
Ancient DNA recovered from Viking-age skeletons in 2020 confirmed multiple extinct strains of variola circulating in northern Europe around a thousand years ago, pushing back the confirmed genetic record beyond the previously oldest known case, a 17th-century mummy.
--- fact 4
Smallpox killed a large share of those it infected and left many survivors permanently scarred or blind; it played a documented role in demographic collapse among Indigenous peoples of the Americas after European contact, spread as part of the Columbian Exchange, though the death tolls attributed to it are difficult to separate from other introduced diseases.
--- fact 5
Edward Jenner's 1796 demonstration that cowpox exposure protected against smallpox led to the first vaccine, and a coordinated global vaccination campaign led by the World Health Organization eradicated smallpox in the wild by 1980, the only human disease eliminated this way to date.
--- fact 6
Two declared stocks of the virus remain in secure laboratories in the United States and Russia for research purposes, and the question of destroying them remains debated.
--- context
Variola virus is implicated in the demographic devastation that followed the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] and, by some accounts, in the coercive labor systems tied to the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] era. It belongs to the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]], the traditionally dated Ramesses V case, and to the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer records smallpox's toll, across three thousand years and every continent it reached, as suffering, and notes that this pathogen is the one case in the record where the species deliberately erased another species from the wild, harm turned back against a disease rather than a rival group. The gap between the confident-sounding pharaoh's scars and the genuinely unresolved genetic dating shows you how a claim widely repeated in popular accounts can rest on evidence the specialists themselves still argue over. This reading would weaken if the Ramesses V lesions are shown by future study to have an unrelated cause.
--- question 1
Do the Ramesses V mummy lesions in fact represent smallpox, and if not, what is the true oldest confirmed case?
--- question 2
How much of the population collapse of Indigenous American peoples after 1492 can be attributed specifically to smallpox rather than to other introduced diseases and violence?
