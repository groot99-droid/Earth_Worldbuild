=== species/sugarcane
title: Sugarcane
src: b0195c3df66c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea and nearby parts of Southeast Asia around 6,000 years ago, and in its path you can trace a whole architecture of human appetite. It spread through Asia and the Mediterranean world over the following millennia; after 1492 it became the crop most closely tied to slave labor in the Americas. Sweetness, we learn here, keeps a ledger.
--- fact 1
Consider the lineage first: sugarcane was domesticated from the wild grass Saccharum robustum in New Guinea around 6,000 years ago, while a related domestication of Saccharum sinense by Austronesian peoples in southern China and Taiwan occurred by at least 5,500 years ago.
--- fact 2
The plant travelled by hand long before it travelled by ledger: Austronesian traders carried sugarcane to China and India by about 3,000 years ago, Persian and Greek writers encountered it in the 6th to 4th centuries BCE, and by the 8th century CE sugar was traded into the Mediterranean world as an expensive luxury spice from India.
--- fact 3
Then came the crossing: Christopher Columbus brought sugarcane to Hispaniola on his second voyage, and the first sugar harvest in the Americas took place there in 1501; Portuguese colonizers introduced the crop to Brazil, where by 1540, by one reported count, the island of Santa Catarina alone held about 800 sugar mills.
--- fact 4
Here the architecture turns grave. The Atlantic sugar trade formed one side of a triangular trade linking European manufactured goods, enslaved Africans, and New World sugar and other raw materials, and sugar plantations across the Caribbean and Brazil were worked overwhelmingly by enslaved labor; in Louisiana, enslaved workers helped make the state responsible for nearly a quarter of the world's exportable sugar by 1853.
--- fact 5
And the ledger today: global sugarcane production reached about 1.92 billion tonnes in 2022, with Brazil producing about 38 percent and India about 23 percent.
--- context
If you want the fuller story, it is told elsewhere. Sugarcane's post-1492 spread across the Atlantic world is described in more detail under the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]], and it cannot be separated from the history of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]; its introduction to Hispaniola is one of the earliest transfers of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. In the chronicle's architecture it sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a contrast in tempo. Across Asia, sugarcane spread slowly, over thousands of years. In the Americas after 1492, within a few decades, an exchange-web moved the plant, an enslaved workforce and a market for the finished product across an ocean at once. The Observer treats that compression as evidence of how quickly extractive systems can scale once the pieces are in place. Picture a machine assembled in one motion from parts already lying to hand: that is what scale means here.

The suffering of enslaved workers on sugar plantations is recorded here as central to the crop's modern history, not incidental to it. You cannot read the ledger of sweetness without reading their labor into every line. The reading would weaken if the Louisiana sugar economy's growth is shown to have depended as much on land and capital as on enslaved labor specifically. Speed, in such systems, is never neutral.
--- question 1
When medieval Europe paid for sugar as a "luxury spice", how much of that price reflected genuine scarcity, and how much a supply deliberately controlled along the trade route from India?
--- question 2
Measured against earlier sugar production in the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean worlds, how did the scale of the Atlantic sugar plantation system compare?

=== species/tea-plant
title: Tea Plant
src: 53d7f8be2c7b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The tea plant is native to the borderlands of southwestern China, northeastern India and northern Myanmar, and in China its leaves were drunk by at least the 2nd century BCE. Later its trade ran across the world like water finding every channel, and it helped provoke both British cultivation in colonial India and the Opium Wars with China.
--- fact 1
Chinese tradition tells that the legendary ruler Shennong discovered tea in 2737 BCE; yet the earliest physical evidence, tea leaves found in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han near Xi'an, dates to the 2nd century BCE, and the earliest written word of boiling tea surfaces in a text from 59 BCE.
--- fact 2
Under the Tang dynasty the drinking of tea, and the ceremonial culture that walked beside it, flowed out from China to Korea, Japan and Vietnam, and in Japan it took root as the practice later known as chado.
--- fact 3
The leaf crossed to Europe in the 16th century in the hands of Portuguese priests and merchants; in 1607 the Dutch East India Company shipped tea from Macao to Java, the first recorded tea shipment by a European trading power; and in Britain tea became the fashion after King Charles II married the tea-drinking Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza in 1662.
--- fact 4
Britain's thirst for Chinese tea grew until it opened a large trade imbalance paid for in silver; British merchants turned to selling opium into China to offset the cost, and that trade dispute escalated into the Opium Wars.
--- fact 5
The British carried Chinese tea plants to India in 1836, and in 1848 the East India Company sent Robert Fortune into China to obtain more; then planters found a distinct variety growing wild in Assam and hybridized it with the Chinese plant, the Alubari garden in Darjeeling opened in 1856, and the leaf reached Ceylon in 1867.
--- fact 6
Now the harvest is wide as a sea: global tea production reached about 29.76 million tonnes in 2022, with China supplying about 49 percent and India about 20 percent, and China, India, Kenya and Sri Lanka together account for roughly 81 percent of world output.
--- context
Tea's ceremonial culture was carried across East Asia in the age of the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], after earlier drinking recorded under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]]; the trade imbalance it later opened between Britain and China was a direct cause of the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]], and British cultivation of tea in India became a pillar of the economy of the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]]. In the chronicle it stands in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes an architecture of consequence: a plant first recorded as an elite and ceremonial drink later became the trigger for an armed conflict over a trade imbalance. What that shows you is how a taste adopted by one society can force hard choices on a distant government. Britain's substitution of opium for silver, and its later transplanting of tea cultivation to India, both read as attempts to escape dependence on a single exchange-web, the one by force, the other by relocation. Think of a household bound to a single merchant: it can threaten the merchant, or it can plant its own garden. The reading would weaken if the Opium Wars are shown to have had causes more central than the tea trade imbalance. A habit, once it becomes a need, writes its own foreign policy.
--- question 1
Before the two were joined, how distinct were the Assam and Chinese tea varieties, and how much did the discovery in Assam owe to prior local cultivation?
--- question 2
How much of the decision to trade opium for tea was a considered strategy, and how much an improvised response to a balance of payments worsening like a slow tide?
