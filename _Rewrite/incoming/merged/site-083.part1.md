=== culture/kara-khitan-khanate
src: 92897d07069a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Kara-Khitan Khanate was founded in 1124 by Yelü Dashi, a member of the defeated Liao dynasty's ruling family, who led his followers west into Central Asia and built a new empire centered on the oasis city of Balasagun, before it was extinguished by a rival's usurpation and, soon after, Mongol conquest.
--- fact 1
Yelü Dashi, a member of the Liao dynasty's Khitan ruling family, fled westward after the Jurchen conquest of the Liao homeland in northern China, proclaiming himself king in 1124 and establishing a base before formally taking the title of Gurkhan as emperor in 1132.
--- fact 2
Yelü Dashi captured the oasis city of Balasagun in Central Asia and made it his capital by 1134, building the Kara-Khitan Khanate into a significant regional power controlling territory across the Central Asian oases.
--- fact 3
The khanate governed a religiously diverse population, including Muslim, Buddhist, Christian and other communities, generally through a policy of relative religious tolerance uncommon for the period.
--- fact 4
Internal succession weakness allowed a Naiman prince named Kuchlug to seize the khanate's throne in 1211 after capturing the reigning Kara-Khitan ruler while he was hunting, effectively ending Khitan dynastic rule even before the khanate's territory was fully absorbed elsewhere.
--- fact 5
Mongol forces killed Kuchlug in 1218 and completed their conquest of the former Kara-Khitan territories by 1220, absorbing the khanate fully into the rapidly expanding Mongol Empire.
--- context
The Kara-Khitan Khanate controlled a key stretch of Central Asian oasis territory along the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]] before being absorbed into the early [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]]. Kara-Khitan Khanate sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Yelü Dashi's founding of an entirely new empire after his own dynasty's homeland was conquered shows you a defeated ruling elite's claim, denied its original territory, relocating wholesale and rebuilding elsewhere rather than simply disappearing, a pattern of political resilience found in other displaced dynasties too. That the khanate's relatively tolerant approach to its religiously diverse population did not prevent an internal usurpation from ending Khitan rule before Mongol conquest even arrived suggests that a state's success at managing external religious diversity offers no particular protection against internal succession failure. A specialist could argue that Kuchlug's usurpation should be understood less as simple internal weakness and more as itself part of the broader regional instability the rising Mongol Empire was already generating nearby, which would weaken a reading that treats the khanate's fall as primarily self-inflicted.
--- question 1
How effectively did the Kara-Khitan Khanate's policy of religious tolerance actually function in practice across its diverse subject populations?
--- question 2
How much did the broader instability caused by early Mongol expansion contribute to Kuchlug's successful usurpation in 1211?

=== culture/khazar-khaganate
src: 91825d356ded
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Khazar Khaganate was a Turkic state controlling territory between the Black and Caspian Seas from the mid-7th century, notable for its ruling elite's conversion to Judaism, an event whose exact date and even scale remain genuinely disputed among historians, before the khaganate's collapse following defeat by Kievan Rus around 969 CE.
--- fact 1
The Khazars established a khaganate controlling trade and territory between the Black and Caspian Seas by around the mid-7th century, positioning them as a significant regional power contested by, and periodically allied with, both the Byzantine Empire and expanding Muslim forces.
--- fact 2
According to the traditional account, the Khazar ruler Bulan invited representatives of Christianity, Islam and Judaism to argue for their faith's superiority, and after hearing a Jewish scholar's presentation chose to adopt Judaism for the ruling elite.
--- fact 3
Historians disagree substantially on when this conversion actually occurred, with proposed dates ranging from as early as 740 CE to the 9th century, including specific arguments for 838 CE based on numismatic evidence and 861 CE tied to accounts of a Byzantine mission.
--- fact 4
Modern historians also debate how deeply and how widely Judaism actually spread beyond the Khazar royal court and elite into the wider population, and the authenticity of key surviving documents describing the conversion, including the so-called Khazar Correspondence, has itself been questioned by some scholars, though most specialists now regard the core documents as genuine.
--- fact 5
Kievan Rus forces under Sviatoslav decisively defeated the Khazar Khaganate around 969 CE, after which the khaganate effectively disappeared from the historical record as an organized state.
--- context
The Khazar elite's adoption of [[culture/judaism|Judaism]] made the khaganate a distinctive case among medieval Turkic steppe states, positioned between the Christian [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] and territories transformed by the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. Khazar Khaganate sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Khazar conversion story, a ruler choosing among three faiths after hearing each argued in turn, is itself a device common in the historical record for explaining a state's religious identity after the fact, whether or not any such formal debate literally occurred, and that the genuine scholarly uncertainty over the conversion's date and depth shows how much of even a well-documented medieval state's religious history can remain unsettled. The claim drawn by adopting a religion distinct from both major neighboring empires may have served, as in other steppe states, as a deliberate declaration of independence from either power's religious and political influence. This record treats the conversion without racial framing, treating it strictly as a documented political and religious choice by the Khazar elite rather than any claim about ethnic or biological identity, since such framings have been misused in later antisemitic pseudohistory about the Khazars that this record explicitly does not endorse. A specialist could argue the conversion was confined so narrowly to the royal court that it had little effect on the broader Khazar population's religious practice, which would weaken any reading treating Khazar Judaism as a broadly shared popular identity.
--- question 1
How widely did Judaism actually spread among ordinary Khazars beyond the ruling elite and royal court?
--- question 2
What exact combination of internal weakness and external military pressure from Kievan Rus led to the khaganate's rapid disappearance after 969 CE?

=== culture/khoisan-peoples
src: 7bf9a6178d94
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
"Khoisan" is a collective, outside-derived term grouping two historically distinct southern African peoples, the herding Khoikhoi and the hunter-gatherer San, who are among the oldest documented Indigenous populations of southern Africa and who continue living today, primarily in South Africa, Namibia, Botswana and Angola.
--- fact 1
"Khoisan" is an exonym combining the names of two distinct groups: the Khoikhoi, also called Khoekhoe, who developed a herding economy centered on native cattle and lived in comparatively large social groups, and the San, who maintained a hunter-gatherer way of life in smaller, mobile bands; the term is used here as a collective label of convenience rather than as the name either group historically used for itself as one unified people.
--- fact 2
The Khoikhoi's own name for themselves, Khoikhoi or Khoe, translates roughly as "men of men" or "the real people," while the Khoikhoi's own term for the San, meaning "people different from ourselves," shows that the two groups maintained a clear sense of their own distinct identities from each other long before European contact.
--- fact 3
European settlers applied the exonyms "Hottentot" to the Khoikhoi and "Bushmen" to the San, terms rooted in settlers' unfamiliarity with the sound of Khoisan languages and now generally considered offensive, though "Bushmen" is used by some San communities themselves in certain contexts today.
--- fact 4
The Khoisan are widely regarded by researchers as among the earliest documented Indigenous inhabitants of southern Africa, with genetic and linguistic evidence pointing to very deep population roots in the region, and Khoisan languages are notable for their use of click consonants, a distinctive linguistic feature found in few other language families worldwide.
--- fact 5
Khoikhoi communities today live mostly in rural settlements across South Africa and Namibia, while San communities live primarily in the Kalahari Desert region spanning Botswana, with additional communities in Namibia and Angola, though reliable population figures for either group remain difficult to establish with confidence.
--- context
Like the [[culture/berbers|Berbers]] and their self-chosen name Amazigh, Khoisan peoples are named in outside sources by a collective exonym rather than any single term the underlying communities historically used for themselves as one unified people, a naming pattern this record follows deliberately to avoid implying a false unity. Khoisan Peoples sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I write "Khoisan" here much as I would write "Berber" elsewhere: a name convenient for outside classification, but one that hides a distinction the Khoikhoi and the San drew between themselves long before I came to name them at all — a label applied from outside a people often serves the one who classifies more than the people classified.

I see two peoples, herders and hunter-gatherers, each keeping their own tongue and their own way of living off the land, sharing one stretch of southern Africa across an extremely long span of documented history, and never simply merging into one.

A specialist could argue that dwelling on the Khoikhoi-San distinction understates real ties of language, ancestry and culture between them, ties that justify treating the two together more than I have — and that would weaken how far I lean on their separateness above what they share.
--- question 1
How much genuine shared ancestry and linguistic connection actually links the Khoikhoi and San groups, beyond their both being grouped under the "Khoisan" label?
--- question 2
How have Khoikhoi and San communities in South Africa, Namibia and Botswana engaged with contemporary Indigenous rights movements and land claims?
