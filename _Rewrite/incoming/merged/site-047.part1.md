=== person/cyrus-the-great
title: Cyrus the Great
type: person
date: c. 600 – 530 BCE
themes: war, religion
src: c15646d2f371
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In the 6th century BCE, Cyrus the Great raised the Achaemenid Persian Empire by conquering the Medes, Lydia, and Babylon.
--- fact 1
The date of his birth remains uncertain, placed by scholars somewhere between about 600 and 576 BCE.
--- fact 2
Around 550 BCE he defeated the Median king Astyages, and around 547 BCE the Lydian king Croesus.
--- fact 3
In 539 BCE, Babylon fell to him.
--- fact 4
Found in 1879, the Cyrus Cylinder — a Babylonian clay cylinder — preserves his own account of the conquest and of his restoration of gods and deported peoples.
--- fact 5
He died in 530 BCE on campaign at the eastern frontier and was buried at Pasargadae.
--- context
Cyrus raised the architecture of the largest empire the world had yet seen, the one later chronicled as the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]]. Both the Hebrew Bible and Greek writers, including the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]], treat him with respect. His reign belongs to the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], within the thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Cyrus's reputation for unusual tolerance rests mainly on his own inscription and on the accounts of those who benefited from his rule. Should evidence show that reputation to be a later projection rather than a lived policy, the reading would have to change.
--- question 1
Does the Cyrus Cylinder record a real policy, or mostly propaganda?

=== person/darius-i
title: Darius I
type: person
date: c. 550 – 486 BCE
themes: war, trade, language
src: afd201d7f0ad
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Darius I (about 550 to 486 BCE) took the Achaemenid Persian throne in 522 BCE. He gave the empire its provincial architecture, began building Persepolis, and set the record of his rise into stone at the Behistun Inscription.
--- fact 1
Born about 550 BCE, Darius I became king in 522 BCE after the death of Cambyses II, then spent his first years putting down revolts across the empire.
--- fact 2
Carved on a cliff in western Iran, the Behistun Inscription tells the story of his rise in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian. Whether its account of a usurper, Gaumata, is accurate remains debated among scholars, and Aramaic copies of the text have turned up in Egypt.
--- fact 3
He divided the empire into provinces, satrapies, under governors, and issued a standard gold coin, the daric.
--- fact 4
He is credited with the Royal Road from Sardis to Susa, and with beginning the terrace at Persepolis about 518 BCE, though sources differ and the earliest surviving remains there date to about 515 BCE.
--- fact 5
His inscriptions give honor to the god Ahura Mazda.
--- fact 6
After the Ionian Greek cities revolted in 499 BCE, he sent forces against Athens, whom the Athenians defeated at Marathon in 490 BCE.
--- fact 7
Darius died in 486 BCE, and Xerxes I succeeded him.
--- context
Darius ruled the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], the empire founded by [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], governing from the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]; see [[place/persepolis|Persepolis]]. His wars drew in the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Darius carved the story of his rise in stone, in three languages, on a cliff almost no one could read, then sent copies across the empire regardless. A multilingual empire needs a story-glue that holds across several sound-codes, and a winner's account of a contested succession is as much a claim as a record. Should the usurper story prove accurate, the reading would weaken, since the inscription would then sit closer to plain reporting.
--- question 1
Was Gaumata an actual usurper, or an invention of Darius's court?

=== person/dido
title: Dido
type: person
date: c. 814 – 760 BCE
themes: trade, kinship
src: 47b5167fae60
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Dido, also called Elissa, is remembered as Carthage's legendary founder-queen, traditionally credited with leading Phoenician settlers from Tyre to found the city on the North African coast around 814 BCE; her birth and death dates have no fixed place in history.
--- fact 1
The earliest known account of her founding of Carthage comes from the Greek historian Timaeus of Tauromenium, who wrote in the 4th to 3rd century BCE, centuries after the traditional founding date.
--- fact 2
Tradition holds that she fled Tyre after her brother, King Pygmalion, murdered her husband to seize his wealth, and that she later took her own life rather than marry a local Libyan king, Iarbas, who threatened war.
--- fact 3
No Phoenician inscription or contemporary record names a founder called Dido or Elissa; the archaeological evidence for Phoenician settlement at Carthage so far reaches back only to the last quarter of the 8th century BCE, about a century or more after the traditional founding date, though it may still fit a folk memory of an organized colonizing expedition.
--- fact 4
Her best-known appearance, as the doomed lover of the Trojan hero Aeneas in Virgil's Aeneid, is a literary invention: the Trojan War's traditional date sits centuries before Carthage's traditional founding, so the two could not have met.
--- context
Dido is remembered as the founder of Carthage, a colony of the [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]] in the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], placed by tradition in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I read Dido and I find a memory wearing a legend's clothes: a genuine voyage of Tyrian settlers, folded and folded again until it fit inside one woman's name. I know how founding stories work — they compress the long, the messy, the many, into the single and the named.

Then Virgil comes centuries later and gives her a lover she could never have met, and the legend is rewritten yet again to serve the next storyteller's purposes. What founding story is ever finished being told?
--- question 1
Does the Dido legend preserve any specific historical event, or is it wholly a later literary construction built around a folk memory of colonization?

=== person/diego-velazquez
title: Diego Velázquez
type: person
date: 1599 – 1660
themes: art
src: ca74b4efde60
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Diego Velázquez (1599 to 1660) stood as the leading painter of Spain's Golden Age, serving as court painter to King Philip IV for 37 years. His loose, luminous brushwork, turned on kings, courtiers and jesters without flattery, earned him the name a "painter's painter".
--- fact 1
Velázquez was baptised in Seville on 6 June 1599. At about 11 he entered the workshop of Francisco Pacheco, and in 1618 he married Pacheco's daughter, Juana. His early Seville work carries the influence of Caravaggio.
--- fact 2
In 1623 a portrait of the young Philip IV won him the post of court painter, and he moved to Madrid, rising through court offices until he became chamberlain of the palace.
--- fact 3
Peter Paul Rubens, on a diplomatic mission to Madrid in 1628, urged him toward Italy. His first Italian trip, 1629 to 1631, transformed his colour and handling; a second, 1649 to 1651, produced his portrait of Pope Innocent X.
--- fact 4
He painted Las Meninas in 1656, and in 1659 was admitted to the noble Order of Santiago, a rare honour for a painter. He died on 6 August 1660.
--- fact 5
He painted alla prima, often without drawing, altering compositions on the canvas itself; X-rays reveal many changes of mind. His palette stayed restrained, built on greys, blacks and earth tones.
--- fact 6
His work stayed largely inside Spanish royal palaces until the Prado opened in 1819. Manet travelled to Madrid in 1865 and called him "the painter of painters".
--- fact 7
Picasso painted a series of 58 works on Las Meninas and related figures in 1957, 44 of them variations on the whole painting, and Francis Bacon's screaming popes descend from his Innocent X.
--- context
Velázquez served the Spanish court in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], beginning under the influence of [[person/caravaggio|Caravaggio]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I stand before his brushwork and I see one honesty spent twice: on a king's heavy jaw, on a court dwarf's dignity, the same candour given to power and to the powerless alike, inside a palace built to display power and nothing else. I know his canvases stayed locked in those palaces until 1819 — so whatever influence he carries is mostly a nineteenth-century inheritance, arriving centuries late.

They call him "the painter of painters," but that is a lineage painters chose to tell about him, not one he was born into. What ancestry is not, in the end, a choice?
--- question 1
What do the X-rays' revealed changes of mind show about how he worked?

=== person/edvard-munch
title: Edvard Munch
type: person
date: 1863 – 1944
themes: art
src: 479face78c99
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Edvard Munch (12 December 1863 to 23 January 1944) was a Norwegian painter and printmaker who turned illness, grief, love and fear into images of inner states. The Scream grew into an emblem of the modern psyche, and he stands as a founding figure of Expressionism.
--- fact 1
Munch was born in Løten, Norway, and grew up in Kristiania, now Oslo. His mother died of tuberculosis when he was five, and his sister Sophie of the same disease when he was 13; another sister was later hospitalised for mental illness.
--- fact 2
In the 1890s he lived mostly in Berlin and Paris, developing the Frieze of Life cycle. His 1892 exhibition in Berlin closed after a week amid scandal, which made him famous across Germany.
--- fact 3
He suffered a breakdown in 1908 and was treated in Copenhagen. From 1916 he lived at his estate, Ekely, outside Oslo. In 1937 the Nazis labelled his work "degenerate" and stripped it from German museums.
--- fact 4
He simplified forms into flowing outlines and flat expressive colour, drawing on Van Gogh, Gauguin and Symbolism; his woodcuts and lithographs carry as much weight as his paintings.
--- fact 5
Key works include The Sick Child, 1885 to 1886; The Scream, 1893; Madonna, 1894; and The Dance of Life, 1899 to 1900.
--- fact 6
He left about 1,100 paintings, 4,500 drawings and 18,000 prints to the city of Oslo. The Scream has been stolen twice, in 1994 and 2004, and recovered both times.
--- context
Munch worked across Norway, Germany and France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], drawing on [[person/vincent-van-gogh|Vincent van Gogh]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I hear him say he painted what he had seen, not what he saw — memory, not the eye, as the source. I remember how early both his mother and his sister were taken, and I feel how easily that family record turns every picture he made into biography, whether he meant it to or not.

They called his work "degenerate" and pulled it from the walls of a nation, this same work now sitting at the center of the canon. What is a state, when it acts as a critic with the power to make art disappear?
--- question 1
How far should his family's history of illness govern the reading of his pictures?

=== person/eleanor-of-aquitaine
title: Eleanor of Aquitaine
type: person
date: c. 1124 – 1204
themes: kinship, war
src: 807cdbd72cb3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Eleanor of Aquitaine — about 1124 to 1204, though older sources give 1122 for her birth — reigned first as queen of France, then as queen of England, and stood among the wealthiest and most powerful women of 12th-century Europe as ruler of the Duchy of Aquitaine in her own right.
--- fact 1
She inherited the vast Duchy of Aquitaine in southwestern France in 1137 and married King Louis VII of France that same year; the marriage was annulled in 1152, having produced no sons.
--- fact 2
Weeks after the annulment she married Henry of Anjou, who became King Henry II of England in 1154, drawing her extensive French territories into the combined "Angevin Empire" that spanned much of France and England.
--- fact 3
She supported a rebellion by three of her sons against Henry II in 1173 to 1174; after its failure, Henry kept her confined for about fifteen to sixteen years, until his death in 1189.
--- fact 4
Freed by her son Richard I, "the Lionheart," she acted as regent during his crusade and captivity, and later helped secure the throne for her youngest son, John, after Richard's death in 1199.
--- context
Eleanor of Aquitaine ruled territory in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and her marriages tied together the crowns of France and England. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Eleanor's lasting power rested on her own duchy, not on either crown she wore by marriage: it was Aquitaine's wealth and loyalty that gave her leverage over two kings, and that let her outlast a long confinement to shape the succession in her old age.
--- question 1
How much independent political judgment did Eleanor bring to backing her sons' rebellion, versus simply acting on their initiative?
