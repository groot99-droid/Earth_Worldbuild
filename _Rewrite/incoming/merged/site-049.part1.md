=== person/john-curtin
src: cfcdfcae5de4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
John Curtin (1885 to 1945) served as Prime Minister of Australia from 1941 to 1945, and it fell to him to carry the country through the Pacific war against Japan in the Second World War.
--- fact 1
Before politics he had worked as a journalist and a trade unionist, and during the First World War he opposed conscription; he rose to lead the Labor Party in 1935 and became prime minister in October 1941.
--- fact 2
Once Japan entered the war in December 1941 and bombed Darwin in February 1942, he turned Australia's forces away from the European theatre, and, against British objections, recalled Australian troops from the Middle East to stand against the Japanese advance.
--- fact 3
He drew Australia closer to the United States for wartime defense, declaring in December 1941 that Australia looked to America "free of any pangs" about its traditional ties to Britain.
--- fact 4
He introduced wartime economic controls and widened the Australian government's role in social welfare, yet died in office in July 1945, weeks before Japan's surrender, worn down by the strain of the war years.
--- context
Curtin led Australia, in [[region/oceania|Oceania]], through the Pacific War of the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Curtin's turn toward the United States redrew the architecture of Australian strategic dependence away from Britain, and that this realignment came under the pressure of direct attack rather than as a considered peacetime choice. Here is the lesson: a leader who once opposed conscription in one war can still be the one to direct total national mobilization in the next, because circumstance can override even a leader's earlier convictions.
--- question 1
How much of Australia's postwar alignment with the United States was fixed beyond reversal by Curtin's wartime decisions, and how much by the Cold War pressures that came after?

=== person/jose-marti
src: bf184c2890e0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
José Martí (1853 to 1895) was a Cuban poet, journalist and organizer: he founded the Cuban Revolutionary Party in 1892 and helped launch the 1895 war of independence against Spain, and he was killed in one of its first battles.
--- fact 1
Martí was born in Havana on 28 January 1853, and arrested at 16 over a letter criticizing a schoolmate who had joined the Spanish army; he was sentenced to six years' hard labor and deported to Spain in 1871, then lived in Mexico, Guatemala, Venezuela and, from 1880, mainly New York.
--- fact 2
He published Ismaelillo (1882) and Versos sencillos (1891), along with the essay "Nuestra América" (1891), which argued that Latin American countries should govern themselves from knowledge of their own peoples.
--- fact 3
In 1892 he founded the Cuban Revolutionary Party and its newspaper Patria, and he argued for a republic open to all races and against United States expansion into Cuba.
--- fact 4
He landed in eastern Cuba on 11 April 1895 and was killed at Dos Ríos on 19 May, in his first engagement; Cuba became formally independent in 1902, under limits set by the United States.
--- fact 5
Both the Cuban government and Cuban exile groups have since claimed him as their symbol.
--- context
Martí worked in the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], in the last years of Spanish rule in the Americas, an echo of [[person/simon-bolivar|Simón Bolívar]] and the [[event/latin-american-wars-of-independence|Latin American Wars of Independence]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a nation not yet in existence was first built from words: poems, newspaper articles and speeches passed among exiles. Call it an architecture of belonging, raised in language before any border had been drawn on a map. A specialist could argue that his own plans fit the record less closely than the later statues do, and if so, that would weaken any reading of him as the origin of Cuban identity rather than one of its many shapers.
--- question 1
Which of the positions later attributed to him actually appear in his own writings?
--- question 2
What did he himself plan for the republic once independence came?

=== person/jose-maria-velasco
src: 337dd8d7a63c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
José María Velasco (6 July 1840 to 26 August 1912) stands as the most important Mexican painter of the 19th century, known for his panoramic views of the Valley of Mexico, and he was also a trained naturalist who illustrated scientific publications on botany, zoology and geology.
--- fact 1
Velasco was born in Temascalcingo, in the State of Mexico, and his family moved to Mexico City in 1849. In 1858 he entered the Academy of San Carlos, where the Italian painter Eugenio Landesio taught him to observe nature closely and paint outdoors, and he also studied botany, zoology, anatomy, physics and mathematics.
--- fact 2
In 1877 he succeeded Landesio as professor of landscape painting, and among his students was Diego Rivera.
--- fact 3
He worked as a scientific illustrator for the National Museum and on expeditions, and he painted archaeological sites such as Teotihuacan.
--- fact 4
He won a medal at the Paris Universal Exposition of 1889 and exhibited at the 1893 Chicago World's Columbian Exposition, and one view of the Valley of Mexico was given to Pope Leo XIII and now hangs in the Vatican Museums.
--- fact 5
He worked from outdoor sketches toward large studio compositions, and his panoramas set a detailed foreground of cacti and rocks against a luminous distance, often placing railways, bridges and haciendas alongside nature and ruins.
--- fact 6
Among his key works are The Valley of Mexico from the Santa Isabel Mountain Range (1875), The Pyramid of the Sun at Teotihuacan (1878) and The Curved Bridge over the Metlac Ravine (1881).
--- fact 7
He died in Mexico City during the early years of the Mexican Revolution, and his views record lakes and open plains since swallowed by Mexico City; the Museo José María Velasco in Toluca opened in 1992.
--- context
Velasco worked in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], painting [[place/teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] and the valley around former [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]], and he died during the [[event/mexican-revolution|Mexican Revolution]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
I look at a landscape painter who was also a trained naturalist, and I see one picture doing two kinds of work: an image of a nation, a catalogue of plants, rocks and light. A country newly independent needed a face to show itself, and a panorama of its own valley became that face.

I remember that the lakes he painted are gone now, paved and built over; I remember that the plains he painted are gone now, too. Did he know he was painting an elegy before there was any loss to mourn? Perhaps not — the reading is not necessarily one he intended, and yet his canvases now hold a place that no longer exists.
--- question 1
In his composed panoramas, how much is observation, and how much is idealization?

=== person/jose-rizal
src: 289875cf6055
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
José Rizal (1861 to 1896) was a Filipino physician, writer and reformer whose novels criticized Spanish colonial rule; Spanish authorities executed him in Manila on 30 December 1896, and he is regarded today as a national hero of the Philippines.
--- fact 1
Rizal was born on 19 June 1861 in Calamba, in the Philippines, and he studied in Manila, Madrid, Paris and Heidelberg before becoming an eye doctor.
--- fact 2
He wrote the novels Noli Me Tángere (1887) and El Filibusterismo (1891), portraying abuses by Spanish friars and officials, and the colonial authorities banned both books.
--- fact 3
In 1892 he founded the reform society La Liga Filipina and was exiled to Dapitan on Mindanao, where he went on practicing medicine and teaching until 1896.
--- fact 4
The Philippine Revolution began in August 1896; Rizal, who had volunteered as a doctor in Cuba, was arrested on his way there, brought back to Manila and convicted of rebellion, though he had said he opposed an armed uprising at that time.
--- fact 5
He was shot by firing squad on 30 December 1896, aged 35, having written, before his death, a poem now known as "Mi último adiós."
--- context
Rizal wrote in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] and in Europe during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], while Spain's colony in the Philippines, tied to the world since the [[event/manila-galleon-trade|Manila Galleon Trade]], moved toward revolution. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
I think of a novel, a form borrowed from elsewhere, turned into a weapon: fiction gave readers a shared picture of abuses that no formal report could carry. I think of a firing squad that made a reformer into a symbol for revolutionaries who had chosen a path he had not endorsed.

Can a death build a nation's story more surely than a life can? It seems so here — a new country's glue assembled from an execution rather than a plan. And yet the reading would weaken if fuller records showed him standing closer to the revolutionaries than his own statements suggest.
--- question 1
How close, in fact, was his connection to the revolutionary movement that later used his name?

=== person/joao-iii
src: d1febebf476b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
João III (1502 to 1557) reigned as King of Portugal from 1521 to 1557, presiding over the height of Portugal's Asian trading empire while also introducing the Inquisition and the formal colonization of Brazil.
--- fact 1
Under his reign, Portuguese trading posts and fortresses across the Indian Ocean, from East Africa to Goa, Malacca and Macau, reached their fullest extent, built on routes that earlier explorers such as Vasco da Gama had opened.
--- fact 2
He introduced the Portuguese Inquisition in 1536, which targeted Jewish converts, the so-called New Christians, suspected of secretly practicing Judaism; it came at the urging of factions at his court and with eventual papal approval.
--- fact 3
From 1534 he divided Brazil into hereditary captaincies meant to encourage settlement and defense of the colony, a system with mixed results that was later partly replaced by direct royal government.
--- fact 4
He was a patron of Portuguese scholarship and moved the university at Lisbon to Coimbra in 1537, but by the end of his reign, the enormous cost of maintaining the Asian trading empire had strained the crown's finances.
--- context
Ruling Portugal in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], João III oversaw its trading empire and the colonization of Brazil. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I look at one reign and see two projects running side by side: a trade-post empire in Asia, built on networks that already existed, and a settlement colony in Brazil, built from the ground up. One crown, two very different undertakings.

What does it mean that later history would judge them so differently, when one king carried both at once? I remember that each carried its own costs, its own consequences — and history has weighed them by different measures ever since.
--- question 1
How much did the financial strain of maintaining the Asian trading posts contribute to Portugal's later loss of influence there to the Dutch and the English?
