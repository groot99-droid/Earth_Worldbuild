src: 3f9ee53b9fcc

--- description
Christopher Dresser (1834 to 1904) was a botanist turned designer, now regarded as one of the first modern industrial designers. He worked as an independent consultant for dozens of manufacturers and produced geometric silverware and ceramics that anticipated 20th-century modernism. His career shows how knowledge in one field can become the architecture of another.

He was born in Glasgow in 1834 and trained at the Government School of Design in London. He lectured on botany, and in 1859 he received a doctorate from the University of Jena for research into plant structure.

He moved into design from about 1858, with ceramic work for firms from the 1860s onwards (sources differ on the start). He published The Art of Decorative Design (1862) and Principles of Decorative Design (1873), arguing that ornament should follow the structural logic of natural forms rather than copy their surface. Structure, not surface: that is the principle to keep.

In 1876 and 1877 he travelled to Japan, one of the first Western designers to do so, partly on commission from Tiffany & Co. to report on Japanese art and manufacturing.

He designed for Minton, Wedgwood, Coalbrookdale, Hukin & Heath and James Dixon & Sons, and was Art Superintendent of the Linthorpe Art Pottery. He died in 1904.

He reduced plants and shells to underlying geometry and embraced industrial methods, electroplating and moulded earthenware, that many reformers rejected. Key works include an electroplated teapot (1879), a toast rack (about 1880) and the Linthorpe "sea urchin" vase.

=== people--cixi--1
src: d7448b05f3f5

--- description
The Empress Dowager Cixi (1835 to 1908) was the dominant political figure of late Qing China, ruling as regent for most of the years from 1861 to 1908.

In 1861 she became regent for her young son, the Tongzhi Emperor, and later for the Guangxu Emperor.

Her years include the Self-Strengthening Movement, an effort to renew China's defenses and industry.

In 1900 she supported the Boxers, and after their defeat she pursued the 'New Policies' reforms.

Historians debate her legacy. Older accounts paint her as a reactionary; recent work stresses her political skill and the limits that hemmed her in like walls around a garden.
