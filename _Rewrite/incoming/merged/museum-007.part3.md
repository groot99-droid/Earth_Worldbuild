=== people--amanitore--1
src: 34451ccce1da
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Amanitore was a queen of the kingdom of Kush who co-ruled from the capital Meroe alongside a king, Natakamani, usually thought to be her son, during a prosperous period in the middle of the 1st century CE. Her story is a lesson in reading shared power from images.

Kushite royal women held the title kandake, often rendered "candace." Inscriptions and temple reliefs show Amanitore with the same royal regalia and war imagery as Natakamani, suggesting genuinely shared rule rather than a purely ceremonial role. In a visual grammar of kingship, equal regalia is an argument.

Together the pair sponsored a major building program, including temples at Meroe, Naqa and Amara, among the best-preserved Kushite monuments to survive.

A relief at the Lion Temple at Naqa shows Amanitore, like Natakamani, smiting bound captives, an image of royal power normally reserved for kings elsewhere in the Nile Valley tradition.

Her exact reign dates are fixed by no king list. Estimates for the coregency center on the middle of the 1st century CE, though some run earlier, and it is sometimes placed as contemporary with the Roman emperor Nero. Where the list is silent, the stones must speak.

=== people--cleopatra-vii--1
src: b08a6bfed457
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Cleopatra VII was the last active ruler of the Ptolemaic Kingdom of Egypt. She allied with Julius Caesar and Mark Antony, and she died in 30 BCE after their defeat by Octavian. Strip away the legend and you find a dynasty's final chapter.

She belonged to the Ptolemaic dynasty, founded by a Macedonian general of Alexander the Great.

Ancient sources say she spoke several languages and, unlike most of her Ptolemaic predecessors, learned Egyptian. Learning the language of the people you rule is its own kind of covenant.

She had a son with Julius Caesar and children with Mark Antony.

Octavian defeated Antony and Cleopatra at the Battle of Actium in 31 BCE.

After her death in 30 BCE, Egypt became a Roman province.

=== people--ezana-of-aksum--1
src: 7e4efb494d62
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Ezana was king of Aksum, in present-day Ethiopia and Eritrea, from about 320 to about 360 CE, though sources differ on the exact dates. He is known for taking up Christianity and for a campaign in the Nile valley, and his inscriptions and coins bear witness to both.

He came to the throne as a child and ruled the Kingdom of Aksum from about 320 until about 360. Dates for his life and reign are approximate, and the sources differ.

His early inscriptions and coins show the older Aksumite religion, a crescent and disc shining on his coins.

Frumentius, a Christian from Tyre (described as Phoenician, and in some accounts Syrian) who rose in the Aksumite court, is reported to have been consecrated Aksum's first bishop by Athanasius of Alexandria, traditionally in 328, though other accounts say between 340 and 346.

Ezana converted to Christianity in the 4th century, though the exact date is uncertain. His later inscriptions call on a "Lord of Heaven," and on his coins the crescent and disc give way to the cross, often described as among the earliest coins to do so, as one light replaces another in the sky.

His inscriptions are in Ge'ez, Sabaean and Greek.

=== people--trung-sisters--1
src: 72a7d73e89d9
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
The Trưng Sisters, Trưng Trắc and Trưng Nhị (Chinese Zheng Ce and Zheng Er), led a revolt against Han Chinese rule in northern Vietnam from 40 to 43 CE. Their birth years are unknown; the dates given here are those of the revolt.

They came from Mê Linh in the Red River region, then part of the Han commandery of Jiaozhi, where the river runs red with silt to the sea.

They raised the revolt in 40 CE. Chinese sources tie it to harsh rule by the Han governor Su Ding, and Vietnamese tradition adds that he executed Trưng Trắc's husband, Thi Sách.

They took towns in several southern commanderies and are recorded as ruling as queens for about three years.

The Han general Ma Yuan led an army south and had defeated them by 43 CE.

The Chinese history Hou Hanshu says they were captured and beheaded, while Vietnamese tradition says they drowned themselves in a river; this second account is traditional.
