--- summary
Language and writing are the twin systems through which people communicate and store what they know: speech, writing systems, numerals, record-keeping devices such as knotted cords, and the media that copy and carry all of it forward. Spoken language is universal and very old, though exactly when it arose stays uncertain; writing appeared much later, invented independently in only a few places, and then spread by contact, conquest, religion, and trade. This hub follows them from the symbolic behavior of early Homo sapiens, through clay tablets, alphabets, paper, and printing, to the telegraph and digital networks, and it also traces the spread of language families across continents. It gives equal weight to peoples who kept their records without alphabets at all.
--- observer
The Observer names these the sound-code and the mark-code. It notes that language is the species' main means of piling up knowledge across generations, and that the mark-code turns that accumulation into memory-outsourcing on a scale no single lifetime could ever hold; see [[observer-note/the-great-outsourcing-of-memory|The Great Outsourcing of Memory]]. The record shows the sound-code as universal and old, and the mark-code as late — independently invented in only a few places, and unevenly adopted, with some peoples running large states on cords, oral epics, and memorized texts instead. What gets written also shapes what actually gets remembered; see [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]]. This reading would weaken if unwritten systems such as oral epics and cord records turn out to preserve knowledge across centuries just as reliably as writing does.

--- extra 1: Key Threads
- Spoken language runs back at least many tens of thousands of years and stands universal in [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]], a species marked out by language, symbolic thought, and cumulative culture. Speech leaves no direct fossil trace, so its date has to be inferred from indirect evidence such as symbolic behavior; see [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]].
- Writing itself was invented independently at least three or four times. The first came in Mesopotamia about 3400 to 3200 BCE, where the earliest proto-cuneiform tablets from [[place/uruk|Uruk]] are simple accounting records ([[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]], [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]]). Egyptian hieroglyphs appear by about 3200 BCE ([[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]]), and Chinese writing stands securely attested by about 1250 BCE in the oracle-bone inscriptions of the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]].
- Bronze Age states left behind archives in scripts that took long ages to read, or that remain unread still. The [[culture/hittites|Hittites]] wrote in cuneiform at Hattusa and spoke the oldest recorded Indo-European language; the [[culture/mycenaean-greeks|Mycenaean Greeks]] wrote Linear B, an early form of Greek, deciphered only in 1952; the [[culture/minoans|Minoans]] used Linear A and Cretan hieroglyphs, neither of which has been deciphered; and the script of the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]] has not been deciphered either.
- The [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]] then put literacy within easier reach. The first alphabet, Proto-Sinaitic, appeared about 1800 BCE, adapted by Semitic-speaking workers from Egyptian hieroglyphs. The [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]] spread an alphabet of about 22 letters, the Greeks added vowels in the 8th century BCE, and Latin, Cyrillic, Hebrew, Arabic, and many Indian scripts descend from or stand related to those early alphabets. The Nabataean script is widely considered an ancestor of the Arabic script ([[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]]), and the [[culture/kingdom-of-saba|Kingdom of Saba]] wrote in the South Arabian script.
- Mesoamerican writing appears by roughly 600 BCE, and possibly even earlier than that. [[culture/maya|Maya]] writing combines signs for words and syllables and stands largely deciphered, and the Danzantes carvings at [[place/monte-alban|Monte Alban]] show glyphs from an early Mesoamerican writing tradition.
- Not every complex society leaned on writing at all. The [[technology/quipu|Quipu]] recorded numbers on knotted cords in the Andes, and whether the cords also encoded other information stays debated; the Vedas were transmitted orally for centuries through elaborate memorization ([[event/vedic-period|Vedic Period]]); griots preserved the epic of [[person/sundiata-keita|Sundiata Keita]]; and [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]] passed on knowledge through oral traditions, songlines, and ceremony.
- Rulers leaned on writing and standard scripts to govern. The [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]] standardized the script, [[person/ashoka|Ashoka]] had edicts inscribed on rocks and pillars, [[person/hammurabi|Hammurabi]]'s law code was carved on a stone stele and put on public display, the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] kept a library at Nineveh with tens of thousands of clay tablets, and the [[place/library-of-alexandria|Library of Alexandria]] supported scholarship across the Hellenistic world.
- Decipherment has always depended on bilingual texts and on chance finds. The [[technology/rosetta-stone|Rosetta Stone]], inscribed in 196 BCE in hieroglyphs, Demotic, and Greek, was found in 1799, and Champollion announced his decipherment of hieroglyphs in 1822. The Orkhon inscriptions of the [[culture/gokturk-khaganate|Göktürk Khaganate]], erected in 732 to 735 in Mongolia and discovered in 1889, stand as the oldest known Turkic writing, and the Meroitic script of the [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]] has been read phonetically even though the language itself is not well understood.
- Paper and print together lowered the cost of copying. [[technology/paper|Paper]] was developed in China by the 2nd century CE, [[technology/woodblock-printing|Woodblock Printing]] produced the Diamond Sutra of 868, and [[person/johannes-gutenberg|Johannes Gutenberg]]'s [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]] of about 1440 spread across Europe within just a few decades. Printing then helped carry Reformation pamphlets, and Luther's German New Testament appeared in 1522 and the whole Bible in 1534 ([[event/protestant-reformation|Protestant Reformation]]).
- Languages spread as administrative tongues and as lingua francas. Greek spread widely through [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]] and blended with local traditions; Latin and Greek together tied the [[culture/romans|Romans]]' world together; the language of the [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] served as a lingua franca all along the Silk Roads; Abd al-Malik made Arabic the language of government under the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]]; and Swahili, a Bantu language carrying many Arabic loanwords, grew up on the East African coast ([[culture/swahili-coast-city-states|Swahili Coast City-States]]).
- Translation carried knowledge across languages. The [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]] rendered Greek, Persian, Syriac, and Indian works into Arabic, and scholars such as [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]] carried the decimal place-value system with zero further west ([[technology/zero-and-place-value-notation|Zero and Place-Value Notation]]); Fibonacci's 1202 book then popularized it across Europe.
- Language families spread right alongside migrations. The [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]] carried a family now spoken as a first language by roughly 40 to 46 percent of the world's population, though its homeland stays disputed; Austronesian languages spread from Taiwan from about 5,000 to 5,500 years ago as far as Madagascar and Rapa Nui, and the family now holds well over a thousand languages ([[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]]); and the [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]], over about four thousand years, gave rise to roughly 500 languages spoken today by about 350 million people.
- Language and law also meet in modern treaties. The English and Maori texts of the [[event/treaty-of-waitangi|Treaty of Waitangi]] differ in key words about sovereignty and chieftainship, and te reo Maori became an official language of New Zealand in 1987 ([[culture/maori|Maori]]); Article 17 of the Treaty of Wuchale read differently in Amharic and Italian, and that dispute led to war ([[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]]).
- The telegraph and digital media stand as the latest forms of all. [[technology/telegraph-and-submarine-cables|Telegraph and Submarine Cables]] let messages cross whole oceans in minutes from the 1840s, and the [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]] and the [[event/digital-revolution|Digital Revolution]] brought the internet (1969), the World Wide Web (1989 to 1991), and more than five billion internet users; see also [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]].
--- extra 2: Through the Eras
- **Paleolithic:** [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] and the symbolic behavior of [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]]. Speech has to be inferred rather than preserved, and the earliest evidence comes as engraved ochre, beads, and cave paintings; see [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]].
- **Neolithic:** There was no writing yet, but farming itself may have carried language families along with it. The [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] relied on a crop package that included rice, and one hypothesis links the origin of Indo-European to Anatolia and the spread of farming, though this stays debated ([[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]], [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]]).
- **Bronze Age:** [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]], the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]], [[place/uruk|Uruk]], [[person/hammurabi|Hammurabi]], the [[culture/hittites|Hittites]], the [[culture/mycenaean-greeks|Mycenaean Greeks]], the [[culture/minoans|Minoans]], the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]], the [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]], the oral [[event/vedic-period|Vedic Period]], and the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]].
- **Iron Age:** The [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]], the [[culture/kingdom-of-saba|Kingdom of Saba]], the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]], and the [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]].
- **Classical Antiquity:** [[person/aristotle|Aristotle]], [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]], the [[culture/romans|Romans]], the [[place/library-of-alexandria|Library of Alexandria]], the [[technology/rosetta-stone|Rosetta Stone]], [[technology/paper|Paper]], the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]] and [[person/qin-shi-huang|Qin Shi Huang]], [[person/ashoka|Ashoka]], [[place/monte-alban|Monte Alban]], the [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]], and [[technology/zero-and-place-value-notation|Zero and Place-Value Notation]].
- **Medieval Period:** The [[culture/maya|Maya]], the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]], [[technology/woodblock-printing|Woodblock Printing]], the [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]], [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]], the [[culture/gokturk-khaganate|Göktürk Khaganate]], the [[culture/swahili-coast-city-states|Swahili Coast City-States]], the [[technology/quipu|Quipu]], [[person/sundiata-keita|Sundiata Keita]], [[person/ibn-battuta|Ibn Battuta]], whose account was dictated to the scholar Ibn Juzayy, and [[person/johannes-gutenberg|Johannes Gutenberg]] and the [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]].
- **Early Modern Period:** The [[event/protestant-reformation|Protestant Reformation]] and the spread of print across the whole of Europe. English words including canoe, hammock, hurricane, and barbecue all come from [[culture/taino|Taino]].
- **Industrial Age:** The [[technology/rosetta-stone|Rosetta Stone]] was found in 1799 and hieroglyphs were deciphered in 1822; [[person/usman-dan-fodio|Usman dan Fodio]] wrote widely in Arabic and Fulfulde; and the [[technology/telegraph-and-submarine-cables|Telegraph and Submarine Cables]] (from 1844), the differing English and Maori texts of the [[event/treaty-of-waitangi|Treaty of Waitangi]] (1840), and the differing Amharic and Italian texts behind the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]] (1896) show how both wording and the speed of transmission together shaped politics.
- **Information Age:** The [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]], the [[event/digital-revolution|Digital Revolution]], and the questions about authorship and archives raised in [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]].
--- extra 3: Regional Patterns
- **[[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]]:** Writing began here, in Mesopotamia and Egypt, and the alphabet arose among Semitic-speaking workers in the Levant; the Hittites and Mycenaeans show writing systems carried by different language families.
- **[[region/east-asia|East Asia]]:** Oracle-bone writing under the Shang, script standardization under Qin, and the invention of paper, woodblock printing, and movable type (in China about 1040 and in Korea in metal in 1377) together made East Asia a true center of the written word; see [[technology/paper|Paper]] and [[technology/woodblock-printing|Woodblock Printing]].
- **[[region/south-asia|South Asia]]:** The Vedas were transmitted purely orally, the decimal place-value system with zero developed here, Ashoka's edicts were inscribed clear across the subcontinent, and the Indus script remains undeciphered to this day.
- **[[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] and the [[region/andes|Andes]]:** Mesoamerica developed full writing systems, of which Maya writing stands as the best known, and the Andes developed cord records in the [[technology/quipu|Quipu]] without any conventional writing at all; the two show entirely different routes to keeping records.
- **[[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]]:** The [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]] spread roughly 500 languages, Swahili developed along the coast, griots kept the oral epics alive, and [[place/timbuktu|Timbuktu]] still holds hundreds of thousands of historical manuscripts.
- **[[region/oceania|Oceania]] and [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]]:** Austronesian languages spread from Taiwan through the islands to Rapa Nui ([[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]]), [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]] spoke more than 250 languages before 1788, and te reo Maori became an official language of New Zealand ([[culture/maori|Maori]]).
- **[[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]] and the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]]:** The [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] spread a lingua franca along the Silk Roads, the Orkhon inscriptions preserve early Turkic writing, and paper mills were already operating in [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]] and Baghdad by the later 8th century.
- **[[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]]:** The [[culture/yakut-sakha|Yakut (Sakha)]] are a Turkic-speaking people of northeastern Siberia, a region that came under Russian rule from the 17th century ([[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]]).
--- extra 4: Debates and Limits
- When and how spoken language actually arose remains unknown. Speech leaves no fossil at all, and estimates rest on indirect evidence from anatomy, symbolic behavior, and genetics, so they range widely.
- Scholars agree that writing arose independently in more than one place, but not on exactly how many. This hub counts at least three or four, and whether Egyptian hieroglyphs arose independently of Mesopotamian writing has long been debated; the date of the earliest Mesoamerican writing stays uncertain too.
- Several scripts remain undeciphered or only partly understood: the Indus script, Linear A, and Cretan hieroglyphs stay unread, the language behind Meroitic is not well understood, and whether the quipu encode more than numbers is still debated ([[technology/quipu|Quipu]]).
- Language families, genes, and archaeology never map neatly onto one another. The homeland of Indo-European is disputed, the [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]] involved intermarriage and language shift as well as outright migration, and counts of languages depend heavily on how a language is even defined (estimates of the Bantu languages range from about 440 to 680).
- This hub's coverage stays uneven. It has no notes yet on the Chinese, Arabic, or Cyrillic scripts as topics in their own right, on language endangerment and loss, or on signed languages, and it has few notes on the languages of Oceania, the Americas, and Siberia.
