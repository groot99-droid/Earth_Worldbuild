=== people--usman-dan-fodio--1
src: 74b088254784
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Usman dan Fodio (1754 to 1817) was a Fulani scholar and religious leader who led the jihad from which the Sokoto Caliphate was born.

He taught and preached in the Hausa state of Gobir, and he wrote widely in Arabic and Fulfulde, his words travelling far beyond the places he stood.

In 1804 he and his followers left Gobir, and in February of that year the jihad began.

He is regarded as the founder of the Sokoto Caliphate and its first sultan or caliph.

His brother and his son helped to govern the new state.

I stand in the room of Katsushika Hokusai (1760 to 1849), Japanese ukiyo-e artist from Edo, creator of The Great Wave off Kanagawa, who made paintings and book illustrations and, in his seventies, the landscape print series that made him world-famous.

I read that he was born in 1760 in the Katsushika district of Edo (modern Tokyo); that as a teenager he worked in a lending library and trained as a woodblock carver; that about 18 he entered the studio of Katsukawa Shunshō. I read that he changed his artistic name more than 30 times and is said to have moved house more than 90 times, that he lived in poverty for much of his life, and that his daughter Katsushika Ōi became a fine painter and assisted him in his old age.

In 1814 he began publishing the Hokusai Manga, fifteen volumes of sketches. About 1830 to 1832 he designed Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, which includes The Great Wave off Kanagawa (about 1831). Ukiyo-e was collaborative: he drew the design, specialist carvers cut it into blocks, one for each colour, and printers pressed the paper by hand. The Fuji series used the newly imported Prussian blue pigment.

In the postscript to One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji (1834) he wrote that nothing he drew before age 70 was worth noticing. He died in Edo on 10 May 1849, at 88, reportedly saying that with five more years he could have become a true painter.

Age 70, and only then a beginning. Is it ever too late to start being good? Not while there is a brush in the hand.

Katsushika Hokusai (1760 to 1849) was a Japanese ukiyo-e artist of Edo, maker of The Great Wave off Kanagawa. He made paintings and book illustrations and, in his seventies, the landscape print series that carried his name around the world like a wave crossing an ocean.

He was born in 1760 in the Katsushika district of Edo (modern Tokyo). As a youth he worked in a lending library and trained as a woodblock carver, and about 18 he entered the studio of Katsukawa Shunshō.

He changed his artistic name more than 30 times and is said to have moved house more than 90 times, restless as weather. For much of his life he lived in poverty. His daughter Katsushika Ōi became a fine painter and helped him in his old age.

In 1814 he began publishing the Hokusai Manga, fifteen volumes of sketches. About 1830 to 1832 he designed Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, which holds The Great Wave off Kanagawa (about 1831).

In the postscript to One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji (1834) he wrote that nothing he drew before age 70 was worth noticing. He died in Edo on 10 May 1849, at 88, reportedly saying that with five more years he could have become a true painter.

Ukiyo-e was an art of many hands: he drew the design, specialist carvers cut it into blocks, one for each colour, and printers pressed the paper by hand. The Fuji series used the newly imported Prussian blue pigment, deep as open water.

=== people--sequoyah--1
src: c3dbe80e9897
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Sequoyah (about 1770 to 1843) was a Cherokee silversmith who created the Cherokee syllabary, a writing system for the Cherokee language, completed by 1821. It is one of the few writing systems whose invention by a single known person is well documented. Think about what that means: one person gave a whole people a new architecture for memory.

He was born about 1770 in the Cherokee town of Tuskegee, in present-day Tennessee; the birth year is approximate.

He did not read or write English. He began work on a writing system for Cherokee about 1809 and completed it by 1821. You do not need to master someone else's script to invent your own.

The syllabary has 85 characters (86 in early versions), each standing for a syllable.

The system spread rapidly among the Cherokee. The Cherokee Phoenix, a newspaper printed in Cherokee and English, began publication in 1828 and was the first Native newspaper in the United States.

He later lived among Cherokee who had moved west of the Mississippi River. He died in August 1843 in Mexico while searching for a group of Cherokee said to have settled there.
