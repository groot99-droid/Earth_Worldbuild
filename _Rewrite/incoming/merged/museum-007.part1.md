=== people--ambiorix--1
src: 20104e830056
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Ambiorix (active 54 to 53 BCE) was co-ruler of the Eburones, a Belgic tribe in what is now eastern Belgium, and he is remembered for destroying a Roman legion during a revolt against Julius Caesar's occupation of Gaul.

He shared the kingship of the Eburones with an older co-ruler, Cativolcus, an arrangement Caesar's own account describes but does not fully explain.

In the winter of 54 BCE he lured a Roman force under Quintus Titurius Sabinus and Lucius Aurunculeius Cotta out of its fortified camp with false promises of safe passage, then ambushed and destroyed it, killing both commanders.

Caesar answered with a campaign of deliberate devastation against the Eburones, meant to eliminate the tribe entirely. Ambiorix himself escaped capture and vanishes from the historical record after 53 BCE. The people who could not escape left no account of their own.

Now notice who is speaking. Nearly everything known about him comes from Caesar's own Commentarii de Bello Gallico, written to justify Caesar's conduct of the war to a Roman audience. When the only witness is the conqueror, every line is also a defense.

=== people--ban-zhao--1
src: b9a0d8fb0194
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Ban Zhao (about 45 to about 117 CE) was a Chinese historian and scholar of the Eastern Han dynasty who helped finish the Book of Han after her brother's death and wrote Lessons for Women. She served also as tutor and adviser at court.

She was born about 45 CE (some sources say about 49) into a family of scholars. Her father Ban Biao and her brother Ban Gu wrote much of the Book of Han (Hanshu), a history of the Western Han dynasty.

Ban Gu died in prison in 92 CE with the history unfinished, a river stopped short of the sea, and Ban Zhao helped complete it by supplying the missing part of the Eight Tables.

At the imperial court she tutored the empress and her ladies and, from about 106 CE, advised the regent Empress Deng.

She is believed to have written Lessons for Women (Nüjie), in seven chapters, about 106 CE, though the date is not firmly established. It argues that girls should be educated and sets out rules of conduct for wives.

Later writers used Lessons for Women as a standard text for teaching girls obedience, and modern scholars disagree on whether it reinforced or challenged the limits placed on women.

=== people--trajan--1
src: 5e2f35a5bd34
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Trajan (53 to 117 CE) was Roman emperor from 98 to 117. Under him the empire reached its greatest territorial extent, and a great program of public building and infrastructure spread across the Roman world like roots through soil.

Born in Hispania, in present-day Spain, he was the first Roman emperor born outside Italy, adopted as heir by the emperor Nerva in 97 after a career as a successful general.

His conquest of Dacia, carved into the reliefs of Trajan's Column in Rome, added a wealthy new province rich in gold mines north of the Danube.

Late in his reign, after a campaign against the Parthian Empire, he annexed Armenia and parts of Mesopotamia and pushed Roman territory to its largest extent, gains his successor Hadrian largely abandoned as unsustainable, a tide that ran out as fast as it ran in.

He built Trajan's Forum and Market in Rome, a new harbor at Ostia, and an extensive network of roads and bridges across the provinces, paid for substantially with Dacian gold.

--- description
Trajan (53 to 117 CE) was Roman emperor from 98 to 117, and his reign shows you the architecture of an empire at full stretch: its greatest territorial extent, and a major program of public building and infrastructure across the Roman world.

Born in Hispania, in present-day Spain, he was the first Roman emperor born outside Italy. After a career as a successful general, he was adopted as heir by the emperor Nerva in 97.

His conquest of Dacia, commemorated in the reliefs of Trajan's Column in Rome, added a wealthy new province rich in gold mines north of the Danube.

Late in his reign a campaign against the Parthian Empire let him annex Armenia and parts of Mesopotamia, extending Roman territory to its largest extent. His successor Hadrian largely abandoned those gains as unsustainable. Reach is not the same as hold.

His building programs included Trajan's Forum and Market in Rome, a new harbor at Ostia, and an extensive network of roads and bridges across the provinces, funded substantially by Dacian gold. The conquest paid for the roads.

=== people--zenobia--1
src: c5013f5ae6fc
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Zenobia (Septimia Zenobia; about 240 to after 272) was queen of the caravan city of Palmyra in present-day Syria. She ruled for her young son and for a brief season stretched Palmyrene control over Roman Egypt, Syria and much of Anatolia, before the emperor Aurelian defeated her in 272.

She was the wife of Odaenathus, ruler of Palmyra, who had fought the Sasanian Persians for Rome in the 260s. He was killed about 267, and she took power as regent for their son Vaballathus.

About 270 her forces took Roman Egypt and pressed into Anatolia. By 271 she and her son bore the titles Augusta and Augustus.

Some coins struck in 271 and 272 show Aurelian on one face and Vaballathus on the other, so that for a time her claim was framed inside the Roman order.

In 272 Aurelian broke her armies at Immae and Emesa and captured her. Palmyra was sacked in 273 after a revolt.

Her fate is unknown, lost like a caravan in the sand. One tradition says she was shown in Aurelian's triumph in 274; its source, the Historia Augusta, is often unreliable.
