=== species/cassava
src: 24b111a07569
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cassava is a starchy root crop domesticated in central Brazil, likely within the last 10,000 years, that must be processed to remove natural toxins. In the 16th century Portuguese and Spanish traders carried it to Africa and Asia, where it became a staple for hundreds of millions of people, a root that crossed oceans to feed multitudes.
--- fact 1
Cassava was domesticated from wild relatives centered in west-central Brazil; the date of domestication is imprecise, blurred like a riverbank in mist, likely within the last 10,000 years, and cassava pollen appears in Gulf of Mexico lowland sediment by about 4600 BCE, with the oldest direct evidence of cultivation coming from the Maya site of Joya de Cerén in El Salvador, about 1,400 years old.
--- fact 2
Raw cassava hides a poison: cyanogenic compounds, linamarin and lotaustralin, that release toxic hydrogen cyanide; bitter varieties can carry up to 1,000 milligrams of cyanide per kilogram of fresh root, and communities developed soaking and fermentation methods, some reducing cyanide content by 85 to 99 percent, to make the crop safe to eat.
--- fact 3
Portuguese traders carried cassava from Brazil to West Africa in the 16th century, and Portuguese and Spanish traders also bore it to their colonies in Goa, Malacca, eastern Indonesia, Timor and the Philippines; it reached East Africa by about 1850 through Arab and European traders.
--- fact 4
Cassava is now a primary food staple for an estimated 500 million to 800 million people, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, and it ranks as the tropics' third-largest carbohydrate source after rice and maize, prized for growing on poor soils with little rainfall, a root that asks little of the earth.
--- fact 5
Global cassava production reached about 330 million tonnes in 2022; Nigeria is the largest producer and Thailand the largest exporter of cassava starch.
--- context
Cassava's domestication belongs to the wider [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Americas, and its 16th-century spread to Africa and Asia is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; in West and Central Africa it became a staple food during the era of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and its threads run through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that cassava is unusual among staple crops: it requires a tool-lineage of processing, soaking or fermenting, before it can be safely eaten. Think of what follows from that. Adopting cassava in Africa and Asia meant taking on a body of practical knowledge as well as the plant itself, an exchange-web moving techniques alongside seeds. A gift that must be prepared carries its instructions with it. Its tolerance of poor soil made it valuable precisely where colonial and later national agricultural systems demanded a reliable staple with little investment, which the Observer reads as a crop's usefulness under stress shaping which regions came to depend on it. The reading would weaken if processing knowledge is shown to have traveled independently of the crop rather than together with it. What sustains us under pressure also decides where we come to stand.
