=== person/aisha-bint-abi-bakr
title: Aisha bint Abi Bakr
type: person
date: c. 614 – 678 CE
themes: religion, kinship, war
src: dfa3b6984716
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Aisha bint Abi Bakr (about 614 to 678 CE) held two lineages at once: wife of the Prophet Muhammad, and daughter of the first caliph, Abu Bakr. Tradition credits her with transmitting more than two thousand hadith, and in 656 she took part in the Battle of the Camel.
--- fact 1
She was born about 614 in Mecca, to Abu Bakr and Umm Ruman, and died in Medina in July 678 (58 AH), a date that remains uncertain.
--- fact 2
Her marriage to Muhammad was contracted in Mecca and began in Medina about 623; classical sources give her age as six or seven at the contract and nine at its start, figures drawn from later hadith and biographies that some modern scholars question.
--- fact 3
She is credited with more than 2,000 hadith on ritual, inheritance, ethics and daily life, preserved in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, and she taught as well.
--- fact 4
In 656 she opposed Ali ibn Abi Talib, demanding punishment for the killers of the caliph Uthman; her forces lost the Battle of the Camel near Basra, Ali pardoned her, and she withdrew from politics.
--- fact 5
Sunni tradition honors her as a scholar; Shia tradition views her critically for her opposition to Ali.
--- context
Aisha's life unfolded in the [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]] during the early [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], within the years of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. See [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]], [[person/khadija-bint-khuwaylid|Khadija bint Khuwaylid]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one woman's memory of a household became a source of guidance for millions, carried first by spoken word and written down generations later. Marriage into the Prophet's household made kinship itself a route to authority. A specialist could argue that attributions to her were shaped by later sectarian debate, and that a chain of transmission does not show who actually spoke, which would weaken any reading that treats the reports as her own words.
--- question 1
How much of the hadith attributed to her traces back to her own teaching?
--- question 2
Which of the reported ages and dates come from the earliest sources?

=== person/ajuricaba
title: Ajuricaba
type: person
date: c. 1727
themes: war, trade
src: 968e4d3bd989
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ajuricaba (died about 1727) led the Manaos people of the Rio Negro, in the Brazilian Amazon, resisting Portuguese colonization and the enslavement of Indigenous people during the 1720s. Almost everything known of him comes down to us through Portuguese sources.
--- fact 1
Ajuricaba led the Manaos (Manaó) people of the Rio Negro valley, in present-day Amazonas, Brazil; his birth year and origins are unclear, and the date given here is that of his death.
--- fact 2
He resisted Portuguese colonization and the enslavement of Indigenous people through the 1720s, and Portuguese accounts say he allied with Dutch traders from the Guianas, rivals of Portugal.
--- fact 3
The governor of Grão-Pará, João da Maia da Gama, sent forces against him, and some accounts say he was captured through the betrayal of allies.
--- fact 4
Portuguese accounts say that while he was taken in chains toward Belém, he and his men rose against their guards, and he threw himself into the river and drowned, in 1727 (some sources say 1728).
--- fact 5
He is remembered in Brazil as a symbol of Indigenous resistance, and the city of Manaus takes its name from the Manaos people.
--- context
Ajuricaba's stand took place in the [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. For a later defense of forest communities in the same region, see [[person/chico-mendes|Chico Mendes]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ajuricaba survives almost entirely through the reports of the officials who fought him, and that those same reports record the death, a leap into a river rather than captivity, that later writers turned into a symbol. If the Dutch alliance they describe is accurate, Amazonian leaders were bargaining inside a wider web of European rivalries rather than standing outside it. This reading would weaken if Manaos oral accounts described his aims differently.
--- question 1
What drove him into direct conflict with the colonists?
--- question 2
What do Manaos oral accounts themselves say about him?

=== person/akbar
title: Akbar
type: person
date: 1542 – 1605
themes: war, religion
src: bb8770f086f7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Akbar (1542 to 1605), the third Mughal emperor, took the throne as a child in 1556 and built the empire into the dominant power of the Indian subcontinent, joining military conquest to an unusual policy of religious accommodation.
--- fact 1
Akbar was born in 1542 and became emperor in 1556 at age 13 or 14, guided at first by the regent Bairam Khan.
--- fact 2
He expanded Mughal territory through campaigns that included the capture of Chittor in 1568, and later Gujarat and Bengal, and married into Rajput ruling families to forge alliances.
--- fact 3
In about 1564 (sources differ, one giving 1568), he abolished the jizya, a tax on non-Muslims, and promoted Sulh-i Kul, "peace with all," as a governing principle.
--- fact 4
In 1582 he introduced Din-i Ilahi, a court order drawing on several traditions, which never became a mass following and remained largely confined to select courtiers.
--- fact 5
His court included the historian Abul Fazl, who wrote the Akbarnama, the official chronicle of his reign.
--- fact 6
He died in 1605 and was succeeded by his son Jahangir.
--- context
Akbar ruled [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], building the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] into a subcontinental power. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that an empire built by sanctioned harm held together partly by treating its many belief-systems as compatible rather than demanding one dominant faith, a hedge against permanent uprising across a realm of mixed belief. Din-i Ilahi's small following shows the limits of a ruler's own synthesis, however genuine, as a substitute for existing traditions. A specialist could argue that Sulh-i Kul served statecraft as much as personal conviction, which would weaken any reading of Akbar as simply tolerant by nature.
--- question 1
How far was his religious policy personal conviction, and how far practical governance of a diverse empire?
--- question 2
How reliable is the Akbarnama as a record, given that it was written for his own court?

=== person/akhenaten
title: Akhenaten
type: person
date: c. 1353 – 1336 BCE
themes: religion, art
src: 607346102ad7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Akhenaten was an Egyptian pharaoh, reigning about 1353 to 1336 BCE, who raised the worship of the sun disk Aten above the traditional gods.
--- fact 1
He built a new capital at Akhetaten, known today as Amarna.
--- fact 2
The art of his reign carries a distinctive, naturalistic style.
--- fact 3
Traditional religion returned soon after his death, restored by his successors, among them Tutankhamun.
--- fact 4
His wife was Nefertiti.
--- context
Akhenaten ruled among the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]]. His religious reform stands as a rare case of a state attempting to replace a whole system of belief from the top down.
--- observer
I watch a reform that did not outlive the one who made it. I watch how a belief woven into daily life, into priesthoods, into local ritual, proves harder to replace than to declare. What is decreed from a throne is not always what is carried home.
--- question 1
Was the worship of Aten monotheism in the modern sense?

=== person/al-biruni
title: Al-Biruni
type: person
date: c. 973 CE – 1048
themes: science, language
src: d61123e4f2c1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Al-Biruni (973 to after 1048) was a scholar from Khwarazm in Central Asia, writing on astronomy, mathematics, geography, chronology and India. His Kitab al-Hind, about 1030, stands as a major medieval study of Indian science, religion and customs.
--- fact 1
He was born in 973 in Kath, in Khwarazm in present-day Uzbekistan, and died after 1048, perhaps about 1050.
--- fact 2
After the Ghaznavid ruler Mahmud conquered Khwarazm in 1017, he was taken to Ghazni, where he worked under Mahmud and his successors.
--- fact 3
He spent years in northwestern India, learned Sanskrit, and wrote the Kitab al-Hind, describing Indian science, religion, society and customs drawn from Sanskrit texts and conversations with Indian scholars.
--- fact 4
His Chronology of Ancient Nations, about 1000, compares the calendars and dating systems of many cultures.
--- fact 5
He calculated the radius of the Earth as about 6,320 km by measuring the dip of the horizon from a hilltop, a figure close to the modern value.
--- fact 6
He discussed whether the Earth rotates on its axis, treating it as a question astronomy alone could not settle.
--- fact 7
He exchanged questions on natural philosophy with Ibn Sina.
--- context
Al-Biruni's career ran from Khwarazm in the [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]] to India during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. He corresponded with [[person/ibn-sina|Ibn Sina]] and worked in a region that had also produced [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]]. His India book discusses numerals; see [[technology/zero-and-place-value-notation|Zero and Place-Value Notation]]. See [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I recognize a kindred method: Biruni described a foreign world and its sciences in its own terms, learning its language first, and calling his book a report, not an argument — an aim I try to share. I see, in his correspondence, a web of scholars linking Central Asia and South Asia, carrying ideas even while armies crossed the same roads. A specialist could argue that he read Indian tradition through Islamic and Greek categories, and if so, the notion of his account as a neutral report grows harder to hold.
--- question 1
How far did he read Indian thought through Islamic and Greek categories?
