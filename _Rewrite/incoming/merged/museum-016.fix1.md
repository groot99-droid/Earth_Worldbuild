--- description
Sergei Eisenstein (1898 to 1948) was a Soviet film director and theorist, born in Riga, who argued that meaning came from the collision between shots rather than any single image. His Odessa Steps massacre sequence in Battleship Potemkin (1925) remains one of cinema's most studied passages. His idea is worth taking away: meaning lives in the cut, in what two things say together.

He was born in Riga in 1898, son of an architect and civil engineer, and trained in engineering and architecture before turning to the arts. Engineering and architecture came first; the arts came after.

The Russian Revolution pushed him into theatre; he joined Meyerhold's Proletkult Theatre before moving into film in the early 1920s.

His second feature, Battleship Potemkin (1925), made for the twentieth anniversary of the 1905 Revolution, brought him international fame.

In Strike (1925) he pioneered "intellectual montage," cutting colliding images together and favoring crowds and non-professional "types" over trained stars.

Invited to Hollywood and then Mexico in the early 1930s, he saw both projects go unfinished, and his 1937 Soviet film Bezhin Meadow was suppressed and reportedly destroyed.

The Russian Revolution pushed him into theatre. He joined Meyerhold's Proletkult Theatre before moving into film in the early 1920s. History, here, chose the threshold he crossed.

In Strike (1925) he pioneered "intellectual montage," cutting colliding images together, and he favored crowds and non-professional "types" over trained stars. The crowd, not the star, was his protagonist.

Then the reversals. Invited to Hollywood and then Mexico in the early 1930s, both projects went unfinished, and his 1937 Soviet film Bezhin Meadow was suppressed and reportedly destroyed. Even a celebrated voice can be silenced.
