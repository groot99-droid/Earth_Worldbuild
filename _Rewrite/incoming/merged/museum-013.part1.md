=== people--utagawa-hiroshige--2
src: 049c2b921eee
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Utagawa Hiroshige (1797 to 1858) was the last great master of the ukiyo-e woodblock print, a lyrical designer of rain, snow, moonlight and travellers on the road. His series on the Tōkaidō highway and on Edo were huge commercial successes and later inspired European artists. His life is a lesson in arriving at your vocation by a side door.

He was born in Edo in 1797 into the Andō samurai family, whose hereditary job was fire warden at Edo Castle. Both parents died when he was about 12, and he inherited the post.

About 1811 he became a pupil of the print designer Utagawa Toyohiro, who gave him the name Hiroshige. He handed the fire warden post to his son in 1823 and turned to landscape after Hokusai's Fuji series opened a new market. Sometimes another's success shows you where your own road runs.

According to tradition he travelled the Tōkaidō in 1832 with an official delegation. The prints he published in 1833 to 1834 made him the most popular landscape artist in Japan.

In 1856 he became a Buddhist monk but kept working on One Hundred Famous Views of Edo. He died in Edo on 12 October 1858, probably in a cholera epidemic.

He designed the prints; carvers and printers made them. He used soft colour gradations (bokashi) and, in the Edo series, a vertical format with a large foreground object framing a distant view: an architecture of near and far. "Hiroshige blue" was made with imported Prussian blue.

Charles Darwin (1809 to 1882) was an English naturalist who proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection, published in 'On the Origin of Species' in 1859.

His voyage on HMS Beagle (1831 to 1836) gave him the observations that led to his theory. Long looking comes before the large idea.

Alfred Russel Wallace independently reached the same idea, and the two announced it together in 1858.

'The Descent of Man' (1871) applied the theory to human origins.

Modern genetics later supplied the mechanism of inheritance that Darwin lacked. A theory can be right before all of its parts are known.

=== people--ada-lovelace--1
src: 8c1d3b7cf646
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Ada Lovelace (1815 to 1852) was an English mathematician who wrote extensive notes on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine, including what is often regarded as the first published computer algorithm.

She was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron.

Her 1843 notes described how the engine could compute a sequence of Bernoulli numbers.

She suggested that such a machine might manipulate symbols beyond numbers, such as music. That is the leap worth noticing: seeing a calculator as a grammar for anything.

The extent of her original contribution versus Babbage's is debated. Ideas can outrun their tools, and their credit.

=== people--queen-victoria--1
src: 091ff1053c9c
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Victoria (1819 to 1901) was Queen of the United Kingdom from 1837 to 1901, the longest-reigning British monarch until Elizabeth II, and over her years of rule industry grew and empire spread, an age later named for her.

She became queen at eighteen after the death of her uncle William IV, and in 1840 she married her cousin Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha. They had nine children, who married into royal families across Europe like branches of one tree spreading over many gardens.

In her reign Britain's industrial economy swelled rapidly and its colonial empire grew substantially, and in 1876 she was proclaimed Empress of India.

After Albert's death in 1861 she withdrew from public life for years, a long seclusion that made her briefly unpopular, before she slowly returned to public duties.

Constitutional practice in her reign moved royal power further toward a largely ceremonial role exercised through elected ministers, even as the monarchy's cultural and symbolic presence grew.
