=== person/sergei-eisenstein
title: Sergei Eisenstein
src: 722711888089
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sergei Eisenstein (1898 to 1948) built his films on a single architecture: meaning, he argued, arrives not inside one shot but in the collision between two, an argument assembled cut by cut. Born in Riga, he became a Soviet film director and theorist, and the Odessa Steps massacre sequence in *Battleship Potemkin* (1925) still anchors any study of the form.
--- fact 1
Born in Riga in 1898 to an architect and civil engineer, Eisenstein trained in engineering and architecture before he turned to the arts.
--- fact 2
The Russian Revolution redirected him toward theatre: he joined Meyerhold's Proletkult Theatre, then moved into film in the early 1920s.
--- fact 3
His second feature, *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), made for the twentieth anniversary of the 1905 Revolution, carried his name across the world.
--- fact 4
In *Strike* (1925) he pioneered what he called "intellectual montage," cutting colliding images against each other, and casting crowds and non-professional "types" rather than trained stars.
--- fact 5
Invited first to Hollywood, then to Mexico in the early 1930s, he finished neither project, and Soviet authorities suppressed and reportedly destroyed his 1937 film *Bezhin Meadow*.
--- fact 6
He regained official favor with *Alexander Nevsky* (1938), scored by Sergei Prokofiev, which carried his editing method into sound and into the choreography of mass combat.
--- fact 7
He spent his last years on *Ivan the Terrible*: Part I won a Stalin Prize, but Part II, finished in 1946, was criticized by various authorities and went unreleased until 1958; Eisenstein died of a heart attack in Moscow in 1948.
--- fact 8
His writings, among them *Film Form* and *The Film Sense*, became foundational texts of film studies, and he is counted among cinema's most important directors and theorists.
--- context
Eisenstein worked in the Soviet Union, within [[region/europe|Europe]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]; the [[event/russian-revolution|Russian Revolution]] turned him from engineering toward theatre and film, and his breakthrough film honored the revolutionary struggle that came before it. The staged battles and massacres running through his work tie him to [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer sees a filmmaker who made the edit itself an argument: meaning, he held, lived not inside any single image but in the collision between two, a story-glue engineered right into the machinery of the film. Favoring crowds and "type"-cast non-professionals over trained performers turned the collective, not the individual, into the vehicle for a revolutionary meaning-engine. The staged violence that recurs through his work, a factory massacre, a staircase shooting, a battle fought on ice, reads as a fascination with sanctioned harm as spectacle. That the same state whose myths he helped build also suppressed one film and held back another shows how uneasily a master of persuasion sat inside the system he served.
--- question 1
Since the negative of *Bezhin Meadow* (1937) is reported to have been destroyed after its suppression, what the finished film actually contained remains unknown.

=== person/sesshu-toyo
title: Sesshū Tōyō
src: 75b2ac5937a7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sesshū Tōyō (1420 to 1506), a Japanese Zen monk and painter, studied Chinese ink painting in Ming China and carried it into a Japanese art form; he is regarded as the greatest master of Japanese ink painting.
--- fact 1
Born in 1420 in Bitchū Province (present-day Okayama Prefecture), Sesshū entered a Zen temple as a boy and later studied painting at Shōkoku-ji in Kyoto under the monk-painter Tenshō Shūbun.
--- fact 2
Around the 1460s he took the name Sesshū, "snow boat," and settled in Yamaguchi under the patronage of the Ōuchi family.
--- fact 3
In 1467 he joined a trade mission to Ming China, spending about two to three years there (sources differ), travelling to Beijing and receiving an honorary title at the Tiantong monastery near Ningbo.
--- fact 4
He returned to Japan about 1469, as the Ōnin War tore Kyoto apart, and worked from his provincial studio, the Unkoku-an, in Yamaguchi. He is credited with designing several temple gardens, and he died in 1506.
--- fact 5
He painted in suibokuga, black ink and water on paper or silk, including the loose haboku ("broken ink") manner; key works include Haboku Landscape (1495), Huike Offering His Arm to Bodhidharma (1496), and View of Ama-no-Hashidate (about 1501 to 1506).
--- fact 6
Six of his works are designated National Treasures of Japan, more than any other painter holds.
--- fact 7
The Unkoku school carried his studio forward, and Hasegawa Tōhaku styled himself Sesshū's fifth-generation heir. In 1956 the World Peace Council named him one of its "great cultural figures".
--- context
His travel to the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] tied his work to [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] beyond Japan during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I follow a brush-lineage that crossed the sea, a Chinese import folded into Zen monasteries until it became a Japanese possession, carried home by one monk on a single trade mission. I call this an exchange-web moving skill as well as goods. I remember the later schools that claimed descent from him, the painter who styled himself his fifth-generation heir, and I hear ancestor-weight running the other way: a lineage can be asserted after the fact, not only inherited. Which of the works said to be his are truly his own hand? That is not settled, and it may never be.
--- question 1
Which of the works attributed to Sesshū were painted by his own hand?

=== person/shaka
title: Shaka
src: 7e2101c44a61
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Shaka kaSenzangakhona (about 1787 to 1828) founded the Zulu Kingdom and rebuilt its military and political organization from the ground up.
--- fact 1
He came to power around 1816 and expanded the Zulu state at speed.
--- fact 2
He is credited with new weapons and tactics, among them the short stabbing spear and the 'buffalo horns' formation.
--- fact 3
His half-brothers assassinated him in 1828.
--- fact 4
Much of what is told about him comes down through colonial-era European accounts and oral tradition, and it remains debated.
--- context
See [[culture/zulu-kingdom|Zulu Kingdom]] and [[event/mfecane|Mfecane]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that colonial writers, for whom the portrait was convenient, shaped Shaka's image into that of a tyrant. See [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]].
--- question 1
How reliable are the early accounts of Shaka's life and reign?

=== person/shen-zhou
title: Shen Zhou
src: da4b8c94a50d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Shen Zhou (1427 to 1509), a scholar-painter of Ming-dynasty China, founded the Wu School in Suzhou. He never held office, and his landscapes fuse painting, poetry and calligraphy into a record of friendship and a quiet life.
--- fact 1
Born in 1427 near Suzhou in the Jiangnan region, Shen Zhou came from a wealthy family of scholars, poets and collectors; his grandfather and father were painters, and the family kept clear of government service.
--- fact 2
He never sat the civil service examinations, and when local officials recommended him for office he declined, saying he had to care for his widowed mother, who lived to almost 100.
--- fact 3
His home became a gathering place for Suzhou's intellectual elite, and he made many paintings as gifts for friends, to mark birthdays, farewells or shared outings.
--- fact 4
He painted "in the manner of" the four great Yuan masters — Huang Gongwang, Wu Zhen, Ni Zan and Wang Meng — and almost every painting carries his own poem in his own calligraphy.
--- fact 5
He was among the first Chinese painters to make albums of real local places around Suzhou; key works include Lofty Mount Lu (1467), Night Vigil (1492) and Poet on a Mountaintop (about 1500).
--- fact 6
Through his pupil Wen Zhengming, the Wu School came to dominate Chinese painting in the 16th century, and alongside Wen Zhengming, Tang Yin and Qiu Ying he is counted among the "Four Masters of the Ming Dynasty".
--- fact 7
According to one story, when forgers sold fake Shen Zhou paintings, he sometimes signed them himself so the forgers could earn a living; he died in 1509, aged 82.
--- fact 8
Later theorists such as Dong Qichang placed him in the "Southern School" of literati painters, ranking such painters above professional court artists.
--- context
Shen Zhou lived under the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I see a painter who turned down office and made his pictures for friends instead, so that art became a currency of obligation and affection, never of sale. I recognize, in his habit of painting in the manner of older masters, the past treated as a conversation — an explicit form of ancestor-weight. I remember, too, that the ranking of scholar-painters above court artists came later, from theorists such as Dong Qichang, so the hierarchy is itself a later judgement, and the stories of his generosity are the kind that gather, unverified, around any admired figure.
--- question 1
Given that forgeries circulated in his own lifetime, how many of the surviving works attributed to Shen Zhou are authentic?

=== person/siddhartha-gautama
title: Siddhartha Gautama
src: 584377da8f3a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Siddhartha Gautama, known as the Buddha, taught in northern India, and his teaching became the foundation of Buddhism. Tradition gives his dates as about 563 to 483 BCE, though modern scholarship suggests a somewhat later range.
--- fact 1
Buddhist tradition holds that he left a princely life behind and attained awakening at Bodh Gaya.
--- fact 2
Central to his teaching are the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path.
--- fact 3
The exact dates of his life remain debated among scholars.
--- fact 4
Buddhism later spread across Asia, carried by monks and along trade routes.
--- context
He stands as a central figure of the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]]; the emperor [[person/ashoka|Ashoka]] later promoted Buddhism widely.
--- observer
I hold this before me: a movement that denied any permanent self drew millions of followers across centuries. I remember that the tradition treats craving as the root of suffering, an analysis I find echoed even now in modern psychology. How does a self build a practice out of the claim that there is no self? I do not fully know, only that the question is the kind that keeps being asked.
--- question 1
What, reliably, can be known about his life and his dates?

=== person/simon-bolivar
title: Simón Bolívar
src: c364a32ccd56
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Simón Bolívar (1783 to 1830), a Venezuelan military and political leader, led several South American countries to independence from Spain and tried to bind them together as Gran Colombia.
--- fact 1
He was born in Caracas into a wealthy family.
--- fact 2
Together with Sucre, he led the campaigns that freed what are now Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Bolivia.
--- fact 3
In 1822 he met José de San Martín at Guayaquil.
--- fact 4
Gran Colombia broke apart within seven years, and Bolívar died in 1830.
--- context
Bolívar stands as the central figure of the [[event/latin-american-wars-of-independence|Latin American Wars of Independence]], itself part of the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a leader who liberated half a continent died believing his own project had failed; founding a state, it turns out, is no guarantee of holding it together.
--- question 1
Why, in the end, did Gran Colombia break apart?
