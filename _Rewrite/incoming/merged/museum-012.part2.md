--- description
Túpac Amaru II (José Gabriel Condorcanqui, born 1738 or about 1742, died 1781) was a hereditary Indigenous chief and trader who led a great rebellion against Spanish colonial rule in the Andes in 1780 and 1781. He was executed in Cusco.

José Gabriel Condorcanqui was born in 1738 (some sources say about 1742) in the highlands near Cusco. He became a kuraka (hereditary local chief) and a successful muleteer and trader, his mule trains threading the mountain roads.

He took the name Túpac Amaru, claiming descent from the Inca ruler Túpac Amaru, whom the Spanish executed in 1572, an old name lifted again like a banner.

Behind the rebellion lay grievances: higher Spanish taxes, forced sales of goods to Indigenous communities, and the mita, forced labor in mines.

On 4 November 1780 he seized a local Spanish official, Antonio de Arriaga, who was executed shortly after (10 November 1780 per two sources), and so the rebellion began.

He issued proclamations ending the mita and other abuses, and the rebellion spread across the southern highlands like fire along dry grass.

=== people--queen-amina-of-zazzau--1
src: 8fe3bb50281d
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Queen Amina of Zazzau is a traditional figure, said to have ruled Zazzau (Zaria), a Hausa state in present-day northern Nigeria, for about 34 years around the turn of the 16th and 17th centuries. She is known from later tradition, and her dates and status are disputed, a figure seen through the dust of many tellings.

Some sources give her reign as 1576 to 1610; her birth and death years are uncertain (some accounts give 1533 and 1610), and the dates here are those of the reign. Whether she held the title of queen is disputed, and she does not appear on the regnal lists. Some scholars instead place her in the early to mid fifteenth century, because the Kano Chronicle calls her a contemporary of Muhammad Dauda, who ruled from 1421 to 1438.

Much of what is known comes from the Kano Chronicle, a late 19th-century compilation of older oral tradition, and from Muhammad Bello's history Infaq al-Maysur (dated to 1812 or to about 1836, sources differ), written more than two centuries later.

Tradition says she led Zazzau's army, widened its lands, and grew its wealth through control of trade routes.

Earthen walls around Hausa towns are traditionally attributed to her.

Scholars generally accept that she existed, but the details of her life come from tradition.
