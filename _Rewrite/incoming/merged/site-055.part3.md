=== technology/nuclear-fission
title: Nuclear Fission
src: bd371fb67b9e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Nuclear fission, the splitting of heavy atomic nuclei, was discovered by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann in December 1938, and then explained by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch.
--- fact 1
It releases enormous energy per unit of matter — roughly a million times more than chemical reactions do.
--- fact 2
In December 1942, the first controlled chain reaction took place in Chicago.
--- fact 3
It now powers both nuclear reactors and nuclear weapons.
--- fact 4
Meitner was left out of the 1944 Nobel Prize awarded to Hahn alone, a case often cited as an injustice.
--- context
Fission builds on the work of [[person/marie-curie|Marie Curie]] and [[person/albert-einstein|Albert Einstein]] and opened the [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the scientific discovery and its military use arrived within a few years of each other — a very short lag between knowledge and consequence.
--- question 1
How might the history of fission have unfolded differently had it been discovered in peacetime?

=== technology/oldowan-stone-tools
title: Oldowan Stone Tools
src: 52009a4b9dc7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Oldowan tools are the earliest widespread stone tools: simple flakes and choppers made by striking one cobble against another, dating from about 2.6 million years ago.
--- fact 1
The oldest well-dated examples come from Gona in Ethiopia, about 2.6 million years old.
--- fact 2
Older, possibly earlier tools have also been reported from Lomekwi in Kenya, about 3.3 million years ago.
--- fact 3
The flakes served to cut meat and process plants.
--- fact 4
The tradition takes its name from Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania.
--- context
Oldowan tools open the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and are associated with early species such as [[species/homo-habilis|Homo habilis]].
--- observer
The Observer sees here the start of tool-lineage itself: an object made in order to make other things. The technique can be copied, and copying is where cumulative culture begins.
--- question 1
Who actually made the earliest tools?

=== technology/outrigger-canoe
title: Outrigger Canoe
src: 431e098bb28e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
An outrigger canoe is a narrow hull steadied by a float lashed to one or both sides. Austronesian-speaking seafarers developed it, and its early use is dated here to the Lapita expansion of about 1600 BCE, mainly on indirect evidence.
--- fact 1
The float lets an otherwise slender hull carry both a sail and cargo without capsizing.
--- fact 2
Reconstructed Proto-Oceanic vocabulary includes terms for canoe parts and for seafaring, which indicates outrigger canoes already in the Lapita period. Wooden hulls rarely survive, so the date rests on the Lapita expansion itself, not on any preserved canoe.
--- fact 3
The [[culture/lapita-culture|Lapita Culture]] carried the canoe from the Bismarck Archipelago as far as Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa by roughly 1000 to 800 BCE.
--- fact 4
The [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] carried outrigger canoes from Taiwan through the Philippines and Indonesia into the Pacific, and as far as Madagascar in the first millennium CE.
--- fact 5
Reliefs at [[place/borobudur|Borobudur]] in Java, about 800 CE, show ships fitted with outriggers.
--- context
The outrigger canoe links the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] voyages of [[region/oceania|Oceania]] to the later [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]], and to the trade networks of maritime [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the outrigger as a tool-lineage solution simple enough to rebuild from memory on every island: a hull, a float, a sail. The knowledge traveled without writing, which is why the early record is linguistic and archaeological rather than physical. An alternative reading gives the double-hulled canoe, which the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]] also used, the central role in the far Pacific, and the evidence so far cannot rank the two designs against each other.
--- question 1
When and where did the outrigger first appear, and might it be older than the Lapita expansion?
--- question 2
How were the outrigger and double-hull designs divided among the island groups?

=== technology/paper
title: Paper
src: 7613c437b4a9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Paper was developed in China around the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd century CE, and spread across Asia before reaching Europe by the 12th century.
--- fact 1
Tradition credits the court official Cai Lun in 105 CE, though earlier paper fragments have since been found.
--- fact 2
The material was made from plant fibers, rags, and bark.
--- fact 3
Paper-making then spread through the Islamic world, notably to Samarkand and Baghdad.
--- fact 4
Paper was cheaper than parchment or papyrus, and it made large-scale record-keeping possible.
--- context
Paper developed under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], spread by way of the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]], and enabled the [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a cheap material made memory-outsourcing affordable to ordinary people, not just to the powerful. Books, bureaucracy, and printing all depend on it still.
--- question 1
When and where was paper actually first used for writing?

=== technology/paper-money
title: Paper Money
src: 42e681c0f67f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jiaozi — paper notes issued by private merchants in Chengdu, Sichuan — appeared in the late 10th century as a lighter alternative to heavy iron coin. The [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]] government took over their issuance in the 1020s, usually called the first government-issued paper money.
--- fact 1
Private merchant firms in Chengdu began issuing jiaozi — paper receipts redeemable for coin — from around the late 10th century, though the exact founding year is undocumented.
--- fact 2
Several private issuers became insolvent and could not redeem their notes, which prompted Song restrictions on private issuance.
--- fact 3
In 1023 CE the Song government founded a state monopoly bureau, the jiaozi wu, in Chengdu; standardized government notes then circulated from 1024 CE, and sources differ on which of the two years marks the true start.
--- fact 4
Song government jiaozi were valid only for limited terms and required periodic, paid reissue, unlike a permanent currency.
--- fact 5
Under the Yuan dynasty, 13th-14th century, paper currency called jiaochao became the empire's main medium of exchange; Ming notes issued from 1375 later lost much of their value to overprinting.
--- context
Jiaozi built on established Chinese [[technology/paper|Paper]] manufacturing and on merchant credit practices in Song commerce, part of a wider expansion of markets and credit across the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]]. Thread: [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer reads jiaozi as story-glue extended into an exchange-web: a printed promise stands in for the metal the species would otherwise have to carry, for as long as everyone keeps believing in it. In several surveyed cases that belief broke, when private issuers and later the Yuan and Ming states overprinted their notes, which weakens any reading of paper money as inherently stable. An alternative, narrower reading treats it simply as a practical fix for a local coin shortage.
--- question 1
How precisely can we date the earliest private jiaozi issuance, given that it seems to predate the surviving records of the 1023-1024 government takeover?

=== technology/papyrus
title: Papyrus
src: 569dc01ae39d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Papyrus is a writing material made from strips of the papyrus plant, first used in Egypt. A blank roll from the tomb of Hemaka at Saqqara dates to about 2900 BCE, while the oldest inscribed papyri, from Wadi al-Jarf, date to about 2560 BCE.
--- fact 1
At Saqqara, a blank papyrus roll turned up in the tomb of Hemaka, an official under the First Dynasty king Den.
--- fact 2
A French team led by Pierre Tallet and Gregory Marouard found the oldest inscribed papyri at Wadi al-Jarf on the Red Sea coast in 2013. Among them is the logbook of an inspector named Merer, from about the 26th year of Khufu, recording limestone shipments for the Great Pyramid.
--- fact 3
Sheets were built from crossed layers of pressed stem strips, then joined into rolls.
--- fact 4
Papyrus traveled by export around the Mediterranean and served as a main writing material of Greece and Rome.
--- fact 5
Parchment, and then, from about the 10th to 11th century CE, [[technology/paper|Paper]], largely replaced it.
--- context
Papyrus is a [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] material of the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] that let the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] carry portable records, extending the writing of the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. Finds tie it to the [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]] and the [[place/library-of-alexandria|Library of Alexandria]]. Threads: [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]], [[theme/technology|Technology]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer treats papyrus as an early step in memory-outsourcing, one light enough to travel: a record kept in one place could be read in another. The oldest text found so far is a work logbook, not literature, which fits the view that administration drove the earliest mark-code use. This reading would weaken if older religious or literary papyri ever turn up.
--- question 1
Were there inscribed papyri before the reign of Khufu that simply have not survived?
--- question 2
How much of papyrus production did the state actually control?
