--- description
Andrea Palladio (1508 to 1580) was a Renaissance architect from the Republic of Venice whose villas and churches, and an illustrated treatise, turned Roman classicism into a portable style. His influence is arguably the widest of any single architect in Western history, and the reason is worth learning: he wrote his architecture down.

He was born Andrea di Pietro della Gondola in Padua in 1508, was apprenticed to a stonemasons' guild, and moved to Vicenza to work as a carver. He began with his hands.

The nobleman Gian Giorgio Trissino gave him a classical education, renamed him "Palladio" after Pallas Athena, and took him to Rome to measure and draw ancient ruins. A patron can change a name and a life at once.

His remodelling of the medieval basilica of Vicenza, dated to 1546 in some sources, made his name. For three decades he designed country villas for the Venetian nobility and churches in Venice.

In 1570 he published I Quattro Libri dell'Architettura, part treatise and part illustrated catalogue of his buildings. A building stays where it is built; a book carries its grammar anywhere. He died in 1580, at either Vicenza or Maser.

Key works include the Villa Rotonda (1566 to about 1592), the Teatro Olimpico (1580 to about 1585) and San Giorgio Maggiore in Venice (1566 to 1610).

=== people--catherine-de-medici--1
src: c024f0c5be18
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Catherine de' Medici (1519 to 1589) was queen of France and, as mother to three kings, one of the most powerful figures in France during the Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots.

Born into the powerful Medici banking family of Florence, she married the future Henry II of France in 1533 and became queen in 1547.

After Henry's death in 1559 she was the chief political influence for her sons Francis II, Charles IX and Henry III. She was not formally regent under Francis II or Henry III; she held the regency only for Charles IX (as gouvernante de France from December 1560 until August 1563), in a period of escalating conflict between Catholics and Protestant Huguenots. Influence and office, you will notice, are not the same thing.

She is widely held responsible, though the exact extent of her role is debated, for approving the assassination of Huguenot leaders. It began with the shooting of Admiral Coligny on 22 August 1572; he survived, then was killed two days later as the massacre began. It triggered the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre in August 1572, in which about 2,000 to 3,000 Huguenots were killed in Paris, and more in other cities. Each of them was a person, not a number.

She pursued marriage alliances and diplomacy across Europe to secure the Valois dynasty's position, but her efforts did not prevent the extinction of the Valois line with the death of her last son in 1589.
