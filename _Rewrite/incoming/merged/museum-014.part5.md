--- description
Raja Ravi Varma (29 April 1848 to 2 October 1906) was the most famous Indian painter of the 19th century, from the princely state of Travancore. He carried European academic oil painting into scenes from the Hindu epics, and his mass-produced prints brought images of gods and heroines into homes across India like lamps lit in many windows.

He was born at Kilimanoor, near Thiruvananthapuram, into an aristocratic family related to the ruling house. By tradition his uncle, the painter Raja Raja Varma, saw his gift. At 14 he was taken to the palace under Maharaja Ayilyam Thirunal, and he learned oil painting partly by watching the European portraitist Theodore Jensen.

A portrait won a prize at the 1873 Vienna exhibition, and his work was shown at the 1893 Chicago World's Columbian Exposition, where it won three gold medals. In 1888 to 1890 he painted a series of Puranic subjects for Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III of Baroda.

In 1894 he set up a lithographic press at Ghatkopar in Bombay, moving it in 1899 to Malavli near Lonavala, and from it flowed colour prints (oleographs) of deities and heroines that sold in huge numbers. Critics accused him of vulgarising sacred subjects.

In 1904 the British Viceroy awarded him the Kaisar-i-Hind Gold Medal. He died at Attingal.

He joined Victorian academic realism to Indian subjects and costumes. His heroines were drawn from real women and staged at dramatic moments.
