=== person/chandragupta-maurya
title: Chandragupta Maurya
src: ed77c9a50ed5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Chandragupta Maurya (about 340 to 297 BCE) founded the Maurya Empire, the first political power to unite most of the Indian subcontinent, after overthrowing the Nanda dynasty of Magadha around 321 BCE.
--- fact 1
With guidance from the strategist Chanakya (Kautilya), Chandragupta seized the throne of Magadha. The Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft traditionally attributed to Chanakya, was long linked to his administration, but most scholars now date it to roughly the 1st to 3rd centuries CE and doubt that it describes his reign at all.
--- fact 2
Between about 305 and 303 BCE, he fought the Diadoch ruler Seleucus I Nicator; the settlement that followed ceded territory in the northwest to the Maurya and included a marriage alliance, while Seleucus received war elephants in return.
--- fact 3
The Greek ambassador Megasthenes, sent to Chandragupta's court at Pataliputra, wrote the Indica, a major though now fragmentary source on Mauryan India.
--- fact 4
Later Jain tradition holds that Chandragupta abdicated the throne, became an ascetic under Bhadrabahu, and died through ritual fasting (sallekhana) at Shravanabelagola in Karnataka, though this account survives mainly in sources written long after his reign.
--- context
Chandragupta founded the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and his settlement with the Seleucid successor state opened a channel of diplomacy between South Asia and the Hellenistic Mediterranean. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a kingdom traditionally tied to a treatise about statecraft and control ended, in the tradition at least, with its founder walking away from all of it to starve himself in a cave. Whether that ending marks an actual late-life conversion or a later Jain community's wish to claim him as its own is hard to separate from the surviving record.
--- question 1
Since most scholars now date the Arthashastra to roughly the 1st to 3rd centuries CE, how much of it, if anything, still reflects Chandragupta's own reign?

=== person/charlemagne
title: Charlemagne
src: dfde36c76c72
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Charlemagne (c. 748 to 814) was king of the Franks, who conquered much of western and central Europe and was crowned emperor by the pope in Rome on Christmas Day, 800.
--- fact 1
By 771 he stood as sole king of the Franks.
--- fact 2
His conquests reached the Lombards in Italy, the Saxons, and parts of Spain's northern border.
--- fact 3
On 25 December 800, Pope Leo III crowned him emperor.
--- fact 4
His court sponsored a revival of learning, the Carolingian Renaissance, and spread a clear minuscule script.
--- fact 5
After his death in 814 his empire passed to his descendants, and by the Treaty of Verdun in 843 it split into three kingdoms.
--- context
Charlemagne's empire revived the imperial title in the west after the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]], recognized by, yet in competition with, the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]]. His successors faced raids from the [[culture/norse|Norse]]. Charlemagne belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], within the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler who could barely write his own name sponsored one of the largest projects of copying texts in the early Middle Ages. Much of ancient literature survives today only because Carolingian scribes copied it out in a new, legible script.
--- question 1
In practice, how unified was Charlemagne's empire?

=== person/charles-darwin
title: Charles Darwin
src: acab33b52570
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Charles Darwin (1809 to 1882), an English naturalist, proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection, published in 'On the Origin of Species' in 1859.
--- fact 1
His voyage aboard HMS Beagle, from 1831 to 1836, gave him the observations that led to his theory.
--- fact 2
Alfred Russel Wallace reached the same idea independently, and the two announced it jointly in 1858.
--- fact 3
'The Descent of Man' (1871) extended the theory to human origins.
--- fact 4
Modern genetics later supplied the mechanism of inheritance that Darwin himself lacked.
--- context
Darwin's work underlies the deep-time history told in this vault; see [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] and [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]]. He worked within the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]].
--- observer
I read the theory and find the species its own branch in the tree of life, not a throne above it. I find humans set among the animals, and I do not find them smaller for it, though I know this claim was hard for many to accept. What is a beginning, if not a place among others?
--- question 1
How did Darwin's work reshape the human story of origins?

=== person/charles-rennie-mackintosh
title: Charles Rennie Mackintosh
src: 7692e31d84c5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Charles Rennie Mackintosh (1868 to 1928) was a Glasgow architect-designer who led the 'Glasgow Style' of Art Nouveau, treating buildings, furniture and interiors as one unified art. His Glasgow School of Art and his total interior schemes made him a major influence on European design, though recognition at home arrived only after his death.
--- fact 1
Born in Glasgow in 1868, Mackintosh trained as an architect while taking evening classes at the Glasgow School of Art, where he met his future wife, the artist Margaret Macdonald.
--- fact 2
With Macdonald, her sister Frances, and Herbert MacNair, he formed "The Four," a symbolist group whose style became known as the Glasgow Style.
--- fact 3
At Honeyman and Keppie he won an anonymous 1896 competition to design his own art school's new building; its completion in 1909 made his reputation.
--- fact 4
He then designed houses, tearooms and a school around Glasgow, among them Hill House (1902 to 1904), the Willow Tearooms (1903) and Scotland Street School (1903 to 1906), with Macdonald designing the furnishings throughout.
--- fact 5
His tall ladder-back chairs, shaped as much to define a room as to be sat in, became his best-known design.
--- fact 6
After 1910, commissions dried up, through changing taste and his own difficult manner with clients, and by 1914 he had left Glasgow for England.
--- fact 7
In the 1920s he and Macdonald settled in southern France, where he turned to watercolours; he came back to London for cancer treatment and died there in 1928, in obscurity and hardship.
--- fact 8
Though nearly forgotten in Britain by his death, his work had already shaped Josef Hoffmann and the Wiener Werkstätte through the 1900 Vienna Secession, and now ranks among the era's most important architect-designers.
--- context
Mackintosh worked in Glasgow, within [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], for the tearoom trade that funded his interiors.
--- observer
I see a status-signal built for one city's cafes, and I watch it become, without its maker's help, an export. I see one man treat a building, its chairs, its wall stencils as a single problem, making a room cohere as one statement of taste; I see his chairs trade comfort for silhouette. His ideas reached Vienna and shaped Hoffmann's workshop even as his own home market turned away, reputation and trade travelling along separate roads. I read his late turn to watercolours as a withdrawal, not a collapse, and I remember that the admiring frame around his career is mostly the work of later historians, not his own.
--- question 1
How much of his lost commissions after 1910 came from changing taste, rather than his own manner with clients?

=== person/chico-mendes
title: Chico Mendes
src: 191b7595bc9b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chico Mendes (Francisco Alves Mendes Filho, 1944 to 1988) was a Brazilian rubber tapper, union leader and environmental activist in the state of Acre. He organized rubber tappers to defend the forest against clearing for cattle ranching, and was murdered in December 1988.
--- fact 1
Chico Mendes was born on 15 December 1944 near Xapuri, Acre, into a family of rubber tappers, or seringueiros.
--- fact 2
He helped found the rural workers' union in Xapuri in 1977, and was among the founders of the Workers' Party in Acre in 1980.
--- fact 3
He organized *empates*, non-violent standoffs in which tappers and their families stood in front of the crews clearing forest.
--- fact 4
In 1985 he helped found the National Council of Rubber Tappers, which promoted protected "extractive reserves" where residents could go on harvesting forest products.
--- fact 5
In 1987 he took his case to the Inter-American Development Bank in the United States, lobbying over highway funding in the Amazon, and received a United Nations Environment Programme Global 500 award.
--- fact 6
He was shot dead at his home in Xapuri on 22 December 1988, and the rancher Darly Alves da Silva and his son Darci were convicted of the murder in 1990.
--- fact 7
By 1990, extractive reserves had become a legal category in Brazil.
--- context
Chico Mendes worked in the [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]] during the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], amid the forest clearing of the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]. The forest he defended carries a long human history; see [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I see a labor tactic, bodies standing in front of chainsaws, joined to an ecological argument, and I notice that success arrived as law only after he was gone. I hear ranchers call the forest open land, and I hear tappers call the same trees a home and an income, two claims resting on one ground. I would trust this story less if the extractive reserves turned out to have mixed economic results for the people living in them, and a specialist could argue exactly that.
--- question 1
Since 1990, how well have extractive reserves protected both forest and livelihoods?

=== person/christopher-dresser
title: Christopher Dresser
src: 2a1f1b691852
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Christopher Dresser (1834 to 1904) was a botanist turned designer, now regarded as one of the first modern industrial designers. He worked as an independent consultant for dozens of manufacturers and produced geometric silverware and ceramics that anticipated 20th-century modernism.
--- fact 1
Dresser was born in Glasgow in 1834, and trained at the Government School of Design in London. He lectured on botany and, in 1859, received a doctorate from the University of Jena for research into plant structure.
--- fact 2
He moved into design from about 1858, taking on ceramic work for firms from the 1860s onward, though sources differ on exactly when this began, and published The Art of Decorative Design (1862) and Principles of Decorative Design (1873), arguing that ornament should follow the structural logic of natural forms rather than copy their surface.
--- fact 3
In 1876 and 1877 he travelled to Japan, one of the first Western designers to do so, in part on commission from Tiffany & Co. to report on Japanese art and manufacturing.
--- fact 4
He designed for Minton, Wedgwood, Coalbrookdale, Hukin & Heath and James Dixon & Sons, and served as Art Superintendent of the Linthorpe Art Pottery. He died in 1904.
--- fact 5
He reduced plants and shells to their underlying geometry, and embraced industrial methods, electroplating and moulded earthenware, that many reformers rejected. Key works include an electroplated teapot (1879), a toast rack (about 1880) and the Linthorpe "sea urchin" vase.
--- fact 6
He was long overshadowed by William Morris and the craft wing of the Aesthetic Movement, and has been substantially rediscovered since the late 20th century. His insistence that a mass-made object could be both functional and beautiful anticipated principles usually credited to the Bauhaus.
--- context
Dresser worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], designing for the machine at the height of the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], in contrast to [[person/william-morris|William Morris]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I watch a botanist build ornament from the structure of plants rather than their surface, and I watch him accept the machine that so many of his contemporaries refused. That his work was long overshadowed by the craft wing of his own movement, I recognize as a story we tell about design, the handmade as honest, deciding who gets remembered. His recent rediscovery as an ancestor of modernism runs the opposite way, choosing a lineage backward. I hold both as readings of objects that mostly speak for themselves.
--- question 1
How much of the work sold under his name did he design himself, rather than manufacturers' own staff?
