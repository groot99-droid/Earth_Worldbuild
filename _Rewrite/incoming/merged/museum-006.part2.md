=== people--ramesses-ii--1
src: 78055b7dd6c4
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Ramesses II (about 1303 to 1213 BCE), also called Ramesses the Great, was the third pharaoh of Egypt's Nineteenth Dynasty, and his life is a study in how power writes its own record. He ruled for about 66 years, fought the Hittites at Kadesh, made one of the earliest surviving peace treaties, and built temples across Egypt and Nubia.

By the most widely used chronology he came to the throne in 1279 BCE and reigned until 1213 BCE; other chronologies differ by several years.

About 1274 BCE he fought the Hittite king Muwatalli II at Kadesh, in present-day Syria. Both sides claimed victory. Ramesses had his version carved on temple walls, though most historians think the Hittites gained more. Notice the lesson: a wall is not a witness.

About 1259 BCE he made a treaty with the Hittite king Hattusili III. It survives in Egyptian and Hittite versions, and each says the other side asked for peace first.

He built the capital Pi-Ramesses, the Ramesseum and the temples at Abu Simbel, which were moved in the 1960s to escape the Aswan High Dam reservoir. Many monuments carry his name over those of earlier kings. This is an architecture of memory, built partly by overwriting.

He is estimated to have had more than 90 children and to have lived to about 90. His thirteenth son, Merneptah, succeeded him.

=== people--fu-hao--1
src: fdaddc10a184
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Fu Hao (died about 1200 BCE) was a consort of the Shang king Wu Ding, named in oracle bone inscriptions as a commander of armies and a leader of rites. Her tomb, found intact in 1976, is among the richest of the Shang period, a sealed chamber of bronze and jade. Her birth year is unknown.

The oracle bones name Fu Hao as a wife of the Shang king Wu Ding, whose reign is dated about 1250 to 1192 BCE, though the dates drift with the chronology. She is thought to have died about 1200 BCE.

The inscriptions tell of her leading military campaigns against neighboring groups and directing ritual sacrifices.

Her tomb (M5) lay at Yinxu, the Shang capital site near Anyang in Henan, undisturbed until 1976, when a team led by Zheng Zhenxiang opened it.

Within were 468 bronze objects and 755 jade objects, with hundreds of bone objects and thousands of cowry shells, gathered like a hoard beneath still water.

Many of the bronzes carry inscriptions naming her, Fu Hao, or "Mother Xin," and on these the tomb is identified with the woman of the oracle bones.

Fu Hao (died about 1200 BCE) shows you how a life can be rebuilt from two kinds of evidence that agree. She was a consort of the Shang king Wu Ding, named in oracle bone inscriptions as a military commander and a ritual leader, and her intact tomb, found in 1976, is among the richest of the Shang period. Her birth year is unknown.

First, the inscriptions. Oracle bones name Fu Hao as a wife of Wu Ding, whose reign is dated about 1250 to 1192 BCE, though dates vary by chronology. She is thought to have died about 1200 BCE. They record her leading military campaigns against neighboring groups and directing ritual sacrifices.

Second, the ground. Her tomb (M5) at Yinxu, the Shang capital site near Anyang in Henan, was found undisturbed in 1976 by a team led by Zheng Zhenxiang. It held 468 bronze objects and 755 jade objects, as well as hundreds of bone objects and thousands of cowry shells.

The bridge between them is a name. Inscriptions on many bronzes name her, Fu Hao, or "Mother Xin," and that is the basis for identifying the tomb with the woman of the oracle bones. When word and object meet, a person comes back into view.

=== people--gilgamesh--1
src: 4977367a30e5
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Gilgamesh was a king of the Sumerian city of Uruk, probably a real ruler of the Early Dynastic period sometime between about 2800 and 2500 BCE, who passed from the throne into story: he became the subject of the Epic of Gilgamesh, the earliest surviving major work of narrative literature.

The Sumerian King List names him the fifth king of Uruk's first dynasty and grants him an implausible 126-year reign, as the list so often stretches its early rulers like shadows at dusk.

Later inscriptions, notably the Tummal Inscription, written under Ishbi-Erra long after the Early Dynastic period, credit a king of this name with building the great city walls of Uruk, and the Epic itself repeats the claim as his lasting achievement.

The Epic, assembled from older Sumerian poems into its best-known Akkadian version around the 12th century BCE, follows his friendship with the wild man Enkidu, his quest for immortality after Enkidu's death, and at last his acceptance of mortality.

No securely contemporary royal inscription from his own lifetime has been found, though one inscription, possibly from a contemporary official at Ur, may name him. Most of what historians infer about a real Gilgamesh comes from later king lists, from dedicatory inscriptions naming him, and from the literary tradition itself, a man seen only through the water of later telling.
