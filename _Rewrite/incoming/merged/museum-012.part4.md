=== people--francisco-goya--1
src: f41ebf0977ed
mode: confessional-poetry
--- description
I stand in the room of Francisco Goya (30 March 1746 to 16 April 1828), Spanish court painter and printmaker, often called the last of the Old Masters and the first of the moderns, whom illness, political repression and the Napoleonic invasion drove from brilliant royal portraits toward satire, violence and the irrational.

I read that he was born in Fuendetodos in Aragon and grew up in Zaragoza; that in 1773 he married Josefa Bayeu, sister of the court painter Francisco Bayeu, who helped him win work designing tapestry cartoons in Madrid; that by 1786 he was painter to the king, and in 1799 First Court Painter to Charles IV.

I read that in 1792 to 1793 an illness left him permanently deaf. In 1799 he published Los Caprichos, satirical etchings mocking superstition and the clergy. When Napoleon's armies invaded Spain in 1808 he witnessed atrocities on both sides and recorded them in The Disasters of War, prints published only 35 years after his death. He painted The Second and Third of May 1808 in 1814.

Disillusioned by the repressive rule of Ferdinand VII, he retreated to a house outside Madrid and covered its walls with the Black Paintings, including Saturn Devouring His Son (1819 to 1823). In 1824 he went into exile in Bordeaux, where he died. His portraits, influenced by Velázquez, use loose brushwork and avoid flattery; he mastered aquatint for deep shadows and later applied paint with knives, rags and fingers.

Deaf, he kept watching. What would I have seen, if I could not look away? Witness is a kind of faith.

=== people--kamehameha-i--1
src: 8ac974bc3023
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Kamehameha I (about 1750 to 1819) was a chief of Hawaiʻi Island who gathered the Hawaiian Islands under one rule by 1810, founding the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi, whose last monarch was Liliuokalani.

He was born at Kohala on Hawaiʻi Island; estimates of his birth year range from 1736 to 1761. He died on 8 May 1819, though some sources say 14 May.

After his uncle, the ruler Kalaniʻōpuʻu, died in 1782, he became guardian of the war god Kūkaʻilimoku and defeated his cousin Kīwalaʻō at the Battle of Mokuʻōhai, winning Hawaiʻi Island after years of war.

He used Western firearms and cannon, and advisers such as John Young and Isaac Davis. In 1795 he invaded Oʻahu, and at Nuʻuanu hundreds of defenders were driven over the Pali cliff.

In 1810 Kauaʻi and Niʻihau came under his authority by negotiation with their ruler Kaumualiʻi, who remained as a tributary king, the last islands joining like stones set in a ring.

The Law of the Splintered Paddle (Māmalahoe Kānāwai), protecting noncombatants, is said to come from a 1782 incident in which fishermen struck him with a paddle.
