=== people--sundiata-keita--1
src: 21d6cba7078a
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Sundiata Keita (about 1217 to 1255) is the founder of the Mali Empire, known largely from oral tradition, above all the Epic of Sundiata, a story carried in the voice rather than on the page.

Tradition says he defeated the ruler Sumanguru at the Battle of Kirina around 1235.

His story was kept and performed by griots, hereditary oral historians, passed from mouth to mouth like fire from torch to torch.

Written sources on him are few, mainly from Arabic writers such as Ibn Khaldun.

Historians treat the details of the epic with caution.

=== people--hojo-tokimune--1
src: d48d621b513a
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Hojo Tokimune (1251 to 1284) was the eighth regent (shikken) of Japan's Kamakura shogunate, ruling as its de facto military leader through the Mongol invasions of Japan in 1274 and 1281.

He became regent in 1268 at about age sixteen by Western reckoning (eighteen by traditional counting), soon after the Mongol court of Kublai Khan first demanded Japan's submission.

He refused every Mongol diplomatic demand and reportedly had several Mongol envoys executed, a choice that made war all but certain.

Japanese defenders drove back the Mongol invasion fleets of 1274 and 1281. In 1281 a great typhoon, and in 1274 traditionally a storm or sudden reverse wind, later remembered as kamikaze ("divine wind"), tore apart large parts of the Mongol fleets after the defenders had already dealt losses to the invaders, the sea itself rising like a wall.

He was a patron of Zen Buddhism. He invited the Chinese monk Wuxue Zuyuan to Kamakura and founded the temple Engaku-ji, reportedly to honor the dead of both sides after the invasions.

=== people--amir-khusrau--1
src: 7b1816603b54
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Amir Khusrau (1253 to 1325) was the foremost court poet of the Delhi Sultanate, writing in Persian while drawing on the Indian world around him. He is regarded as the founding figure of Indo-Persian literature and is traditionally credited with founding qawwali, the devotional music of South Asian Sufi shrines.

He was born in 1253 near Delhi, to a Turkic father who had come from Central Asia and an Indian mother, and he spoke of this mixed heritage throughout his work, two rivers meeting in one voice.

He served as court poet under a rapid succession of Delhi sultans over more than four decades, through palace coups and dynastic changes.

He wrote in Persian, the language of the Indo-Islamic courts. His five long narrative poems, dated about 1298 to 1302, answer the Khamsa of the Persian poet Nizami Ganjavi and retell Shirin and Khusrau, Layla and Majnun, and the legend of Alexander the Great.

He was a devoted follower of the Sufi mystic Nizamuddin Auliya. Legend says Khusrau died within months of his master in 1325; their shrines stand together in Delhi.

He is traditionally credited with founding qawwali, still performed at Sufi shrines across India, Pakistan and Bangladesh. Later legend also credits him with inventing the sitar and tabla, without firm historical evidence.

=== people--mansa-musa--1
src: 8675ea5d4e58
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Mansa Musa (about 1280 to about 1337) ruled the Mali Empire, famous for his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 and for the great wealth of his realm.

Mali held major gold-producing regions of West Africa.

His pilgrimage to Mecca, with a large retinue and quantities of gold glittering like a river in sunlight, was widely reported and reportedly unsettled the gold market in Cairo.

He supported building and scholarship in cities such as Timbuktu.

What we know of his life comes mainly from Arabic accounts and later oral tradition.

=== people--ibn-battuta--1
src: 6a8094702a6d
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Ibn Battuta (1304 to about 1369) was a Moroccan scholar and traveller whose account, the Rihla, tells of journeys of about 117,000 kilometers (73,000 miles) across much of the Old World.

He left Tangier in 1325 on pilgrimage and kept travelling for about 30 years, like a wind that would not settle.

He visited Arabia, East Africa, Persia, India, Southeast Asia, and the Mali Empire (1352 to 1353).

He dictated his account to the scholar Ibn Juzayy.

Historians debate whether he truly visited every place he describes.
