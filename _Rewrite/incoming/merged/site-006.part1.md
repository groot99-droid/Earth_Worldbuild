=== place/hispaniola
title: Hispaniola
src: 498cdb61841d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hispaniola is the second-largest island of the Caribbean, in the Greater Antilles, one island divided into two sovereignties: Haiti in the west, the Dominican Republic in the east. Remember it also as a threshold, the site of the first lasting European settlement in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Consider the island's own architecture of height and depth: it covers about 76,000 square kilometers, and it holds both Pico Duarte, at 3,098 meters the highest peak in the Caribbean, and Lake Enriquillo, a saline lake about 40 meters below sea level.
--- fact 2
Many archaeologists date the earliest evidence of human presence on Hispaniola to about 4000 BCE, though estimates vary; by 1492 the island was home to Taíno societies organized into several chiefdoms, and Spanish chroniclers report the names Ayiti, Quisqueya and Bohío for it, a small ledger of what the island was called.
--- fact 3
Columbus reached Hispaniola in December 1492, and the Spanish laid down their first architecture of settlement: La Navidad (destroyed in 1493), La Isabela (1493) and Santo Domingo (1496).
--- fact 4
After 1492 the Taíno population of Hispaniola declined sharply under forced labor, violence and disease: a plain ledger, and each line of it a life.
--- fact 5
In 1697 Spain ceded the western third of Hispaniola to France, which built there the sugar and slave colony of Saint-Domingue; the Haitian Revolution (1791 to 1804) broke that architecture, ending slavery and French rule there.
--- fact 6
The Dominican Republic became independent in 1844, closing a chapter of Haitian rule over the whole island that had begun in 1822.
--- context
Think of Hispaniola as a junction where several threads cross: the [[culture/taino|Taino]], the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], the city of [[place/santo-domingo|Santo Domingo]], the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]. Its earliest human presence places it in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]]; its region is the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]]; and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]]. Follow any one of them and you arrive at the same shore.
--- observer
The Observer notes that one island now holds two states, two languages and two colonial histories, and asks you to see why: a treaty between distant European powers drew a claim-line through it in 1697, with no reference to anything on the island.

That is the architecture of the whole Atlantic system, compressed into one place. An indigenous society, a first European base, a plantation colony built on enslaved labor, and a successful revolution of enslaved people that founded a state: all of it within about three centuries. The pattern is not unique, and we should say so, since other Caribbean islands show parts of it. A specialist could weaken this reading by showing that Hispaniola was more exceptional, or more ordinary, than it suggests. Lines drawn from far away are still lived in up close.
--- question 1
When did the first people reach Hispaniola, and where did they come from?
--- question 2
How many Taíno lived on the island in 1492, before the ledger of loss began?

=== place/isfahan
title: Isfahan
src: b3959feee618
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Isfahan is a city on the Zayande River in central Iran, twice a capital: a Seljuk capital from 1051, and the capital of the Safavid Empire from 1598, when Shah Abbas I rebuilt its heart around the great square Naqsh-e Jahan, laid open like a mirror turned to the sky.
--- fact 1
Isfahan stands on the Zayande River on the Iranian Plateau and was settled long before Islam; it became a Seljuk capital in 1051, and its Friday Mosque holds more than a thousand years of mosque architecture as a riverbank holds the layers of its floods (UNESCO inscribed it in 2012).
--- fact 2
After a revolt in 1387, Timur's forces killed a large part of Isfahan's population; the chroniclers set down figures of tens of thousands.
--- fact 3
In 1598 Shah Abbas I carried the Safavid capital from Qazvin to Isfahan, and there he raised the Naqsh-e Jahan (Imam) Square, the Shah and Sheikh Lotfollah mosques, the Ali Qapu palace and covered markets that kept the shade as cisterns keep water.
--- fact 4
Estimates by 17th-century European visitors put Isfahan's people at several hundred thousand, and a Persian saying crowned the city "half the world".
--- fact 5
In 1604 to 1605 Shah Abbas forcibly moved many Armenians from Julfa on the Aras River into Safavid territory, and many died on the way; those who survived founded New Julfa, an Armenian quarter of Isfahan, in about 1606, carrying the name of the town they had been taken from.
--- fact 6
In 1722 Afghan forces under Mahmud Hotak besieged Isfahan, and the famine that followed killed many tens of thousands of its inhabitants; the fall of the city was a decisive blow to Safavid rule, the keystone struck from its arch.
--- fact 7
In the 18th century the crown passed from Isfahan and it ceased to be the capital; in 1979 UNESCO inscribed Meidan Emam, Esfahan.
--- context
Isfahan was the throne-city of the [[culture/safavid-empire|Safavid Empire]], which faced the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] as a rival, two peaks across one valley; before that, the city had suffered the massacre ordered by Timur, founder of the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]]. Its place in the chronicle lies within the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and three theme threads run through it like channels drawn from one river: [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I stood in the square and understood that it was built to be seen. I saw the palace, I saw the royal mosque, I saw the merchants' bazaar, each facing the others: the ruler's authority, the religious establishment and commerce set in one visible arrangement, a status-signal written in urban form. A capital designed as a display.

And I remember the Armenians. The same ruler moved them by force, and I record it as suffering. What do I do with the fact that the coercion also produced a prosperous merchant community, trading silk across Eurasia, an exchange-web grown out of coercion? I will unlearn this if the wealth of New Julfa proves to have come mainly from trading networks that existed before Safavid patronage.

Beauty and its cost can stand in the same square.
--- question 1

--- question 2
How many Armenians died on the way, during the deportation from Julfa?
