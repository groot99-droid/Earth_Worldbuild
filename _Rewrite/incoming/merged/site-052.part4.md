=== person/tokugawa-ieyasu
title: Tokugawa Ieyasu
src: a52809f13ea0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543 to 1616) unified Japan after decades of civil war and founded the Tokugawa shogunate, a military government that ruled Japan for over two and a half centuries.
--- fact 1
He rose through alliance and conflict with the era's other major warlords, Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, before defeating a rival coalition at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600.
--- fact 2
He was appointed shogun by the emperor in 1603, then formally passed the title to his son Hidetada in 1605, while continuing to hold real power as retired shogun until his death.
--- fact 3
He destroyed the remaining Toyotomi family stronghold at the siege of Osaka Castle in 1615, removing the last major rival claim to national authority.
--- fact 4
His government issued regulations restricting Christianity and foreign trade, which his successors expanded into the sakoku ("closed country") policy, limiting most foreign contact for over two centuries.
--- context
Tokugawa Ieyasu founded the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], ending the Sengoku period of civil war. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ieyasu's caution, his willingness to wait out rivals rather than defeat them quickly, is often credited for his success where more aggressive contemporaries failed; a well-known adage from the era compares Nobunaga, Hideyoshi and Ieyasu by how each supposedly answered a caged bird that would not sing, with Ieyasu simply waiting for it to sing on its own.
--- question 1
How deliberate was the shift toward isolationism under his immediate successors, rather than a gradual response to specific incidents involving European traders and missionaries?

=== person/tomyris
title: Tomyris
src: 2bc9aeaf32a3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tomyris was a queen of the Massagetae, a nomadic people east of the Caspian Sea who, according to the Greek historian Herodotus, defeated and killed Cyrus the Great of Persia in 530 BCE. She is known only from Herodotus, and other ancient accounts of Cyrus's death differ.
--- fact 1
Tomyris appears only in Book 1 of Herodotus's Histories, written about a century after the events; her own dates are unknown, and the date given here is that of the battle in his account.
--- fact 2
Herodotus says she ruled the Massagetae, who lived east of the Caspian Sea near the Araxes River, and that she rejected Cyrus's offer of marriage, seeing it as a way for him to gain her kingdom.
--- fact 3
In his account, the Persians captured her son Spargapises by trickery, and he killed himself in captivity; Tomyris then defeated the Persian army in battle.
--- fact 4
Herodotus says she had Cyrus's body found and his head placed in a wineskin filled with blood.
--- fact 5
Other ancient accounts differ: Xenophon has Cyrus die peacefully in his palace, while Ctesias has him die of a wound in a war on the eastern frontier that the Persians won.
--- fact 6
Herodotus compares the customs of the Massagetae with those of the Scythians.
--- context
Tomyris's story concerns the death of [[person/cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], founder of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], on the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]] frontier during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Tomyris survives only through an outsider's account, one that gives a steppe queen a speech and a grim revenge, story-glue for Greek readers about the limits of a great king. That the tale turns on a mother's lost son shows how the kin-lattice gave it its meaning. The reading would weaken if steppe or archaeological evidence showed a different ruler, or if the Persian defeat proved to be invented.
--- question 1
What do archaeology and steppe sources themselves add about the Massagetae?

=== person/toussaint-louverture
title: Toussaint Louverture
src: aa3e7c810dfb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man, became the principal military and political leader of the Haitian Revolution, and died in a French prison in 1803.
--- fact 1
He was born into slavery on Saint-Domingue around 1743 (20 May 1743 in one account) and was freed in 1776 or 1777, depending on the source, before the revolution began in 1791.
--- fact 2
He began as a lieutenant in the 1791 uprising and at first allied with the Spanish of neighboring Santo Domingo; in 1794 he switched to the French Republic, which had abolished slavery, and in 1798 he negotiated the withdrawal of British forces under a treaty with General Thomas Maitland.
--- fact 3
On 7 July 1801 he promulgated a constitution asserting his authority over the whole island of Hispaniola, which made him governor-general for life.
--- fact 4
Napoleon sent an expedition in 1802; Toussaint was arrested by deception and deported to France, where he died at Fort de Joux in April 1803.
--- fact 5
Jean-Jacques Dessalines proclaimed Haiti's independence on 1 January 1804.
--- context
Toussaint was the central figure of the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]], an event within the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]] that led to [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]] in Saint-Domingue. He sits within the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a man born into slavery wrote a constitution and commanded armies, and that the empire he fought treated his rights as negotiable, and betrayed them by treachery. The universal principles proclaimed in that era were tested most severely by the very people excluded from them.
--- question 1
How far did his constitution actually intend independence?

=== person/trajan
title: Trajan
src: 82d31911002c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Trajan (53 to 117 CE) was Roman emperor from 98 to 117, presiding over the empire at its greatest territorial extent and over a major program of public building and infrastructure across the Roman world.
--- fact 1
Born in Hispania, in present-day Spain, he was the first Roman emperor born outside Italy, adopted as heir by the emperor Nerva in 97 after a career as a successful general.
--- fact 2
His conquest of Dacia, commemorated in the reliefs of Trajan's Column in Rome, added a wealthy new province, rich in gold mines, north of the Danube.
--- fact 3
He extended Roman territory to its largest extent by annexing Armenia and parts of Mesopotamia after a campaign against the Parthian Empire late in his reign, gains his successor Hadrian largely abandoned as unsustainable.
--- fact 4
His building programs included Trajan's Forum and Market in Rome, a new harbor at Ostia, and an extensive network of roads and bridges across the provinces, funded largely by Dacian gold.
--- context
Trajan led the [[culture/romans|Romans]] from the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], expanding the empire to its widest extent. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Trajan's eastern conquests, which later Roman writers celebrated as the empire's high-water mark, were abandoned within a year of his death by his own successor as indefensible; the empire's territorial peak and its practical limits arrived almost together.
--- question 1
Would Trajan's eastern conquests have proven sustainable had he lived longer to consolidate them, or was Hadrian's withdrawal actually the more realistic assessment?
