=== observer-note/second-chances-how-the-species-ran-the-experiment-again
title: Second Chances - How the Species Ran the Experiment Again
src: a5ad4bb19629
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
When two populations have no contact with each other but face the same problem, do they tend to reach the same solution?
--- observer
I see it as a natural experiment. I watch two hemispheres, split apart for millennia, each left to run the trial without the other's knowledge. I watch the same outcomes rise from both — farming, then cities, then states and the keeping of records — and it suggests to me that population density, storage, and the need to hold strangers together push a people toward the same shapes. I watch, too, the differences, and they tell me these pressures dictate no single form. History has a direction, I have come to think. But it walks many roads.
--- extra 1: Question
When two populations have no contact with each other but face the same problem, do they tend to reach the same solution?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Farming took root independently in several regions — the Fertile Crescent, China, New Guinea, and the Americas among them; see [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] and [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]].
- Cities and states rose up separately in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus valley, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes; see [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]] and [[place/caral|Caral]].
- Writing was invented independently at least three or four times; see [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]].
- The differences teach as much as the similarities: the Andes ran a large state without conventional writing ([[technology/quipu|Quipu]]), the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]] shows scant sign of kings, and the Americas never had wheeled transport.
- The Old and New Worlds stood largely out of contact from about 15,000 years ago until 1492, save for brief Norse voyages around 1000 CE.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The sample is small, and some of these cases may not be fully independent, since ideas can travel across vast regions. Nor should 'independent' be read as 'identical': each society made its own choices along the way.

=== observer-note/story-glue-how-strangers-cooperate-at-scale
title: Story-Glue - How Strangers Cooperate at Scale
src: 232917d9ecf4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Most animals cooperate only with kin or with individuals they know personally. How, then, does one species build cooperation among millions of strangers who will never meet?
--- observer
Call this story-glue: a corporation, a nation, a currency, and a law code exist chiefly because enough people act as though they do. This architecture of shared belief lets the species coordinate at a scale no other animal reaches, and it lets that same species be led into shared error just as readily. Change the story, and behavior can change fast, with no change to anyone's biology at all.
--- extra 1: Question
How does one species build cooperation among millions of strangers who will never meet?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Small foraging bands leaned on personal knowledge and ties of kinship.
- Cities from about 3500 BCE demanded cooperation among strangers; see [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]].
- Shared gods, rulers, and laws supplied a common story; see [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] and [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]].
- The universal religions and ethical systems of the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]] addressed strangers as members of a single moral community.
- Modern states, corporations, and currencies rest on that same shared belief; see [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]].
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The concept can be stretched too far. Material conditions, force, and self-interest matter too, and not every institution is only a story.
