=== person/christopher-wren
title: Christopher Wren
src: 2504edb36778
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Christopher Wren (1632 to 1723) was an English mathematician and astronomer, a founder of the Royal Society, who turned architect after the Great Fire of London in 1666. He designed more than fifty City churches and a new St Paul's Cathedral, topped out in 1708 and declared complete by Parliament in 1711.
--- fact 1
Wren was born in East Knoyle, Wiltshire, the son of a clergyman. He was appointed professor of astronomy at Gresham College at 25, then Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford, and became a founding member of the Royal Society in 1662.
--- fact 2
His career as an architect began with the Sheldonian Theatre in Oxford. The Great Fire of September 1666 then destroyed old St Paul's and most of the City.
--- fact 3
He was appointed Surveyor of the King's Works in 1669, and spent the rest of his working life on the City's parish churches and on St Paul's. He died in 1723, aged 90, and lies buried in the cathedral crypt.
--- fact 4
He combined the classical vocabulary of Inigo Jones and Palladio with French and Italian Baroque elements studied on a research trip to Paris, the only time he ever travelled outside England.
--- fact 5
The dome of St Paul's rests on three independent shells nested one inside the other, so that exterior profile, interior ceiling and structural core could each be optimised on its own.
--- fact 6
His key works include the Royal Observatory, Greenwich (1675 to 1676), the Royal Hospital Chelsea (1682 to 1692) and the Monument to the Great Fire (1671 to 1677).
--- fact 7
Many City churches were later damaged or demolished, by Victorian redevelopment and by German bombing, but a substantial number still stand. Photographs of the dome rising above the smoke of the Blitz in 1940 circulated worldwide.
--- context
Wren worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]], drawing on [[person/andrea-palladio|Andrea Palladio]]'s classical vocabulary. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I think of a fire that destroyed a city, and of the mathematician it handed the chance to become that city's chief builder, so that catastrophe became the hinge of a career and of a skyline. I see the triple-shell dome as pattern-taming turned to stone. And I watch St Paul's become, through the photographs of 1940, a story we tell ourselves about endurance, a meaning laid on the building by later events rather than one Wren ever intended.
--- question 1
Which of the City churches he designed still survive in their original form?

=== person/cixi
title: Cixi
src: b3c21ce93957
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Empress Dowager Cixi (1835 to 1908) stood as the dominant political figure of late Qing China, ruling as regent for most of the period from 1861 to 1908.
--- fact 1
She became regent in 1861 for her young son, the Tongzhi Emperor, and later still for the Guangxu Emperor.
--- fact 2
Her era included the Self-Strengthening Movement, an effort to modernize China's defenses and industry.
--- fact 3
She supported the Boxers in 1900, and after their defeat turned to pursue the 'New Policies' reforms.
--- fact 4
Historians still debate her legacy: older accounts portray her as a reactionary, while recent work stresses her political skill and the limits she faced.
--- context
Cixi ruled the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]] through the aftermath of the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]] and the [[event/boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]]. Compare [[person/wu-zetian|Wu Zetian]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that, as with earlier women rulers, the record on Cixi comes to us largely through her opponents. See [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]].
--- question 1
How should we assess Cixi's role in the Qing decline?

=== person/claude-monet
title: Claude Monet
src: a562288853e0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Claude Monet (14 November 1840 to 5 December 1926) was a French painter, the leader and purest practitioner of Impressionism. His Impression, Sunrise gave the movement its name, and he spent a lifetime painting the changing effects of light.
--- fact 1
Monet was born in Paris and grew up in Le Havre, where as a teenager he sold caricatures. Eugène Boudin persuaded him to paint outdoors, and in Paris he met Camille Pissarro and befriended Renoir, Sisley and Bazille.
--- fact 2
The 1860s and 1870s were years of poverty for him. During the Franco-Prussian War he took refuge in London, where he saw Turner's paintings and met the dealer Paul Durand-Ruel.
--- fact 3
In 1874 he and his friends held an exhibition independent of the official Salon. Critics mocked them for it, and the name "Impressionist" stuck.
--- fact 4
In 1883 his family settled at Giverny in Normandy, where he built a flower garden and a water garden with a Japanese bridge, which became his main subject.
--- fact 5
He painted the same subject again and again under changing conditions: haystacks, poplars, Rouen Cathedral. Key works include Impression, Sunrise (1872), Stacks of Wheat (1890 to 1891) and the Water Lilies.
--- fact 6
In his last decade he fought cataracts while working on the giant Grandes Décorations, which he gave to the French state as a monument to peace after the First World War. He died at Giverny.
--- fact 7
The Abstract Expressionists rediscovered his late water lilies in the 1950s; his house and gardens opened to the public in 1980, and in 2019 one of his Haystacks sold for $110.7 million.
--- context
Monet worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], learning from [[person/j-m-w-turner|J. M. W. Turner]] and from Japanese prints by [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]] and [[person/utagawa-hiroshige|Utagawa Hiroshige]], and gave his last great work to France after [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I find a painter whose true subject was the act of seeing, and I find his method, the same motif returned to under changing light, proof enough that one scene is many scenes. I remember that critics named the movement to mock it, and that the name became a badge instead, insult and category folded into one. And I think of his paintings turned into objects of enormous price, $110.7 million for a single Haystacks in 2019, a status-signal the mocking critics of 1874 could never have foreseen.
--- question 1
How did his cataracts change the colours of his last paintings?

=== person/cleopatra-vii
title: Cleopatra VII
src: bd876fb79b44
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cleopatra VII was the last active ruler of the Ptolemaic Kingdom of Egypt. She allied with Julius Caesar and Mark Antony, and died in 30 BCE after their defeat by Octavian.
--- fact 1
She belonged to the Ptolemaic dynasty, founded by one of Alexander the Great's Macedonian generals.
--- fact 2
Ancient sources say she spoke several languages, and, unlike most of her Ptolemaic predecessors, learned Egyptian.
--- fact 3
She had a son with Julius Caesar, and children with Mark Antony.
--- fact 4
At the Battle of Actium in 31 BCE, Octavian defeated Antony and Cleopatra.
--- fact 5
Following her death in 30 BCE, Egypt passed into a Roman province.
--- context
Cleopatra was a Greek-speaking ruler over the land of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]], descended from the generals of [[person/alexander-the-great|Alexander the Great]]. Her defeat by [[person/augustus|Augustus]] ended the last Hellenistic kingdom. Cleopatra VII belongs to [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], within the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that most of what is known about her comes down through Roman authors who had reasons of their own to portray her as a threat. The record was written by her enemies.
--- question 1
How much of her traditional portrait comes from Roman propaganda?

=== person/confucius
title: Confucius
src: 029acc93bdc1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Confucius (Kong Fuzi, 551 to 479 BCE) was a Chinese teacher and thinker whose ideas about ethics, family and government shaped East Asian societies for over two thousand years.
--- fact 1
His disciples gathered his sayings into the Analects.
--- fact 2
Among his central ideas are ren, or humaneness, li, or ritual propriety, and filial piety.
--- fact 3
He was not widely influential during his own lifetime; his ideas rose to prominence only later, under the Han dynasty.
--- fact 4
Confucian learning later became the very basis of the imperial civil service examinations.
--- context
Confucius is counted among the figures linked to the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]]. See [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and [[region/east-asia|East Asia]].
--- observer
I find a teacher who held no political power at all, and yet supplied the ethical framework of an entire state. I find the kin-lattice, the bonds of family, projected outward until it became a model for how to govern. What is government, if not a family grown too large to know all its own names?
--- question 1
How much of the Analects truly reflects Confucius himself?
