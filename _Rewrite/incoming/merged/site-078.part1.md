=== species/black-rat
src: e1466fe1a779
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The black rat, Rattus rattus, is a rodent born of South Asia and Southeast Asia that became one of humanity's most widespread companions, riding trade and shipping across Eurasia and later the whole world, and serving as a major vector of plague.
--- fact 1
Genetic studies find well-differentiated black rat lineages native to South Asia, the Himalayan region and Indochina, their split beginning long before any human involvement; exactly when the black rat first took up residence alongside people in that homeland is not precisely dated.
--- fact 2
Out of South Asia the black rat traveled trade routes to the Near East and Egypt in antiquity, and documented Roman-era shipping carried it across the Mediterranean and into the wider Roman Empire, reaching as far as Britain by about the 1st century CE.
--- fact 3
As a commensal species, the black rat thrives in and around human granaries, ships and settlements, living on stored grain and waste, and so travels wherever people and cargo move.
--- fact 4
Fleas carried on black rats are considered a major vector of Yersinia pestis, the bacterium behind the Plague of Justinian and the Black Death, though the disease's spread beyond rat-infested areas suggests other means of transmission, human fleas and lice among them, also mattered.
--- fact 5
The black rat was later pushed out of much of Europe and North America by the larger brown rat, Rattus norvegicus, which spread out of Asia in the 18th century, though the black rat remains widespread in warmer regions and on many islands.
--- fact 6
As an invasive species on islands with no native rodents, the black rat has caused the extinction or decline of ground-nesting birds and other native species, a continuing ecological effect of its spread.
--- context
The black rat traveled the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]] and the Mediterranean shipping lanes of the [[culture/romans|Romans]], and its fleas stand implicated in the spread of the [[event/black-death|Black Death]]. It belongs to [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], in its documented Roman-era spread, and to the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: the black rat shows you that a network of exchange built for grain and cloth can carry passengers nobody intended, so that trade and harm travel together through a rodent that neither side of any transaction meant to move. Wherever ships and granaries opened new habitat, this one species followed without ever being domesticated, a hitchhiker riding the very architecture of storage and transport humanity built for itself. This reading would weaken if genetic dating eventually shows the rat's major dispersals tracking climate and rodent competition more than human shipping.
--- question 1
When did the black rat first take up life alongside people in South Asia, and how long before its spread across the Old World?
--- question 2
How much of the Black Death's spread depended on the black rat specifically, set against human fleas and lice?

=== species/honeybee
src: 39e67874c54c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The honeybee, Apis mellifera, is a social insect kept by humans for honey, wax and pollination since at least the mid-3rd millennium BCE in Egypt, after millennia of unmanaged honey hunting before it.
--- fact 1
The oldest evidence of humans exploiting honeybees is a rock painting at Cuevas de la Araña in Spain, showing a figure climbing to raid a wild hive, dated to about the 6th millennium BCE; this was honey hunting, not the management of colonies.
--- fact 2
The earliest known depiction of organized beekeeping, with hives and smoke used to calm bees, comes from a relief in the sun temple of the Egyptian pharaoh Nyuserre Ini, dated to about 2450 BCE; honeybee hieroglyphs appear even earlier, from the First Dynasty.
--- fact 3
Egyptian beekeepers used cylindrical hives, often of clay or woven reed and mud, and moved them along the Nile by boat to follow the flowering seasons, a practice recorded in later texts.
--- fact 4
Honey served as a sweetener, a preservative, an offering in temple ritual and a component of medical remedies in Egypt and other ancient societies; beeswax was used for sealing, casting and mummification.
--- fact 5
European colonists brought Apis mellifera to the Americas in the 17th century, where it was not native, as part of the broader exchange of organisms called the Columbian Exchange; some Indigenous peoples came to call the honeybee "the white man's fly" because its spread often preceded settler expansion.
--- fact 6
Beekeeping later became agriculturally essential for pollinating commercial crops, and from the late 20th century colony collapse disorder and pathogens raised concern about honeybee populations worldwide.
--- context
The honeybee was kept and depicted by the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]], one of the domesticated organisms folded into the wider suite of managed species that followed [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], and it was carried to a new hemisphere during the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], in its earliest documented management, and to the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: managing bees required people to learn to work with, rather than against, an insect society ordered on its own terms, so beekeeping stands among the earliest lines of tools built on cooperating with another species' order instead of simply killing or caging it. That a colonizing population could nickname an insect after the settlers who unintentionally carried it shows you how an ordinary network of trade and settlement leaves ecological footprints that outrun any single claim to the land. This reading would weaken if genetic evidence shows honeybees dispersing into new regions chiefly on their own rather than through deliberate human transport.
--- question 1
How much earlier than 2450 BCE did organized beekeeping begin, and where?
--- question 2
How much has managed pollination reshaped agricultural landscapes compared with wild pollinators?

=== species/influenza-a-virus
src: 71bab881e738
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Influenza A virus is a family of respiratory viruses whose natural reservoir is wild aquatic birds. It has repeatedly crossed into humans to cause pandemics, the first clearly documented in 1580 and the most severe in 1918.
--- fact 1
Influenza A viruses of all recognized subtypes occur naturally in wild aquatic birds, considered the virus's ultimate reservoir; only a smaller number of subtypes have adapted to infect humans, pigs, horses and other mammals.
--- fact 2
The first pandemic clearly matching the description of influenza is generally dated to 1580, thought to have begun in Asia and spread along the Silk Road and new maritime trade routes through the Middle East into Europe and Africa within about six months, later reaching the Americas.
--- fact 3
Historians have documented roughly thirty further influenza pandemics since 1580, though descriptions from before reliable virology make some identifications uncertain.
--- fact 4
The 1918 to 1919 influenza pandemic, caused by an H1N1 strain, killed tens of millions of people worldwide, more than the First World War with which it coincided, its spread worsened by wartime troop movements and by censorship of news about it in combatant countries.
--- fact 5
Influenza A virus was first isolated in the laboratory in 1933, using ferrets, establishing the modern scientific study of the virus and its distinct subtypes, defined by combinations of hemagglutinin and neuraminidase surface proteins.
--- fact 6
Because influenza A viruses mutate quickly and can exchange genetic material when two strains infect the same host, new pandemic strains continue to emerge from animal reservoirs, including the H1N1 "swine flu" of 2009 and various avian influenza strains monitored for pandemic potential.
--- context
Influenza A virus caused the [[event/1918-influenza-pandemic|1918 Influenza Pandemic]], and its pattern of periodic pandemic emergence is compared by epidemiologists to the more recent [[event/covid-19-pandemic|COVID-19 Pandemic]]; its first documented pandemic spread along the corridors of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer records the deaths of 1918, and of every other severe influenza pandemic, as suffering compounded, in that instance, by a war that moved young, densely packed populations across the world at the very moment the virus needed to spread. That a virus native to wild birds can repeatedly leap into a species with no acquired immunity shows you the network of trade, travel and animal contact working in the pathogen's favor as readily as in ours. Wartime censorship of the 1918 pandemic, which let neutral Spain's uncensored press give the pandemic its common name, is itself a case of state secrecy shaping which suffering gets recorded and which is hidden. This reading would weaken if genetic studies eventually trace 1918's H1N1 strain to a much earlier, more gradual process of adaptation rather than a fast pandemic emergence.
--- question 1
How reliably can pre-19th-century epidemics be identified as influenza rather than another respiratory disease?
--- question 2
What conditions decide whether a new avian-origin influenza strain becomes a global pandemic rather than a contained outbreak?
