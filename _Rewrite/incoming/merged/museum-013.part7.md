=== people--claude-monet--1
src: c0e88bc4ca71
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Claude Monet (14 November 1840 to 5 December 1926) was a French painter, the leader and purest practitioner of Impressionism. His Impression, Sunrise gave the movement its name, and all his life he painted the shifting play of light, as water forever changes under the sky.

He was born in Paris and grew up in Le Havre, where as a youth he sold caricatures. Eugène Boudin persuaded him to paint out of doors. In Paris he met Camille Pissarro and befriended Renoir, Sisley and Bazille.

The 1860s and 1870s were years of poverty. During the Franco-Prussian War he took refuge in London, where he saw Turner's paintings and met the dealer Paul Durand-Ruel.

In 1874 he and his friends held an exhibition apart from the official Salon. Critics mocked them, and the name "Impressionist" stuck.

In 1883 his family settled at Giverny in Normandy, where he made a flower garden and a water garden with a Japanese bridge, which became his chief subject.

He painted the same subject again and again in changing conditions: haystacks, poplars and Rouen Cathedral. Key works include Impression, Sunrise (1872), Stacks of Wheat (1890 to 1891) and the Water Lilies.

=== people--jose-maria-velasco--1
src: 14fee25fa47e
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
José María Velasco (6 July 1840 to 26 August 1912) was the most important Mexican painter of the 19th century, known for his panoramic views of the Valley of Mexico, the land laid open beneath a vast sky. He was also a trained naturalist who illustrated scientific publications on botany, zoology and geology.

He was born in Temascalcingo, in the State of Mexico, and his family moved to Mexico City in 1849. In 1858 he entered the Academy of San Carlos, where the Italian painter Eugenio Landesio taught him to watch nature closely and paint out of doors. He also studied botany, zoology, anatomy, physics and mathematics.

In 1877 he followed Landesio as professor of landscape painting, and Diego Rivera was among his students.

He worked as a scientific illustrator for the National Museum and on expeditions, and he painted archaeological sites such as Teotihuacan.

He won a medal at the Paris Universal Exposition of 1889 and showed at the 1893 Chicago World's Columbian Exposition. One view of the Valley of Mexico was given to Pope Leo XIII and now hangs in the Vatican Museums.

He worked from outdoor sketches toward large studio compositions. His panoramas set a detailed foreground of cacti and rocks against a luminous distance, and often include railways, bridges and haciendas beside nature and ruins.
