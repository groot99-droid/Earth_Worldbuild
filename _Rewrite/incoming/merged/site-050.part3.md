=== person/mimar-sinan
title: Mimar Sinan
src: b43e2a3b5657
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mimar Sinan (about 1489 to 1588) served as chief royal architect to three Ottoman sultans across nearly fifty years. He is credited with several hundred structures, and his Selimiye Mosque at Edirne is regarded as the summit of classical Ottoman architecture.
--- fact 1
Sinan was born about 1489 or 1490 in Ağırnas, near Kayseri in central Anatolia, into a Christian family; sources disagree on whether that family was Greek or Armenian.
--- fact 2
He was conscripted through the devşirme levy into the Ottoman elite corps, converted to Islam, and trained as a Janissary. He rose through military engineering, building bridges and fortifications on campaign for Suleiman the Magnificent.
--- fact 3
Chelebi Lütfi Pasha, on becoming Grand Vizier in 1539, appointed him chief royal architect, a post Sinan held into his late nineties, and he died in Istanbul in 1588.
--- fact 4
His principal buildings are the Şehzade Mosque (1543 to 1548), the Süleymaniye Mosque (1550 to 1557), and the Selimiye Mosque (1568 or 1569 to 1574 or 1575), which he himself described as his apprenticeship, his journeyman work, and his masterpiece.
--- fact 5
He worked to raise domes wider and more daring than Hagia Sophia, the Byzantine cathedral converted to a mosque after 1453, using semi-domes, buttressing piers and pendentives. He also built aqueducts and bridges, some of which are still in use.
--- fact 6
He trained a generation of students. Turkey's largest architecture prize carries his name, and the Selimiye Mosque was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2011.
--- context
Sinan served the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] in [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], a century after the [[event/fall-of-constantinople|Fall of Constantinople]] left Hagia Sophia as his measure. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I see a boy taken from a Christian village by a levy, and I see that same boy rise to design an empire's mosques — the state's demand for people becoming, in his one case, an engine of monumental building. Each dome he raised is a claim in masonry against an older building, and I remember his own three-stage account of apprenticeship, journeyman work and masterpiece, a tool-lineage where each structure teaches the next. That account is his own narrative first, and later reverence has only added more to it. A man conscripted as tribute still chose, stone by stone, what to build with his life.
--- question 1
Of the several hundred structures credited to Sinan, how many did he personally design?
--- question 2
Which parts of his own and later accounts of his career and birth can actually be documented?

=== person/mirabai
title: Mirabai
src: 64f0c39dcb70
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mirabai (traditionally about 1498 to about 1546) was a Rajput princess from Rajasthan and one of the best-known poet-saints of the Bhakti movement, devoted to Krishna; almost nothing about her life is documented, and her surviving poems were set down long after her time.
--- fact 1
By tradition Mirabai was born about 1498 at Kudki in Rajasthan, into the Rathore Rajput family of Merta, and died about 1546 or 1547; both dates come only from later tradition.
--- fact 2
Tradition holds that she married Bhoj Raj, heir of Mewar, in 1516, and that he died about 1521 of battle wounds.
--- fact 3
Her poems, or padas, are composed in Rajasthani and related Hindi, and address Krishna in the language of love and separation.
--- fact 4
Tradition credits her with a very large number of poems, though scholars think only a few hundred may be authentic, and only two are known from manuscripts older than the early eighteenth century.
--- fact 5
Stories that her in-laws tried to poison her, and that she vanished into an image of Krishna, are hagiographic legends; the earliest records, from the seventeenth century, do not even mention her marriage or her in-laws.
--- context
Mirabai's life falls within [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], as the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] replaced the [[culture/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]] across northern India in 1526. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I think of a woman who left almost no trace in her own time, and I think of how she became one of the most widely sung voices of her tradition anyway. I remember the songs that place devotion to a god above the duties of marriage and household, and I remember that singers have restated that same claim about the kin-lattice for five centuries. A specialist could argue that a single historical Mirabai may never be recovered at all, which would weaken any reading of the poems as her own biography. What we sing of her may be older than any woman who lived it.
--- question 1
Which of the poems attributed to her, if any, can actually be dated to the sixteenth century?
--- question 2
How far do the persecution stories reflect later debates over women's devotion versus family duty?

=== person/montezuma-ii
title: Montezuma II
src: 35635d356962
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Montezuma II (Moctezuma; about 1466 to 1520) was the ninth tlatoani, or ruler, of the Aztec Empire, reigning at its greatest territorial extent until the arrival of Hernan Cortes and the Spanish conquest.
--- fact 1
He extended Aztec control through further conquests mainly in Oaxaca and Guerrero, along with a campaign around 1514 into the Huastec region, and reorganized the empire's systems of tribute and administration.
--- fact 2
Cortes and a small Spanish force reached the capital, Tenochtitlan, in November 1519; Montezuma received them, and was afterward held hostage within his own palace.
--- fact 3
Accounts of his final months diverge: Spanish sources hold that he tried to calm his people during the fighting that followed the 1520 Toxcatl massacre and Cortes's return, and died from a stone thrown by his own subjects, while some later Aztec-descended chroniclers hold that the Spanish killed him directly.
--- fact 4
His death in June 1520 came shortly before the Spanish were driven from the city in the "Noche Triste"; his brother Cuitlahuac, and then his nephew Cuauhtemoc, carried on the resistance that ended with Tenochtitlan's fall in 1521.
--- context
Montezuma II ruled the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], and his capture set off the chain of events that ended Aztec independence. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that nearly every detailed account of Montezuma's final year was written by a side that stood to benefit from portraying him as passive, or as already discredited among his own people. The surviving record cannot fully separate the man from the story that his conquerors, and his own later-defeated dynasty, each had reason to tell.
--- question 1
Did Montezuma initially treat the Spanish as returning gods, as later legend holds, or simply as an ordinary diplomatic and military problem?

=== person/muhammad
title: Muhammad
src: 5dfc20c76fbf
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Muhammad (about 570 to 632 CE) was an Arabian religious and political leader, regarded by Muslims as the last prophet, and the founder of the community that grew into the Islamic world.
--- fact 1
Born in Mecca, he began, according to Islamic tradition, receiving revelations around 610, later compiled as the Quran.
--- fact 2
In 622 he and his followers migrated to Medina, the Hijra, marking the start of the Islamic calendar.
--- fact 3
By the time of his death, most of the Arabian Peninsula had joined the community.
--- fact 4
Today Islam is followed by roughly a quarter of the world's population.
--- context
See [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. After his death, his community's expansion led to the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] and a new zone of shared learning.
--- observer
The Observer approaches this as it approaches every meaning-engine, with respect for what believers hold, and notes only the historical pattern: a movement out of a marginal region that rapidly built a shared law, script, and civilization.
--- question 1
How early were the surviving accounts of his life actually written down?

=== person/muhammad-ali-of-egypt
title: Muhammad Ali of Egypt
src: 96d418d2d70a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Muhammad Ali, an Ottoman officer of Albanian family, became governor of Egypt in 1805, broke the power of the old Mamluk elite, built a conscript army and a state-run economy, and founded the dynasty that ruled Egypt until 1952.
--- fact 1
Muhammad Ali was born in Kavala, in what is now Greece, to an Albanian family from the Korçë region; his father worked as a tobacco merchant and served as an Ottoman military commander.
--- fact 2
He rose to power after the French withdrawal from Egypt in 1801. In 1805 prominent Egyptians, including the religious scholars, demanded his appointment as governor, or wali, and the Ottomans accepted.
--- fact 3
In 1811 he invited the leading Mamluks to a celebration at the Cairo Citadel, where his forces ambushed and killed them; more Mamluks were then hunted down across Cairo and elsewhere, for a death toll of perhaps 3,000 in total.
--- fact 4
He introduced long-staple cotton, built an army of conscripts trained by European officers, and established a state-run industrial economy.
--- fact 5
He founded the dynasty that would rule Egypt until 1952.
--- context
Muhammad Ali governed Egypt as a nominal servant of the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] while pursuing reforms of his own, in the same period as the [[event/tanzimat-reforms|Tanzimat Reforms]] under way in Istanbul. His rule came before the building of the [[event/suez-canal|Suez Canal]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a reformer who began his rule by killing a rival elite at a banquet meant to look like a celebration, and who built a modern state on conscription and compulsory production. The order he made was real, and it lasted; the cost of it was carried by those who were drafted into his army, and by those who did not survive the killing at all.
--- question 1
How much of Egypt's later modernization can be credited to Muhammad Ali's programme, and at what cost to its rural population?
--- question 2
How many people, in total, were killed in the wave of violence that followed the Citadel massacre?
