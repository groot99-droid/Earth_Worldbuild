=== people--henry-ossawa-tanner--3
src: 2163a8197a03
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Henry Ossawa Tanner (21 June 1859 to 25 May 1937) was an American painter, the first African American artist to win fame across nations. Frustrated by racism at home, he settled in Paris in 1891 and built his career there, painting dignified genre scenes and biblical subjects that glow like lamps in a dim room.

He was born in Pittsburgh, eldest of nine children. His father, Benjamin Tucker Tanner, became a bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and his mother, Sarah, had escaped slavery by the Underground Railroad. At 13 he chose to become an artist.

In 1879 he enrolled at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts and studied with Thomas Eakins. He was the only Black student, and he faced harassment. In 1891 he left for Europe and studied at the Académie Julian in Paris.

On a return visit in 1893 he painted The Banjo Lesson, and in 1894 The Thankful Poor, both genre scenes of Black American life that challenged racist stereotypes.

Back in France he turned to biblical subjects. The Resurrection of Lazarus won a medal at the 1897 Salon, and the French government bought it. His patron Rodman Wanamaker paid for journeys to Egypt and Palestine.

He married the singer Jessie Olsson in 1899. In 1923 he was made a Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur, and he died in Paris.

=== people--alphonse-mucha--3

--- description
Alphonse Mucha (1860 to 1939) was a Moravian-born designer whose decorative posters for Sarah Bernhardt and commercial clients shaped the visual style of Art Nouveau, all tendril and halo. He resisted the label, and he saw his true work in a cycle of paintings on the history of the Slavic peoples.

He was born in Ivančice in Moravia, then part of the Austrian Empire, and worked as a theatrical scene painter before he went to Paris in 1887.

In December 1894 a Paris printing house needed a poster for Sarah Bernhardt's play Gismonda at short notice over the holidays, and turned to Mucha as the only designer to hand. Its success led Bernhardt to bind him to a six-year contract for posters, sets and costumes.

In 1902 he published Documents décoratifs, a pattern book of his ornamental style for other designers and craftsmen.

From about 1910 he gave much of his life to The Slav Epic, a cycle of twenty monumental canvases on Slavic history, which he gave to the city of Prague. It was placed on display at the National Gallery Prague's Trade Fair Palace in 2012.

His formula was elongated women within ornamental frames, in muted colour lithography on theatre posters, cigarette papers, bicycles and calendars, and it reached shops, cafés and middle-class homes across Europe like seed on the wind.
