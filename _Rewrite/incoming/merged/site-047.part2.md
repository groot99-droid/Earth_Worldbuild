=== person/eleanor-roosevelt
title: Eleanor Roosevelt
type: person
date: 1884 – 1962
themes: war, kinship
src: 66984b508e42
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Eleanor Roosevelt (1884 to 1962) served as First Lady of the United States from 1933 to 1945, and afterward as a diplomat who chaired the United Nations commission that drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.
--- fact 1
Born on 11 October 1884 in New York City, she married Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1905.
--- fact 2
As First Lady she redefined the role: holding her own press conferences, writing a daily newspaper column, and speaking for civil rights and the poor, sometimes publicly at odds with her husband's policies.
--- fact 3
After his death in 1945, President Truman named her a delegate to the new United Nations that December.
--- fact 4
She chaired the UN Commission on Human Rights, which drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the General Assembly on 10 December 1948 — the achievement she called her greatest.
--- fact 5
She later chaired a presidential commission on the status of women under President Kennedy.
--- fact 6
She died on 7 November 1962 in New York City.
--- context
Eleanor Roosevelt worked in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], through [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]] and into the early [[event/cold-war|Cold War]], the years when the United Nations came into being. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a document meant to bind every state to common claim-lines around a person's treatment was drafted under the chairmanship of a woman holding no formal power beyond her office, relying instead on the public standing she had built as a president's wife. The declaration functions as story-glue with no enforcement behind it, only the weight of near-unanimous agreement. A specialist could argue that later adoption of the Declaration commits signatory states to little in practice, which would weaken any reading of 1948 as a settled achievement rather than a beginning.
--- question 1
How much practical effect has the Declaration had on states that signed it?
--- question 2
How much of her influence came from her own record, and how much from the Roosevelt name?

=== person/emperor-meiji
title: Emperor Meiji
type: person
date: 1852 – 1912
themes: technology, kinship
src: 2765ccfa75ba
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Emperor Meiji (1852 to 1912) reigned as Emperor of Japan from 1867, and in his name the Meiji Restoration transformed Japan into a modern industrial state.
--- fact 1
He came to the throne in 1867 at about the age of fourteen.
--- fact 2
The Charter Oath, issued in his name in 1868, promised reform.
--- fact 3
His reign saw a new constitution in 1889, industrialization, and Japan's rise as a regional power.
--- fact 4
He died in 1912.
--- context
He stood as the symbol of the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]], which ended the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]].
--- observer
I see a boy on a throne, his real powers held and spent by advisers, and I see how his very youth lent legitimacy to a change that swept everything before it. I see continuity wearing a young face while revolution moves beneath it. What else could make an upheaval look like inheritance?
--- question 1
How much personal influence did the Emperor actually hold?

=== person/enheduanna
title: Enheduanna
type: person
date: c. 2285 – 2250 BCE
themes: religion, language, art
src: c5bd83dec33f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Enheduanna, flourishing around 2300 BCE (the span of about 2285 to 2250 BCE is only an estimate), served as high priestess of the moon god Nanna at the Sumerian city of Ur and was a daughter of Sargon of Akkad. She is often called the earliest author known by name, though her dates and the attribution of her texts remain debated.
--- fact 1
Enheduanna served as en-priestess of Nanna at Ur, appointed to the office by her father, Sargon of Akkad; her dates depend on the chronology used.
--- fact 2
Found at Ur in 1927 by Leonard Woolley's team, a disc shows a figure identified by inscription as Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon, in a ritual scene.
--- fact 3
Sumerian poems are attributed to her, including 42 temple hymns and hymns to the goddess Inanna, among them "The Exaltation of Inanna."
--- fact 4
The surviving copies come from schools of the Old Babylonian period, about 1800 BCE, some five to six centuries after her lifetime, and scholars disagree over whether she wrote every text attributed to her.
--- fact 5
In "The Exaltation of Inanna" the speaker describes being driven from office by a usurper, Lugal-ane, and then restored; whether this reflects real events is debated.
--- context
Enheduanna wrote in the Sumerian language of the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] while serving a ruler from the Akkadian-speaking north, in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]; her work belongs to the story of the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I see a conquering house place its own daughter at the head of a conquered city's oldest cult, binding a new claim to an old sacred order through blood. I see, in that same priestess, the first author the record gives a name to — one office, at once political and devoted.

But the words survive only in later copies, centuries removed, and I know the reading would falter if the name attached to them turned out to be a tradition rather than a fact. What is authorship, when it arrives to us already retold?
--- question 1
Which of the texts attributed to her can be tied to her own lifetime?

=== person/ezana-of-aksum
title: Ezana of Aksum
type: person
date: c. 320 – 360 CE
themes: religion, war, language
src: 8e63e0548c7f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ezana ruled Aksum, in present-day Ethiopia and Eritrea, from about 320 to about 360 CE, though sources differ on the exact dates. He is known for adopting Christianity and for a campaign in the Nile valley, both recorded in his inscriptions and coins.
--- fact 1
Ezana came to the throne as a child and ruled the Kingdom of Aksum from about 320 until about 360; dates for his life and reign remain approximate, and sources differ.
--- fact 2
His early inscriptions and coins show the older Aksumite religion, including a crescent and disc on his coins.
--- fact 3
Frumentius, a Christian from Tyre — described as Phoenician, and in some accounts Syrian — rose within the Aksumite court and is reported to have been consecrated Aksum's first bishop by Athanasius of Alexandria, traditionally in 328, though other accounts place it between 340 and 346.
--- fact 4
Ezana converted to Christianity in the 4th century, though the exact date is uncertain. His later inscriptions invoke a "Lord of Heaven," and his coins replace the crescent and disc with the cross, often described as among the earliest coins to do so.
--- fact 5
His inscriptions appear in Ge'ez, Sabaean and Greek.
--- fact 6
About 350 an Aksumite force campaigned in the Nile valley against Kush and neighboring peoples; the campaign is often linked to the end of Kush's power, though historians debate the causes.
--- context
Ezana ruled the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] and led campaigns against the [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]] in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a king's change of religion is visible in both stone and coin: the same ruler's inscriptions change which god they invoke, and his coinage changes which symbol it carries. A state's meaning-engine was switched from above and announced as a status-signal to the traders who carried those coins across the Red Sea world. The reading would weaken if evidence showed that most of his subjects kept the older practices for a long time after his conversion.
--- question 1
When did his conversion take place, and how quickly was it announced?

=== person/francisco-goya
title: Francisco Goya
type: person
date: 1746 – 1828
themes: art, war
src: 6af906c428fa
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Francisco Goya (30 March 1746 to 16 April 1828) worked as a Spanish court painter and printmaker, often called the last of the Old Masters and the first of the moderns. Illness, political repression and the Napoleonic invasion pushed him from brilliant royal portraits toward satire, violence and the irrational.
--- fact 1
Goya was born in Fuendetodos in Aragon and grew up in Zaragoza. In 1773 he married Josefa Bayeu, sister of the court painter Francisco Bayeu, who helped him win work designing tapestry cartoons in Madrid; by 1786 he was painter to the king, and by 1799 First Court Painter to Charles IV.
--- fact 2
An illness in 1792 to 1793 left him permanently deaf. In 1799 he published Los Caprichos, satirical etchings mocking superstition and the clergy.
--- fact 3
When Napoleon's armies invaded Spain in 1808, he witnessed atrocities on both sides and recorded them in The Disasters of War, prints not published until 35 years after his death. He painted The Second and Third of May 1808 in 1814.
--- fact 4
Disillusioned by the repressive rule of Ferdinand VII, he retreated to a house outside Madrid and covered its walls with the Black Paintings, including Saturn Devouring His Son, 1819 to 1823. In 1824 he went into exile in Bordeaux, where he died.
--- fact 5
His portraits, influenced by Velázquez, use loose brushwork and avoid flattery; he mastered aquatint for deep shadows, and later applied paint with knives, rags and fingers.
--- fact 6
Goya named Rembrandt as one of his three masters. Both Manet's Execution of Emperor Maximilian and Picasso's Guernica descend from The Third of May.
--- fact 7
The Prado holds the greatest collection of his work.
--- context
Goya worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] and lived through the [[event/napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], drawing on [[person/diego-velazquez|Diego Velázquez]] and [[person/rembrandt-van-rijn|Rembrandt van Rijn]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a court painter who recorded sanctioned harm from inside the very society that practised it, and who kept that record private: The Disasters of War stayed withheld for 35 years. The same hand produced unflattering royal portraits and images of witches and monsters, showing that the meaning-engine of the period, Church and crown, did not exhaust what one person could see. Later artists took his war images as a model, an inheritance chosen by those who wanted a precedent for witnessing.
--- question 1
Why did he withhold The Disasters of War, and what did he intend for the Black Paintings?

=== person/frank-lloyd-wright
title: Frank Lloyd Wright
type: person
date: 1867 – 1959
themes: art, technology
src: 86792ffed9e1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Frank Lloyd Wright (1867 to 1959) stands as the most influential American architect of the 20th century. Across seven decades he argued for "organic architecture" — buildings designed from the inside out, around their site, materials and users.
--- fact 1
Wright was born in Richland Center, Wisconsin, studied engineering briefly at the University of Wisconsin, and moved to Chicago, where he worked under Louis Sullivan, whom he later called his "Lieber Meister".
--- fact 2
He set up his own practice in Oak Park in the 1890s and developed the low, wide-eaved Prairie house style. His personal life ran turbulent: an affair, the killing of seven people at his Wisconsin home, Taliesin, in 1914, and bankruptcy during the Depression.
--- fact 3
In his late sixties he designed Fallingwater and the Johnson Wax headquarters, both 1936 to 1939, and finally the Guggenheim Museum, its design and construction spanning 1943 to 1959; he died in 1959, aged 91.
--- fact 4
He designed around open, flowing interior space, with art-glass bands and deep cantilevered overhangs, using concrete, steel and glass without disguising them as historical stonework; he designed most of the furniture, textiles and stained glass in his buildings himself.
--- fact 5
He designed more than a thousand structures, roughly half of them built. Fallingwater and several others now form a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
--- fact 6
Late in life he planned an unrealised city, Broadacre City, built around low-density, car-based suburban living; he was also a major collector and dealer of Hiroshige's prints.
--- context
Wright worked in [[region/north-america|North America]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], collecting the prints of [[person/utagawa-hiroshige|Utagawa Hiroshige]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I look at a man whose whole principle was that a building should grow from its site and its people, never from a borrowed style — and I watch his own name become a style anyway. I see his open plan everywhere now, standard in houses that never speak his name; an idea can spread that far and still go uncredited.

He never built Broadacre City, and yet the car-based suburb he imagined arrived without him, which suggests a vision can shape a world its author never gets to raise. What idea does not, in the end, outrun the hands that first held it?
--- question 1
How much of postwar suburban design can be traced to his ideas rather than to independent development?
