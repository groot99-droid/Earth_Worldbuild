=== people--cyrus-the-great--1
src: d0a6b42960c1
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Cyrus the Great founded the Achaemenid Persian Empire in the 6th century BCE, conquering the Medes, Lydia and Babylon one after another like rivers joining a single sea.

His birth date is uncertain; scholars give about 600 to 576 BCE.

Around 550 BCE he defeated the Median king Astyages, and around 547 BCE the Lydian king Croesus.

In 539 BCE he took Babylon.

The Cyrus Cylinder, a Babylonian clay cylinder found in 1879, carries his own account of the conquest and of his restoration of gods and deported peoples.

He died in 530 BCE campaigning on the eastern frontier, and he was laid to rest at Pasargadae.

=== people--cyrus-the-great--2
src: d0a6b42960c1
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Cyrus the Great founded the Achaemenid Persian Empire in the 6th century BCE by conquering the Medes, Lydia and Babylon. Think of it as an empire assembled in three moves.

Even his beginning is uncertain: scholars give his birth as about 600 to 576 BCE.

He defeated the Median king Astyages around 550 BCE and the Lydian king Croesus around 547 BCE. He took Babylon in 539 BCE.

What makes him memorable is how he explained it. The Cyrus Cylinder, a Babylonian clay cylinder found in 1879, records his account of the conquest and his restoration of gods and deported peoples. A conqueror who writes about restoring is drafting a covenant with the conquered.

He died in 530 BCE campaigning on the eastern frontier and was buried at Pasargadae.

=== people--dido--1
src: 4a7abdd64611
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Dido, called also Elissa, is the legendary founder-queen of Carthage, traditionally said to have led Phoenician settlers from Tyre across the sea to found the city on the North African coast around 814 BCE. Her birth and death dates are not historically fixed.

The earliest known telling of her founding of Carthage comes from the Greek historian Timaeus of Tauromenium, writing in the 4th to 3rd century BCE, centuries after the traditional founding date.

Tradition holds that she fled Tyre after her brother, King Pygmalion, murdered her husband to seize his wealth, and that she later took her own life rather than marry a local Libyan king, Iarbas, who threatened war.

No Phoenician inscription or contemporary record names a founder called Dido or Elissa. Evidence of Phoenician settlement at the site of Carthage has so far been found only from the last quarter of the 8th century BCE, about a century or more after the traditional founding date, though it may still fit a folk memory of an organized colonizing expedition, a memory carried like salt on the wind.

Her best-known appearance, as the doomed lover of the Trojan hero Aeneas in Virgil's Aeneid, is a literary invention: the Trojan War's traditional date lies centuries before Carthage's traditional founding, so the two could never have met.

=== people--laozi--1
src: 71ed9b144247
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Laozi (Lao Tzu, "Old Master") is the traditional author of the Daodejing and a founding figure of Daoism, said to have lived in the 6th century BCE. Whether he was a single historical person is disputed; he comes to us like mist on a mountain road.

Tradition places him in the 6th century BCE, in the time of Confucius. The date is uncertain, and no date of death is known.

The historian Sima Qian, writing about 100 BCE, names him Li Er, keeper of archives at the Zhou court, and also lists other men who might have been meant, so the uncertainty was already old.

In Sima Qian's account he met Confucius, then left the Zhou domain for the west and wrote the text at the Hangu Pass at the request of the gatekeeper Yin Xi. This account is traditional.

The Daodejing holds about 5,000 Chinese characters in 81 short chapters. Its earliest excavated versions, from Guodian (about 300 BCE) and Mawangdui (tomb sealed 168 BCE), fit the view of most scholars that the text took shape in the 4th to 3rd centuries BCE.

Later religious Daoism honored him as a deity, Laojun, and the Tang dynasty's imperial family, named Li, claimed him as an ancestor.

=== people--siddhartha-gautama--1
src: 1886893021d8
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Siddhartha Gautama, known as the Buddha, was a spiritual teacher in northern India whose teachings founded Buddhism. Traditional dates are about 563 to 483 BCE, but modern scholarship suggests a somewhat later range.

Buddhist tradition holds that he left a princely life and attained awakening at Bodh Gaya. Notice the shape of that story: a threshold crossed, a comfort set down.

His teachings include the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path, a whole architecture of practice.

The exact dates of his life are debated among scholars.

Buddhism later spread across Asia, carried by monks and along trade routes. Ideas travel as far as the people who carry them.

=== people--tomyris--1
src: f1babb9f044c
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Tomyris was a queen of the Massagetae, a nomadic people east of the Caspian Sea, who according to the Greek historian Herodotus defeated and killed Cyrus the Great of Persia in 530 BCE. She is known only from Herodotus, and other ancient accounts of Cyrus's death differ.

She appears only in Book 1 of Herodotus's Histories, written about a century after the events. Her dates are unknown; the date given here is that of the battle in his account.

Herodotus says she ruled the Massagetae, who dwelt east of the Caspian Sea near the Araxes River, and that she refused Cyrus's offer of marriage, which he saw as a road to her kingdom.

In his account the Persians took her son Spargapises by trickery, and he killed himself in captivity. Then Tomyris defeated the Persian army in battle.

Herodotus says she had Cyrus's body found and his head put in a wineskin filled with blood.

Other ancient accounts run another way: Xenophon has Cyrus die peacefully in his palace, and Ctesias has him die of a wound in a war on the eastern frontier that the Persians won.
