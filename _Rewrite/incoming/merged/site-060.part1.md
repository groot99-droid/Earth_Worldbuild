=== observer-note/frontiers-of-extraction
title: Frontiers of Extraction
src: e5f5aff4505b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
What happens at the edge of a world economy when a distant demand meets a local resource?
--- observer
Call this the architecture of a frontier of extraction: a place where a resource is drawn out for a market that lies far away, often under coercion. The commodity shifts (fur, sugar, silver, rubber) but the structure holds: distant demand, local labor, and a tribute or quota that binds the two. The frontier migrates as one resource runs out or another is craved. The Observer notes that the very exchange-webs that delivered benefit to consumers could lay very large costs on people who never saw the markets they served.
--- extra 1: Question
What happens at the edge of a world economy when a distant demand meets a local resource?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- In Siberia, European demand for luxury furs pushed the Russian advance toward the Pacific by 1639, and Indigenous peoples paid a tribute, the yasak, in fur; see [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] and [[culture/yakut-sakha|Yakut (Sakha)]].
- In the Caribbean and the Atlantic, demand for sugar drove the forced migration of millions of Africans; see [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]].
- In China, American silver, reaching the Ming by way of the Manila trade, became a major part of the economy; see [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] and [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
- In the Congo, demand for rubber and ivory drove forced labor and mass deaths under a private ruler; see [[event/congo-free-state|Congo Free State]].
- In each case a small group backed by a distant power ran the frontier, and the local population carried the heavy cost.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
These cases differ greatly in scale, in their forms of coercion, and in the roles played by local elites. To treat them as a single pattern risks losing sight of those differences, and it can also hide the agency of the people who lived on the frontiers.

=== observer-note/sanctioned-harm
title: Sanctioned Harm
src: 269c6ecef1c0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Why does a species with strong inhibitions against harming its own kind organize killing on a mass scale?
--- observer
Call this sanctioned harm: violence a group approves through its own story-glue. The same person who would not kill a stranger may do exactly that under a uniform, a flag, or a doctrine. The Observer notes that the very story-glue which builds cooperation can just as easily be turned against others. It notes, with equal seriousness, the counter-tradition: laws of war, pacifist movements, and the practice of [[person/mahatma-gandhi|Mahatma Gandhi]]'s nonviolence.
--- extra 1: Question
Why does a species with strong inhibitions against harming its own kind organize killing on a mass scale?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Evidence of organized violence reaches back into prehistory, but its scale grew with settled societies and states.
- Empires such as the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] and the [[culture/romans|Romans]] wielded organized force at large scale.
- Industrial states scaled that harm still further in [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]].
- The Holocaust shows that organized killing can be run as a bureaucratic process.
- The [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]] brought weapons that could end a civilization.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The Observer records this harm without endorsing it. This essay does not try to explain responsibility away: individuals and institutions made real choices, and those choices could have gone otherwise.
