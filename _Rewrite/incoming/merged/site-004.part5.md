=== species/stromatolites
src: 39d75d10baab
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Stromatolites are layered rock structures raised by communities of microbes that trap sediment and precipitate minerals, books of stone written one thin page at a time; the oldest widely cited examples are about 3.48 billion years old.
--- fact 1
Stromatolites form when microbial mats, often including cyanobacteria, bind sediment into thin repeating layers, like the rings of a tree.
--- fact 2
The oldest widely cited examples lie in the Dresser Formation of the Pilbara region of Western Australia, dated to about 3.48 billion years; some researchers dispute that they are biological at all, and the stone keeps its silence.
--- fact 3
Living stromatolites still grow today in a few places, among them Shark Bay in Western Australia, where the ancient work goes on.
--- fact 4
Stromatolites were abundant in shallow seas for much of the Precambrian, crowding the sunlit margins like cities of stone.
--- fact 5
They declined later, and that waning is often attributed to grazing animals, though other causes have been proposed.
--- context
Stromatolites are the main fossil record of early microbial life, a bridge of stone from the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]] to the oxygen-producing [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]] that drove the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]]. They sit in the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and their thread runs through the theme of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
We think of monuments as the work of minds. The Observer notes that for more than three billion years the only visible monuments on Earth were built by organisms without brains. Think of a shoreline lined with stone mounds, raised grain by grain with no plan and no builder's eye. Later monument-builders, the species itself among them, are a very recent development in that longer record. Architecture is older than intention.
--- question 1
Are the oldest structures truly biological, or only stone that looks like life?
--- question 2
When did stromatolite-building microbes first make oxygen, their first breath into the old air?

=== species/tiktaalik
src: fc252d339feb
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tiktaalik is a 375-million-year-old fossil fish with limb-like fins and a flexible neck, a transitional form standing at the water's edge between fishes and four-limbed animals.
--- fact 1
It was discovered in 2004 on Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic by Neil Shubin, Edward Daeschler and Farish Jenkins, drawn out of the northern cold.
--- fact 2
It had a flat head crowned with eyes on top, and fins that held wrist-like bones.
--- fact 3
It lived in shallow freshwater habitats in the Late Devonian, in the light-shot shallows of an old world.
--- fact 4
It is a well-known example of a predicted transitional fossil, a find foretold by reasoning before the rock gave it up.
--- context
Tiktaalik illustrates the vertebrate move onto land told in [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]], the long crossing from water to shore.
--- observer
Look at your own hand. The Observer notes that the bones of the human hand trace back to the fins of animals like this. The species' body carries its history within it. Think of a house whose oldest beams are still inside the walls, holding them up. You are an archive you did not write.
--- question 1
How closely does Tiktaalik resemble the true, direct ancestor of tetrapods?

=== species/trilobites
src: 6ec359150f85
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Trilobites were marine arthropods armored in a hard, segmented exoskeleton, and they thrived for about 270 million years, from the early Cambrian until the end-Permian extinction closed their long reign.
--- fact 1
More than 20,000 species have been described, a great host kept in stone.
--- fact 2
Their compound eyes had lenses made of calcite, a mineral: they looked out upon the sea through stone.
--- fact 3
They are widely used to date and correlate Paleozoic rocks, clocks and signposts pressed into the strata.
--- fact 4
The last trilobites died out in the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]], and the sea forgot them.
--- context
Trilobites are a signature animal of the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]] and of the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], their shape stamped on that age like a seal on wax.
--- observer
We tend to trust whatever has lasted. The Observer notes that a body plan can succeed for hundreds of millions of years and still disappear in a single crisis. Think of a great house that stood through every storm and fell in one night. Duration is no guarantee. Survival so far is not a promise.
--- question 1
Why did trilobites wane before the final extinction?

=== species/woolly-mammoth
src: cea3c5979418
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The woolly mammoth was a cold-adapted elephant of the northern Ice Age steppe, a shaggy lord of the frozen grasslands, which survived on Wrangel Island until about 4,000 years ago, long after its kin had gone.
--- fact 1
It had long curved tusks, a shaggy coat and a thick layer of fat, armor against the wind.
--- fact 2
Most populations died out around the end of the last ice age, about 11,000 years ago, and the steppe lost its giants.
--- fact 3
Humans hunted mammoths, and from their bones and tusks they made shelters and art.
--- fact 4
Ancient DNA has been recovered from frozen remains, a voice kept in the ice.
--- context
Woolly mammoths are a symbol of the Ice Age of the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]], and they walk across Paleolithic art; see [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]].
--- observer
I want one answer. I want the spear, or I want the thaw. I read the debate about the role of human hunting against climate change in the megafauna extinctions, and neither side will let the other go. Why do I need a single hand to blame? Both may have mattered. Grief rarely has one cause.
--- question 1
What was the relative role of hunting and climate, the spear and the changing sky?
