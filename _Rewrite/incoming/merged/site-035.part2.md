=== species/barley
src: caa00fe59033
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Barley is a cereal grass domesticated in the Fertile Crescent from around 9000 BCE, one of the earliest crops humans ever farmed. In ancient Mesopotamia it was a staple that served as food, as drink and even as a standard of value, a grain that filled both the bowl and the scale.
--- fact 1
Barley was domesticated from wild barley in the Fertile Crescent, and early domesticated forms are recorded in the Jarmo region of modern Iraq from about 9000 to 7000 BCE; yet grinding stones at Ohalo II, near the Sea of Galilee, keep starch traces of wild barley, showing it was eaten well before domestication, by about 23,000 BCE, while the grain still grew untamed.
--- fact 2
Domestication gave the plant grain heads that hold together instead of shattering, making it far easier to harvest but binding its fate to deliberate replanting, to the hand that sows it.
--- fact 3
Barley was likely among the first grains brewed into beer, one of the earliest alcoholic drinks that Neolithic farmers drew from the field.
--- fact 4
In Mesopotamia barley moved like coin, a de facto currency, with the shekel functioning as a standard unit of its weight; cuneiform records from around 2350 BCE, under the ruler Urukagina, describe monthly barley rations issued to adults.
--- fact 5
By about 2000 BCE barley had spread across Eurasia like a slow golden tide, reaching Finland by about 4200 BCE, the Indus civilization region between roughly 5,700 and 3,300 years ago, and the Korean peninsula by about 1500 to 850 BCE.
--- fact 6
In 2023 the world's fields yielded about 146 million tonnes of barley, the fourth largest cereal harvest after maize, rice and wheat; about 70 percent goes to feed animals and about 30 percent to malting, for beer and whisky.
--- context
Barley was domesticated beside [[species/wheat|Wheat]] as one of the founding crops of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent, and it underwrote the economies of early Mesopotamian cities such as [[place/ur|Ur]] and, later, [[place/babylon|Babylon]], the grain beneath the walls. It belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and its threads run through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I weigh a handful of barley and cannot tell whether I hold supper or wages. Here one grain was both food and a unit of account, two functions that many later societies kept separate, food and money. Here the rationing records turn barley into a record of a kin-lattice's obligations: who was owed what, and by whom.

And then the brewing, one of the oldest recorded transformations of a raw crop into something that mattered socially, tying the field to gathering and to celebration. Is there an older way to say that we belong to each other?

But if barley's early monetary role proves more local and limited than the shekel-based accounts suggest, this reading weakens.

Hunger keeps the oldest ledger.
--- question 1
How standard was barley's role as a unit of value from one Mesopotamian city and period to the next?
--- question 2
How soon after domestication did brewing become widespread, rather than barley being eaten mainly as porridge or bread?

=== species/cacao
src: a9f39d96f777
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Cacao, the source of chocolate, was first domesticated in the Amazon basin of South America by at least 3300 BCE. Follow the bean and you trace a whole lineage: a ritual drink and a currency in Mesoamerica, then a cargo that Spanish traders carried to Europe in the 16th century.
--- fact 1
The threshold of this story lies in the south: cacao's native range is the Amazon rainforest, and genetic and archaeological evidence places its earliest domestication at the Santa Ana-La Florida site in present-day Ecuador, by the Mayo-Chinchipe culture, at least 5,300 years ago, before it reached Mesoamerica.
--- fact 2
Chemical residue on pottery from Puerto Escondido, Honduras, shows that cacao products were consumed there between about 1500 and 1400 BCE, and the sweet pulp around the beans was likely valued before the beans themselves.
--- fact 3
Here money and sacrament shared one grammar: across Mesoamerica before the Spanish conquest, cacao beans served as a currency for everyday purchases, and Olmec and Maya elites drank cacao in spiritual ceremonies; a Spanish chronicler recorded that the Aztec ruler Moctezuma II drank only chocolate, reportedly consuming dozens of servings a day from a golden goblet.
--- fact 4
Spanish traders introduced chocolate to Europe, where it became a popular drink by the mid-17th century; the Spanish also spread cacao cultivation to their colonies in the West Indies and the Philippines.
--- fact 5
The ledger turns grave here: Ivory Coast and Ghana together grow most of the world's cacao today, and reporting on the industry has documented large-scale child labor, with an estimated 2.1 million children involved in cocoa harvesting in the two countries as of 2017, often exposed to pesticides and hazardous tasks; a 2021 lawsuit against major chocolate companies over forced child labor was dismissed for lack of a traceable link to specific plantations.
--- fact 6
Global cacao production reached about 5.8 million tonnes in 2022, and Ivory Coast alone supplied about 38 percent of it.
--- context
Place cacao in its lineage and you find it at the centre of the ritual and economic life of the [[culture/olmecs|Olmecs]], the [[culture/maya|Maya]] and the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] before Spanish contact; its spread to Europe and other continents is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I remember that this bean left a South American forest. I remember that in Mesoamerica it became a meaning-engine, money and medicine and a marker of elite status all at once. I remember that Europe took it, stripped away most of that ritual weight, and remade it as a sweet.

What did the sweetness cost? Today's cacao economy repeats an old pattern in a new form: a crop grown mainly by smallholders in West Africa supplies a global confectionery industry. The labor of children within that supply chain I record here as harm, whatever the difficulty of tracing it to a specific buyer.

If cacao's currency role in Mesoamerica proves more limited or local than the general accounts suggest, this reading weakens.

A sweetness that forgets whose hands it passed through is not innocent.
--- question 1
How did cacao travel from its South American origin to the centre of Mesoamerican culture, by what route, and over what timescale?
--- question 2
How effective have industry pledges and lawsuits been at reducing child labor in West African cacao production?
