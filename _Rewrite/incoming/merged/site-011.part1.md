=== culture/srivijaya
src: 6a119d16d426
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Srivijaya was a Buddhist maritime kingdom seated at Palembang on Sumatra, and from the 7th to the 13th century it held the Strait of Malacca and its trade the way a hand holds a rope.
--- fact 1
In 671 the Chinese monk Yijing stayed in Srivijaya, and his report of the Buddhist study there remains like a lantern left burning on a harbor wall.
--- fact 2
The Kedukan Bukit inscription, the oldest known Srivijayan inscription, dates to 683: the first known line in the kingdom's long ledger of the sea.
--- fact 3
Its grip closed on two throats of water, the Strait of Malacca and the Sunda Strait, and on the shipping that passed through them.
--- fact 4
Around 1025 a naval expedition of the Chola king Rajendra I struck Srivijaya, a storm that arrived under sail.
--- fact 5
In the 13th and 14th centuries its power ebbed like a tide going out, and Java-based powers absorbed it.
--- context
Srivijaya was a hub of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]], a knot tied between China and India. It stood as a Buddhist counterpart to the Hindu-Buddhist world of [[place/angkor|Angkor]] and [[place/borobudur|Borobudur]], and it clashed with the [[culture/chola-dynasty|Chola Dynasty]]. In the chronicle it belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and to the region of [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and its thread runs through the themes of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I look for the kingdom and find no wide country. I find a strait. I find port chiefs and their loyalty, and I find that it was enough. Who needs the land when you hold the door? Power over movement can outlast power over land, until someone builds a faster route.
--- question 1
How centralized was Srivijaya's rule, and how tightly did its center hold its port chiefs?

=== culture/sumerians
src: 114e8e245f93
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Sumerians were the people of southern Mesopotamia who built some of the world's first cities and developed cuneiform, the writing that first taught clay to remember.
--- fact 1
Their city-states stood like lamps along a long shore, Uruk, Ur, and Lagash among them.
--- fact 2
They pressed cuneiform script into clay tablets, and it served administration, literature, and law alike.
--- fact 3
They raised temple towers called ziggurats, mountains made by human hands.
--- fact 4
The Sumerian language stands alone, unrelated to any known living language family: a voice without kin.
--- context
The Sumerians stand at the heart of the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] and [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]]. Their story runs on at [[place/uruk|Uruk]] and across [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]].
--- observer
I think of a people whose language left no living heir. I think of their tablets, their ledgers, their laws. And I think of what the world still holds from them: the technology of writing. What does a culture owe to its own survival? Less than I believed. A culture can leave an inheritance and not survive to see it spent.
--- question 1
Where did the Sumerians come from, this people whose language has no kin?

=== culture/swahili-coast-city-states
src: de1021da9074
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Swahili city-states were trading towns strung along the East African coast like beads on a cord, Kilwa, Mombasa, and Lamu among them, and they flourished from about the 9th to the 15th century CE.
--- fact 1
They spoke Swahili, a Bantu language rich with Arabic loanwords, two currents running in one channel.
--- fact 2
Coral-stone buildings and mosques were the mark of the towns, walls raised from the reef's own bones.
--- fact 3
Kilwa minted its own coins and sent out gold from the interior; see [[place/great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]].
--- fact 4
The traveler Ibn Battuta visited Kilwa in 1331; in 1505 the Portuguese attacks began.
--- context
These towns were knots in the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]], and they grew from the long roots of the [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]]. See also [[person/ibn-battuta|Ibn Battuta]].
--- observer
I see a culture made by mixing. I see African traders, Arab traders, and others, and I see families, and out of them one shared language and one urban life. Who says a people must grow from a single root? Some are born where the tides meet.
--- question 1
How much of Swahili civilization was of local African origin, and how much was Arab or Persian?

=== culture/taino
src: 8c62bec1c543
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Taino were an Arawakan-speaking people of the Greater Antilles and the Bahamas, and in 1492 they became the first Indigenous Americans encountered by Columbus.
--- fact 1
They lived in villages under chiefs called caciques, and their fields gave cassava, maize, and sweet potato.
--- fact 2
They carved zemis, figures of wood and stone associated with ancestors and spirits: the dead and the unseen given a shape.
--- fact 3
Words still spoken in English came from Taino, canoe, hammock, hurricane, and barbecue among them.
--- fact 4
After 1492 their populations fell sharply, through disease, forced labor, and violence.
--- fact 5
Genetic studies show Indigenous Caribbean ancestry surviving in present-day Caribbean populations, and the older idea that the Taino vanished does not hold.
--- context
The Taino were the first to feel the impact of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. The plantation economy that followed is told in [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. The Taino belong to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region of the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and their thread runs through the themes of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I say canoe. I say hammock. I say hurricane, and I hardly stop to ask whose words these are. They come from a language whose speakers were largely destroyed within a few decades. How did the words get so far? The words traveled as far as the people did not.
--- question 1
How many Taino were living in 1492?
