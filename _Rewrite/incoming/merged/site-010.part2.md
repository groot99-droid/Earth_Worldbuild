=== culture/kushan-empire
title: Kushan Empire
src: 2bd19d031f6a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Kushan Empire, from about 30 to 375 CE, was a Central Asian empire that spanned Bactria and northern India like a bridge of kings, holding key Silk Road routes in its hands and patronizing Buddhism.
--- fact 1
It grew out of the Yuezhi confederation, and around 30 CE Kujula Kadphises gathered its strands into a single cord.
--- fact 2
Kanishka, whose reign dates are uncertain, is remembered as a patron of Buddhism, a name that shines brighter than its own calendar.
--- fact 3
Gandhara art, where Greek and Indian styles flowed together like meeting rivers, produced some of the earliest images of the Buddha in human form.
--- fact 4
Kushan coins carried deities from Greek, Iranian, and Indian traditions, a small bright pantheon in every palm.
--- fact 5
Through its markets the empire joined Rome, Parthia, India, and Han China, a knot tied in the middle of the world's roads.
--- context
The Kushans held the crossroads of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]] and carried the traditions of [[person/siddhartha-gautama|Siddhartha Gautama]] eastward, as wind carries seed. Their rule lay where the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and Indian states met. In the chronicle's codex, the Kushan Empire belongs to [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I hold a Kushan coin in my mind and count its gods: three traditions, side by side. As though a state could be a shared account among many. As though a coin could be a small treaty. Did the merchants need that translation, or did the rulers? It appears that trade across cultures requires this kind of translation in its symbols. What passes from hand to hand must speak to every hand.
--- question 1
In what years did Kanishka truly reign?

=== culture/lapita-culture
title: Lapita Culture
src: b30e77a00bfd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Lapita culture was a seafaring society that appeared in the Bismarck Archipelago around 1600 BCE and spread rapidly east through the western Pacific like light running along a wave, and its people were ancestors of the Polynesians.
--- fact 1
Lapita is known by its pottery, pressed with fine stamped geometric patterns like the tracks of a careful tide.
--- fact 2
By roughly 1000 to 800 BCE their sites had scattered like thrown seed from the Bismarck Archipelago to Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa.
--- fact 3
Lapita people carried obsidian, black glass of the fire-mountains, and other goods over long distances.
--- fact 4
Ancient DNA from the Teouma cemetery in Vanuatu shows the first settlers there carried ancestry related to East Asians; later, like a newer tide washing over an older one, Papuan ancestry became dominant in Vanuatu.
--- fact 5
In later centuries Lapita descendants went on to settle Polynesia, the children following the long wake of their forebears.
--- context
The Lapita were the Pacific arm of the [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]], an outstretched hand of that great reaching, and the direct ancestors of the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]]. Their voyages laid the keel for the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. In the chronicle's codex, Lapita Culture belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I know them almost entirely by their pots. I know them by stamped clay, by patterns pressed with care. And yet the pots record boats: a sailing tradition that crossed hundreds of kilometers of open ocean. Where are the hulls now, and the hands that sailed them? Gone to water. The material that survives tells the story of what did not.
--- question 1
What drove the rapid push into islands where no human foot had yet stood?

=== culture/majapahit
title: Majapahit
src: a4e315dc79c2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Majapahit was a Hindu-Buddhist empire based in eastern Java, founded in 1293 in the wake of the Yuan dynasty's failed invasion of Java, and in the 14th century it stood dominant over the Indonesian archipelago like a lamp over scattered water.
--- fact 1
Raden Wijaya founded the kingdom in 1293: he helped Mongol forces defeat a rival, then turned on those allies as a tide turns.
--- fact 2
In the mid-14th century it climbed to its height under Hayam Wuruk, with his minister Gajah Mada at his side.
--- fact 3
The court poem Nagarakretagama, written in 1365, unrolls a long list of regions it names as dependencies.
--- fact 4
How far its actual control extended is debated, a shoreline blurred by mist.
--- fact 5
Its power ebbed in the 15th and 16th centuries as Islamic port-sultanates rose like a new tide.
--- context
After [[culture/srivijaya|Srivijaya]], Majapahit took up the mantle as the main power of the archipelago, and it threw back the naval expedition of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]]. It drank deep from the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]]. In the chronicle's codex, Majapahit belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that modern Indonesia invokes Majapahit as a precedent for an archipelagic state, though the empire's actual reach is uncertain. Consider what that means. A nation wants a lineage, and so it reaches into the past for an architecture that resembles its own. States often choose their ancestors and then enlarge them. Ask of every founding story: who is holding the magnifying glass?
--- question 1
How much of the archipelago lay truly in Majapahit's hand?

=== culture/mal-ta-and-the-ancient-north-eurasians
title: Mal'ta and the Ancient North Eurasians
src: e757577e01fe
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Mal'ta boy, a child who lived near Lake Baikal about 24,000 years ago, carries a genome that revealed a previously unknown population, the 'Ancient North Eurasians', who contributed to the ancestry of both Europeans and Native Americans. Think of it as a single page that rewrote a whole genealogy.
--- fact 1
Consider the long pause in this ledger: the remains were excavated at Mal'ta in Siberia in the 1920s, and their genome was sequenced in 2013.
--- fact 2
At that moment it was the oldest modern human genome ever published, the deepest threshold yet crossed.
--- fact 3
Different estimates place the share differently: between about 14 and 38 percent of the boy's genome is held in common with present-day Native Americans.
--- fact 4
The result showed that Native American ancestry grows from two roots, East Asian and Ancient North Eurasian: a genealogy braided from both.
--- context
The finding redrew the architecture of the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]], a migration that occurred around the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. See [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]].
--- observer
I think of a single child's bones, buried with ornaments. I think of the hands that laid them there. I think of how they spoke across 24,000 years to a question about the far side of the world. Who could have guessed a child would answer? The past keeps its answers in unexpected places.
--- question 1
By what paths did Ancient North Eurasian ancestry spread across continents?
