=== culture/judaism
title: Judaism
src: 05829dcf0cf3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Judaism is the religion and communal tradition of the Jewish people. It traces its origins to the patriarch Abraham and to the covenant given to Moses at Sinai, and for most of its history it has been organized around a body of law and commentary rather than a central authority. That is a distinctive architecture: a tradition whose center is a long conversation rather than a throne.
--- fact 1
Tradition dates Abraham, regarded as the first Hebrew and the forefather of the Jewish people, to about 2000 to 1800 BCE, though this is traditional rather than documented history, a distinction worth holding carefully.
--- fact 2
Its founding text is the Torah, the five books attributed to Moses, later joined by other scriptures collected as the Hebrew Bible: the codex at the center of the covenant.
--- fact 3
Rome destroyed the Second Temple in Jerusalem in 70 CE. Without a central temple, rabbis took up the work of interpreting the Torah, and their extensive commentary was later compiled as the Talmud.
--- fact 4
Today's main movements, Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and Reconstructionist, range from traditional to progressive in how they apply Jewish law, a shared inheritance read in different registers.
--- fact 5
Worldwide adherents numbered about 15 million as of the early 2020s, most of them living in Israel and the United States.
--- context
Judaism's traditional origins lie in the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], among the empires of ancient Mesopotamia such as the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]]; its history includes conquest by the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] and, later, the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] in the same region. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I think of a single building, destroyed in 70 CE. I think of how its loss turned an entire tradition's meaning-engine from temple sacrifice toward text and interpretation. I think of that shift in memory-outsourcing, from the altar to the page, and of a community that could persist without a capital. What do you keep when the center is gone? What you can carry.

Adherents hold the tradition's core claims as historical and covenantal. A specialist could argue that its earliest patriarchal accounts describe an ancestor-weight built up over centuries of retelling rather than a documented individual, which would weaken any confident dating of its earliest layer. I hold both of those sentences in the same hand. What is carried cannot be torn down.
--- question 1
How much of the patriarchal narrative reflects documented history, and how much later tradition?
--- question 2
How did the loss of the Temple reshape the tradition's practice over the centuries that followed?

=== culture/kalinago
title: Kalinago
src: ecf888140019
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Kalinago, called Caribs by Europeans, are an Indigenous people of the Lesser Antilles who resisted colonization for nearly two centuries after Columbus, and today they live mainly in the Kalinago Territory on Dominica. Theirs is a lesson in endurance, and in the difference between a name given by others and a name kept.
--- fact 1
Begin before the ships: the Kalinago descend from earlier inhabitants of the Lesser Antilles and were already there when Columbus reached the region in 1493.
--- fact 2
They earned a reputation as effective resistance fighters, and their raids helped keep Europeans from permanent settlement on some islands for close to two centuries.
--- fact 3
Terrain can be a kind of sovereignty: Dominica's mountainous volcanic interior gave them ground colonizers could not easily penetrate, and the island became a refuge for other Indigenous people fleeing colonization.
--- fact 4
France claimed Dominica in 1635, but Kalinago resistance blocked permanent settlement for a time. Catholic missionaries who arrived in 1642 recorded Kalinago language and life, a ledger kept by outsiders.
--- fact 5
Kalinago raids on plantations on neighboring islands continued into the eighteenth century, and their descendants remain in the Kalinago Territory today.
--- context
The Kalinago lived in the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]] into the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], sharing the region with the [[culture/taino|Taino]] and resisting the disruptions of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] longer than most of their neighbors. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that difficult terrain, more than any single tactic, let one Caribbean people hold off colonization for two centuries after most of the region had fallen, and then drew refugees from neighboring islands, turning a stronghold into a shared last refuge. Think of a house on high ground in a flood: its walls matter, but so does the hill. Their presence today contradicts accounts that treat the Caribbean's Indigenous peoples as vanished, and we should let that correction stand plainly. A specialist could argue that European rivalries left gaps in colonization as much as Kalinago strength did, which would weaken a reading that credits resistance alone. Survival rarely has a single cause; it is an architecture of ground, people and circumstance.
--- question 1
How much of the Kalinago's long resistance reflected their own strength, and how much European powers that were divided or distracted?
--- question 2
How much pre-contact Kalinago culture survives on Dominica today, and how much is later adaptation?
