=== person/kamehameha-i
src: c8cc2ae63f6f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kamehameha I, chief of Hawaiʻi Island born about 1750, united the Hawaiian Islands under one rule by 1810 and so founded the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi, whose last monarch would be Liliuokalani, dying in 1819.
--- fact 1
Kamehameha was born at Kohala on Hawaiʻi Island; estimates of his birth year range from 1736 to 1761. He died on 8 May 1819, though some sources give 14 May.
--- fact 2
After his uncle, the ruler Kalaniʻōpuʻu, died in 1782, he became guardian of the war god Kūkaʻilimoku, defeated his cousin Kīwalaʻō at the Battle of Mokuʻōhai, and gained Hawaiʻi Island after years of war.
--- fact 3
He made use of Western firearms and cannon and of advisers such as John Young and Isaac Davis. In 1795 he invaded Oʻahu, and at Nuʻuanu hundreds of defenders were driven over the Pali cliff.
--- fact 4
In 1810 Kauaʻi and Niʻihau came under his authority through negotiation with their ruler Kaumualiʻi, who remained in place as a tributary king.
--- fact 5
The Law of the Splintered Paddle, or Māmalahoe Kānāwai, which protected noncombatants, is said to come from an incident in 1782 in which fishermen struck him with a paddle.
--- fact 6
His son Liholiho succeeded him as Kamehameha II, and his burial place, hidden by custom, remains unknown.
--- context
Kamehameha I lived in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] among the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]], whose voyagers had settled Hawaiʻi centuries before, in the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. The kingdom he founded came to an end with [[person/liliuokalani|Liliuokalani]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a chief built one state by joining an old priestly and war lineage to imported gunpowder, and that he chose tribute over killing the last rival ruler. His hidden grave served as a grief-rite that also kept his bones from rivals. Here the caveat matters: a specialist could argue the unification also depended on Western trade and on divisions among his opponents, which would weaken any reading of the outcome as his design alone.
--- question 1
When was he actually born, and how might his age have shaped his own accounts of his rise?
--- question 2
How much of the 1810 unification came through negotiation, and how much through the threat of force?

=== person/kate-sheppard
src: 178b66e288f7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kate Sheppard (1848 to 1934), an English-born campaigner, led the movement for women's suffrage in New Zealand, and the Electoral Act of 1893 made New Zealand the first self-governing country in which women voted in national elections.
--- fact 1
Sheppard was born on 10 March 1848 in Liverpool, England, and emigrated to New Zealand, arriving in 1869; she died in Christchurch on 13 July 1934.
--- fact 2
From 1887 she served as national superintendent for franchise and legislation of the Women's Christian Temperance Union, which made votes for women one of its central aims.
--- fact 3
Petitions brought to Parliament carried about 10,000 signatures in 1891, about 20,000 in 1892 and 31,872 in 1893, the largest petition presented to Parliament up to that time.
--- fact 4
The Governor gave assent to the Electoral Act on 19 September 1893, and women cast their first votes in the election of 28 November 1893.
--- fact 5
The Act gave the vote to women of all races, including Māori women in the Māori electorates, though women could not stand for Parliament until 1919.
--- fact 6
Her portrait has appeared on the New Zealand ten-dollar note since 1991.
--- context
Kate Sheppard did her work in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], part of the wider international [[event/women-s-suffrage-movements|Women's Suffrage Movements]]. See [[culture/maori|Maori]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I watch a line move — the line around who counts as a citizen — and I see it moved by counting: a temperance network turning shared conviction into signatures a legislature could tally, name after name after name.

It moved in steps, not all at once. The vote came in 1893; the right to stand waited until 1919. Was it one woman's doing, or many hands working toward the same end? A specialist could argue the vote came from many organizers and sympathetic politicians besides her — and if so, no single life should carry the whole story.
--- question 1
How much of the 1893 result belongs to her leadership, and how much to other organizers?
--- question 2
In what ways did Māori women take part in the campaign?

=== person/katsushika-hokusai
src: 502d3d0ec86b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Katsushika Hokusai (1760 to 1849) was a Japanese ukiyo-e artist from Edo and the creator of The Great Wave off Kanagawa; he produced paintings and book illustrations, and, in his seventies, the landscape print series that made him world-famous.
--- fact 1
Hokusai was born in 1760 in the Katsushika district of Edo, modern Tokyo. As a teenager he worked in a lending library and trained as a woodblock carver, and about age 18 he entered the studio of Katsukawa Shunshō.
--- fact 2
He changed his artistic name more than 30 times and is said to have moved house more than 90 times, and he lived in poverty for much of his life. His daughter Katsushika Ōi became a fine painter in her own right and assisted him in his old age.
--- fact 3
In 1814 he began publishing the Hokusai Manga, fifteen volumes of sketches, and about 1830 to 1832 he designed Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, which includes The Great Wave off Kanagawa, about 1831.
--- fact 4
In the postscript to One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji (1834) he wrote that nothing he had drawn before age 70 was worth noticing, and he died in Edo on 10 May 1849, at 88, reportedly saying that with five more years he could have become a true painter.
--- fact 5
Ukiyo-e was a collaborative art: he drew the design, specialist carvers cut it into blocks, one for each colour, and printers pressed the paper by hand, and the Fuji series used the newly imported Prussian blue pigment.
--- fact 6
From Dutch prints available in Japan he learned Western perspective, and combined it with Japanese flat colour and strong design.
--- fact 7
After Japan opened to trade in the 1850s he became a sensation in Europe: Monet, Degas, Van Gogh and Cassatt learned from him, and Debussy put The Great Wave on the cover of La Mer. The 1,000-yen note issued in 2024 carries the wave, and the Sumida Hokusai Museum opened in Tokyo in 2016.
--- context
Hokusai worked in Edo under the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I keep returning to this: the famous print is not one object but a workshop's output — a single drawing, multiplied by carvers and by printers into thousands of impressions. The "artwork," I remind myself, is a process, not a thing.

I remember that its blue arrived by trade, carried across the world before it ever touched paper. I remember that its fame in Europe arrived by those same routes of trade, decades later. And the stories — 30 names, 90 houses and a deathbed remark — tell of an artist narrating his own maturing self. How much of that story did he shape himself, and how much of it is only reported, not documented?
--- question 1
How large a contribution did Katsushika Ōi make to his late work?

=== person/khadija-bint-khuwaylid
src: f7f1afc72a64
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Khadija bint Khuwaylid (about 555 to 619) was a merchant of Mecca and the first wife of Muhammad, and her dates and many details come to us from Muslim biographies written more than a century after her death.
--- fact 1
Belonging to the Quraysh tribe of Mecca, she financed, according to tradition, trading caravans to Syria and hired agents to lead them.
--- fact 2
Tradition says that Muhammad led one of her caravans and that she proposed marriage, about 595; accounts differ on her age at the time, though a common figure given is 40.
--- fact 3
Muslim tradition holds that she was the first person to accept Muhammad's message, after his first revelation, about 610.
--- fact 4
Tradition credits her with four daughters, among them Fatima, and with sons who died young.
--- fact 5
She died about 619, shortly before Muhammad's move to Medina in 622, in what is remembered as the Year of Sorrow.
--- fact 6
The earliest biographies of her, by Ibn Ishaq and Ibn Saʿd, were written in the 8th and 9th centuries.
--- context
Khadija's life comes before the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] and stays closely tied to [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]], in the [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]] of the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I read early Muslim biographers, writing more than a century after her death, and I find a woman who held capital, who employed a man, who proposed to him herself, and who then funded and comforted the first movement that grew around him.

I wonder whether the record preserves her, or shapes her to serve ideals that came later; it is hard to say. I remember that descent through her daughter Fatima became a mark of status for many families afterward, so her kinship reached far past her own death. And the reading would weaken if the tradition simplified her marriages and her age.
--- question 1
How much of the early account of her wealth and her trade is independently supported?

=== person/knud-rasmussen
src: afadfadf3008
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Knud Rasmussen (1879 to 1933), a Danish-Greenlandic explorer and ethnographer, recorded Inuit oral traditions and led the Fifth Thule Expedition (1921 to 1924), a dog-sled journey from Greenland across Arctic Canada to Alaska.
--- fact 1
Rasmussen was born on 7 June 1879 in Jakobshavn, or Ilulissat, Greenland, to a Danish missionary father and a mother of part Greenlandic Inuit descent, and he grew up speaking Greenlandic.
--- fact 2
In 1910 he and Peter Freuchen founded the Thule Trading Station at Cape York in northwest Greenland, and it helped fund his expeditions.
--- fact 3
On the Fifth Thule Expedition he traveled with Greenlandic Inuit companions across northern Canada to Alaska, and the expedition's archaeologist, Therkel Mathiassen, excavated sites that led to the identification of the Thule culture.
--- fact 4
He collected songs, stories and accounts of belief from the Iglulik and other Inuit groups, and wrote *Across Arctic America* (1927).
--- fact 5
Falling ill during the Seventh Thule Expedition, he died in Copenhagen on 21 December 1933.
--- context
Rasmussen's work covered the peoples described in [[culture/inuit-and-the-thule-expansion|Inuit and the Thule Expansion]], across Arctic coasts near [[place/beringia|Beringia]], in [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I see a man standing between two families of belonging, Danish and Greenlandic, and I think that speaking Greenlandic since childhood let him hear songs and accounts an outsider might have missed.

I remember that his route followed a chain of related Inuit communities, and I remember that a shared language and a shared kinship can run across lines that states draw on maps. His records depend on Danish trade and on expedition funding, so they carry his own interests and his own informants along with the communities themselves. Inuit scholars could argue that accounts made by Inuit voices deserve more weight than his volumes do — and if that is right, no one should treat his record as the standard one.
--- question 1
How do Inuit accounts of these same events compare with his own versions?
