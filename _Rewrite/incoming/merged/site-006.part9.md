--- summary
London is the capital of the United Kingdom, on the Thames River, founded as the Roman town of Londinium in about 47 CE. Like a river gathering its tributaries, it grew into a leading center of trade, finance and empire and, in the 19th century, the largest city in the world.
--- fact 1
The Romans founded Londinium in about 47 CE near a crossing of the Thames; Boudica's revolt destroyed it in 60 or 61, yet it rose again to become the capital of Roman Britain, until Roman rule ebbed away around 410.
--- fact 2
William I was crowned at Westminster in 1066, and in about 1078 he began the Tower of London, a fist of stone.
--- fact 3
The Black Death of 1348 to 1349 killed a large share of London's people, and the Great Plague of 1665 killed tens of thousands; then the Great Fire of 1666 devoured about 13,000 houses and 87 parish churches.
--- fact 4
From the 1830s to the 1920s London was probably the largest city in the world, and in 1863 the world's first underground railway opened there, iron veins beneath the streets.
--- fact 5
During the 1854 cholera outbreak in Soho, John Snow mapped deaths by address and linked them, as one traces a stream back to its spring, to a water pump; the "Great Stink" of 1858 led to the building of a sewer system by Joseph Bazalgette.
--- fact 6
London was a major center of shipping, insurance and finance for the Atlantic slave trade and for British colonial trade, and it was the capital of the British Empire.
--- fact 7
The German air raids of 1940 to 1941 killed about 20,000 Londoners or more.

--- observer
The Observer notes that London was founded as a bridge town for a foreign state's administration and grew into a hub of an exchange-web that spanned oceans. Its wealth included profits from the sale and transport of enslaved people, and the Observer records that as suffering; a city's ledger of success has to carry that line too.

Now look at the Snow map of 1854. It shows a city becoming an object of pattern-taming: tabulating deaths by address led to the identification of a source of disease, a method later used across public health. That is the quiet architecture of modern care, a list turned into a map and a map turned into an answer. The reading would weaken if the record shows that London's global role rested more on later trade than on the earlier ones it emphasizes. Count carefully, and a city will tell you what it is.
