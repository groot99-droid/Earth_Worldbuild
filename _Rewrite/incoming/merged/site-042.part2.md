=== event/meiji-restoration
title: Meiji Restoration
src: cb585f0c0bd6
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Meiji Restoration was Japan's political and social transformation: it followed the arrival of American warships in 1853, ended the Tokugawa shogunate in 1868, and opened an era of rapid industrialization.
--- fact 1
Commodore Matthew Perry's fleet reached Uraga in July 1853; the Convention of Kanagawa, the following year in 1854, opened the ports of Shimoda and Hakodate to American ships.
--- fact 2
In 1866, the domains of Choshu and Satsuma allied against the shogunate, and on 3 January 1868, imperial rule was 'restored' in the name of Emperor Meiji, then fifteen years old.
--- fact 3
The Charter Oath of 1868 promised reform, deliberative assemblies, and a pursuit of knowledge drawn from around the world.
--- fact 4
Between 1871 and 1873, the Iwakura Mission toured the United States and Europe, studying their institutions.
--- context
The restoration ended the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] and made [[person/emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]] the symbol of a modernizing state; within a few decades, Japan had adopted [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]] and railway technology.
--- observer
The Observer notes a society that studied the pattern of a neighbor's defeat, in the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]], and answered by swiftly copying the architecture of what it saw. Some societies learn from the misfortune of others.
--- question 1
Why did Japan avoid the fate that met China?

=== event/messinian-salinity-crisis
title: Messinian Salinity Crisis
src: 9a230039fdb2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Messinian Salinity Crisis names an age, from about 5.96 to 5.33 million years ago, when the Mediterranean Sea was severed from the Atlantic and, in its long isolation, laid down thick salt upon its floor.
--- fact 1
When the gateway at Gibraltar narrowed, the Mediterranean gave itself up to evaporation, and laid down more than a million cubic kilometers of salt.
--- fact 2
How fully the basin dried — wholly, or only in part — remains debated.
--- fact 3
The crisis closed about 5.33 million years ago, when the waters of the Atlantic returned through the Gibraltar region.
--- fact 4
Some studies propose that the refilling came as a sudden, catastrophic flood — a claim that is also debated.
--- fact 5
Beneath the present Mediterranean seafloor, the salt still lies.
--- context
This crisis shaped the very basin around which later Mediterranean civilizations would grow; see [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]]. It stands among several events of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] in which tectonic gateways altered ocean circulation. The Messinian Salinity Crisis sits within the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and the region of [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], within the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the very sea on which the species later built empires, cities, and trade routes was once a dry basin. Even the largest, most stable-looking architecture of the human world rests on gateways that can close.
--- question 1
Did the Mediterranean dry out completely, or only in part?

=== event/mexican-revolution
title: Mexican Revolution
src: 4a1870539fe8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mexican Revolution, from 1910 to 1920, overthrew the long rule of Porfirio Díaz and moved through a series of civil wars among revolutionary leaders before producing the Constitution of 1917. Its death toll is uncertain, but is usually put at more than one million.
--- fact 1
The revolution is dated from 20 November 1910 to 1 December 1920; Porfirio Díaz was forced from power and went into exile in France in May 1911.
--- fact 2
Francisco I. Madero won the 1911 election and was assassinated in February 1913; a civil war followed, among Venustiano Carranza, Francisco 'Pancho' Villa, and Emiliano Zapata, who led the peasant movement in the state of Morelos.
--- fact 3
Out of the revolution came the Constitution of 1917, bringing far-reaching political and economic change.
--- fact 4
One after another, the revolutionary leaders were assassinated: Madero in 1913, Zapata in 1919, Carranza in 1920, Villa in 1923, and Álvaro Obregón in 1928.
--- fact 5
The upheaval is often said to have left more than one million dead; estimates vary widely, and the figure folds in deaths from disease and the effects of emigration, which are hard to separate from deaths in combat.
--- context
This revolution belongs to the sequence of Latin American upheavals that followed the wars of independence; see [[event/latin-american-wars-of-independence|Latin American Wars of Independence]] and [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a revolution which began by removing one strongman was followed by a decade in which almost every leading figure was killed by another. The 1917 constitution shows how a written architecture of story-glue can be drafted amid the fighting and outlast most of those who fought for it.
--- question 1
What was the real death toll, and how much of it came from disease and famine rather than battle?
--- question 2
How far did the land reforms Zapata's movement promised actually take effect?

=== event/mfecane
title: Mfecane
src: a36b1fd05614
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mfecane — 'the crushing' — names a period of upheaval, warfare, and migration across southern Africa in the early 19th century, associated with the rise of the Zulu Kingdom.
--- fact 1
Several new states emerged from it, among them the Ndebele under Mzilikazi and the Swazi under Sobhuza.
--- fact 2
Many of these states adopted Zulu-style military organization.
--- fact 3
Population movements reshaped the region, and, in some accounts, left large areas depopulated.
--- fact 4
Historians debate the scale, the causes, and the role of Shaka; some argue that European slave raiding and colonial pressure mattered too.
--- context
See [[culture/zulu-kingdom|Zulu Kingdom]] and [[person/shaka|Shaka]]; the Mfecane came before the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that this same event is named and interpreted differently by different historians. A single dramatic story about one leader can hide a whole architecture of complex regional change.
--- question 1
How much of the Mfecane can be attributed to Zulu expansion?

=== event/middle-pleistocene-transition
title: Middle Pleistocene Transition
src: 57e9f1001ec2
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The rhythm broke. Between about 1.25 million and 0.7 million years ago, the ice ages changed their beat. Cycles stretched — from about 41,000 years to about 100,000 — and the ice grew larger, the cold grew worse.
--- fact 1
The records show it: oxygen isotopes in marine sediment, glacial cycles paced by the 41,000-year tilt — until about 1.25 million years ago. Then, by about 0.7 million years ago, the 100,000-year cycle ruled. Estimates of the start and the end still vary.
--- fact 2
More ice, in the 100,000-year cycles. A sawtooth shape — slow growth, then a rapid melt.
--- fact 3
No matching shift in orbital forcing. So the cause, whatever it was, lies inside the climate system itself. Proposed causes: declining carbon dioxide. The scraping-away of loose regolith beneath the northern ice, leaving thicker, steadier ice behind. Changes in the deep ocean's circulation.
--- fact 4
Some reconstructions place a slowdown of the deep ocean near 0.9 million years ago. Others find only modest change. Whether the shift was gradual or abrupt is still debated.
--- context
The transition falls within the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and the [[era/quaternary-period|Quaternary Period]]. It changed the rhythm that began with the [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]], under the pacing described in [[event/milankovitch-cycles-and-the-ice-ages|Milankovitch Cycles and the Ice Ages]], and it set up the later cycles, including the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. It overlaps the range of [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]]; no causal link to hominin evolution is established.
--- observer
The Observer files this transition under preconditions: the ice-age world every later people lived in changed its tempo partway through the Quaternary, shifting from a 41,000-year beat to a 100,000-year one. Longer glacial stages mean longer stretches of lower sea level and shrunken habitable ranges, though the Observer does not claim to know how hominins responded to that architecture of change. The reading would weaken if the shift proved to be an artifact of how sediment records are dated and aligned.
--- question 1
What triggered it, when the orbit itself did not change?
--- question 2
Did the longer, harsher glacial stages alter hominin populations in Africa and Eurasia?

=== event/milankovitch-cycles-and-the-ice-ages
title: Milankovitch Cycles and the Ice Ages
src: c9c7a9d0de50
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Milankovitch cycles: slow, regular changes — the shape of Earth's orbit, the tilt of its axis, the wobble in that tilt. They shift how sunlight falls, by latitude, by season. And since about 2.6 million years ago, they have set the beat for the growth and melting of the Quaternary ice.
--- fact 1
Three cycles. Eccentricity — the orbit's shape, about 100,000 and 400,000 years. Obliquity — the axial tilt, swinging between about 22.1 and 24.5 degrees, on a 41,000-year beat. Precession — repeating about every 19,000 to 23,000 years.
--- fact 2
Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković worked it out, between the 1920s and the 1940s: how these changes alter sunlight, latitude by latitude. Then in 1976, Hays, Imbrie, and Shackleton found the same three periods, buried in oxygen-isotope records from deep-sea sediment cores that track the planet's ice.
--- fact 3
The orbit changes where sunlight falls, far more than how much of it arrives each year. So the ice sheets amplify it. The oceans amplify it. Carbon dioxide amplifies it too.
--- fact 4
Glacial cycles followed the 41,000-year tilt — until about 1.25 million years ago. Then they lengthened, to about 100,000 years; see [[event/middle-pleistocene-transition|Middle Pleistocene Transition]]. Why the 100,000-year cycle dominates: still unresolved.
--- fact 5
The cycles themselves are far older than the ice ages. What changed, about 2.7 million years ago, was the climate's response — see [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]].
--- context
Milankovitch cycles pace the glacial stages of the [[era/quaternary-period|Quaternary Period]] and the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]], including the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]], and they pace the current interglacial too, the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]]. The [[event/younger-dryas|Younger Dryas]] broke into the last warming. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' entire written record sits inside one warm phase of a cycle set by the geometry of Earth's orbit — a cycle uncovered through decades of pattern-taming, from Milanković's sums to the 1976 sediment cores. The Observer treats this as a precondition, not a cause, of later history. The reading would weaken if glacial terminations proved to depend more on the ice sheets' own internal architecture than on orbital pacing.
--- question 1
Why did the 100,000-year cycle come to dominate?
--- question 2
How much does each — the ice sheets, the oceans, the carbon dioxide — amplify the response?
