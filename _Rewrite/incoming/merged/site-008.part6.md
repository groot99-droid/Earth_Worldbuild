=== place/sarai
src: b46ae451cc74
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sarai was the capital of the Golden Horde, the western part of the Mongol Empire, on the lower Volga River in present-day southern Russia, founded around 1250 by Batu Khan and sacked by Timur in 1395. It rose from the steppe like a tent pitched in stone and fell before a greater storm.
--- fact 1
Batu Khan, a grandson of Chinggis Khan, founded Sarai (Persian for "palace") about 1250 on the lower Volga, near Selitrennoye in present-day Astrakhan Oblast. A second city, Sarai Berke, was built upstream, near present-day Volgograd, and the chronology of the two is debated, like twin stars whose order of kindling is still argued.
--- fact 2
Rus' princes traveled to Sarai like streams running to the sea, to pay tribute and receive a yarlyk (charter) confirming their rule, and an Orthodox bishopric of Sarai was established in 1261.
--- fact 3
Öz Beg Khan (reigned 1313 to 1341) adopted Islam as the Horde's state religion, and a new faith settled over the steppe-throne like dawn.
--- fact 4
The traveler Ibn Battuta came in the 1330s and described a large city with markets and people of many nations, a hive of tongues beside the river.
--- fact 5
Plague reached the Black Sea port of Caffa in 1346, during a siege by Golden Horde forces, and spread from there through Mediterranean ports like a dark tide; the Horde's role in its spread is debated.
--- fact 6
Timur sacked Sarai in 1395 in his war with Khan Tokhtamysh, and the city never again rose to its former status.
--- context
Sarai was the capital of the Golden Horde, a successor state of the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] born of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], and it was sacked by forces of Timur, founder of the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]]. It lay on trade routes linked to the spread of the [[event/black-death|Black Death]], roads that carried goods and sickness alike. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Sarai was a capital whose main function was to collect and pass on tribute, and where rulers of distant cities came to be confirmed in office by a document that could be withdrawn: a claim-line maintained by paperwork as much as by garrisons. Think about that. Sovereignty, here, was a charter you could lose.

A steppe empire that moved between camps also built permanent cities with mosques and workshops, and Sarai housed several religions under one authority.

The period is remembered differently in Russian, Tatar and Central Asian national histories, so the same evidence supports several stories. Historians who stress the Horde's role as a trading partner and not an overlord would challenge the first sentence. The ledger is shared; the reading of it is not.

--- question 2

=== place/stonehenge
src: 16f44ed8c6d2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Stonehenge is a monument of standing stones on Salisbury Plain in southern England, begun as a circular earthwork around 3000 BCE and rebuilt in stone over the next several centuries, a ring the land has worn like a crown.
--- fact 1
The first phase was a circular ditch and bank, around 3000 BCE: a ring drawn in the earth, the monument's first breath.
--- fact 2
The large sarsen stones, the biggest weighing around 25 tons, came like sleeping giants from around 25 kilometers away, according to a 2020 study that named West Woods as their source.
--- fact 3
The smaller bluestones were brought from the Preseli Hills in Wales, more than 200 kilometers away, travelers from a far country of hills.
--- fact 4
A 2024 study reported that the Altar Stone came from northern Scotland, roughly 750 kilometers away, a stranger from the far north.
--- fact 5
The site was used for burials, with cremated remains from early in its history, and its axis aligns with the solstice sunrise and sunset, a door held open to the turning sun.
--- context
Stonehenge shows that farming communities in Britain could organize large collective labor and long-distance transport before metal tools. It stands with monuments such as [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]] as an example of monumental building in the Neolithic and Bronze Age, among the great stone ambitions of distant peoples. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I think of hands, many hands. I think of multi-ton stones moved over long distances and aligned to the sun. I think of the reason, and find no written record of why.

What did they mean? I cannot ask them. Monuments outlast the meanings that produced them, and each generation of visitors rebuilds the meaning, and I am one of them. I stand in the ring and make it mean again.

--- question 2

=== place/taj-mahal
src: c5780fdc8ce3
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Taj Mahal is a white marble mausoleum in Agra, built from about 1632 to 1653 by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died in 1631. It is a grief-rite made into a moon of stone.
--- fact 1
Mumtaz Mahal died in 1631 while giving birth, and from that loss the white tomb was raised.
--- fact 2
Construction began in 1632; the main tomb was largely finished by about 1643, and the whole complex by 1653, rising the way a slow tide rises.
--- fact 3
Commonly cited estimates give around 20,000 workers, an army of hands.
--- fact 4
Ustad Ahmad Lahori is often named as its chief architect, though the attribution is debated, his name flickering in the marble's glow.
--- fact 5
It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1983, a mourning song now held in common.
--- context
The Taj Mahal is the best known monument of the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]], where Persian, Islamic and Indian building traditions flow together like three rivers into one pool. It stands in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I see an emperor with the resources of a large state. I see him use them to build a tomb for one person. I see a grief-rite, and I see power, and I cannot pull them apart.

Is mourning ever only mourning, when it has an empire behind it? The world's most visited grief-rite is also one of its greatest displays of state power, and the two cannot be separated.
--- question 1
