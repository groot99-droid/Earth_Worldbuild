=== place/tenochtitlan
src: 3df8019a7305
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tenochtitlan was the capital of the Aztec Empire, founded on an island in Lake Texcoco in 1325 and destroyed after a siege in 1521, a city that sat upon the lake like a lotus of stone.
--- fact 1
Population estimates for 1519 range from about 100,000 to 400,000, with 200,000 often cited, making it one of the largest cities in the world, a multitude gathered on the water.
--- fact 2
Causeways and canals bound it to the mainland like roots reaching for the shore, and chinampas gave it food.
--- fact 3
The Templo Mayor was its main temple, the city's sacred heart.
--- fact 4
Mexico City was built over its ruins, a new city laid upon the old like snow upon stone.
--- context
Tenochtitlan was the beating center of the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]]. Its fall belongs to the wider tide of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Spanish visitors described the city with awe. Hold that beside what followed: the destruction of such a city in a single siege is a sharp example of how disease and alliances can decide events. Wonder, it turns out, is no protection.
--- question 1
How many people lived in the city before the epidemic came?

=== place/teotihuacan
src: 4acd60c788e9
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Teotihuacan was a huge planned city in the Valley of Mexico that flourished from about 100 BCE to 550 CE, home to the Pyramids of the Sun and Moon, mountains raised by human hands.
--- fact 1
At its peak, around 450 to 500 CE, it may have held 125,000 to 200,000 inhabitants, a sea of lives in the valley.
--- fact 2
The city was laid out on a grid along the 'Avenue of the Dead', its streets ruled straight as a spear's flight.
--- fact 3
It had wide trade and political links, including with Maya cities, its reach running out like roots beneath the forest.
--- fact 4
The Aztecs later gave it its name, meaning roughly 'place where the gods were created', a name laid over the ruins like a mantle.
--- context
Teotihuacan cast its influence over the [[culture/maya|Maya]] and other peoples of [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], like a great light seen from far off.
--- observer
I stand among builders who left no deciphered writing. I stand among later peoples who treated the ruins as sacred. I stand, too, inside my own way of seeing.

Whose meaning am I reading? Even ruins are read through a later culture's meaning-engine, and I am a later culture too.
--- question 1
Who ruled Teotihuacan, and why did the great city collapse?

=== place/tikal
src: 4c59b22dbbc4
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tikal is a large Maya city in the rainforest of northern Guatemala, with traces of farming from about 1000 BCE and a dominant Classic-period kingdom whose rulers left inscriptions from the 3rd to the 9th century CE. After about 950 the city was almost deserted, and the forest kept its silence.
--- fact 1
Tikal, called Yax Mutal in inscriptions and the seat of a kingdom called Mutul, lies in the Petén region of Guatemala; traces of farming date to about 1000 BCE, and monumental building began about 400 to 300 BCE, stone rising out of green.
--- fact 2
Inscriptions record that in January 378 a warlord named Siyaj K'ak' arrived from the west, and Tikal's king died the same day; a new king linked to Teotihuacan was then installed, and scholars debate whether the event was a conquest or an alliance, a storm from the west or a joining of hands.
--- fact 3
In 562 Tikal was defeated by Calakmul and Caracol, and its king was apparently captured and sacrificed; in 695 Jasaw Chan K'awiil I defeated Calakmul, and Tikal rose again from its long shadow.
--- fact 4
Estimates of Tikal's peak population range from about 10,000 to 90,000 for the city, with far higher numbers proposed for its surrounding region, and archaeologists debate them, counting shadows beneath the canopy.
--- fact 5
The last dated monument was set up in 869, and after about 950 Tikal was almost deserted, one flame among many guttering in the wider Classic Maya collapse.
--- fact 6
Modesto Méndez and Ambrosio Tut visited in 1848, Alfred Maudslay documented the site in 1881 to 1882, and the University of Pennsylvania excavated it from 1956 to 1970; Guatemala made it a national park in 1955, and UNESCO inscribed it in 1979. The sleeping city woke to new eyes.
--- context
Tikal was one of the largest kingdoms of the [[culture/maya|Maya]], bound by ties to [[place/teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] in central Mexico, and its emptying belongs to the [[event/classic-maya-collapse|Classic Maya Collapse]]. By its earliest settlement it stands in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]], with its peak in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], in the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and its story runs along the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Tikal's kings set their wars, accessions and rites in stone in a mark-code, so the city's political history is read largely through the claims of its rulers, which favor legitimacy. You are reading a ledger kept by the people it flatters.

Temples raised over royal tombs tied the ruler's body to the standing of the city, a status-signal built to be seen across the forest. And the emptying of the city shows that a monument-building center can be left within a few generations: architecture built to endure does not guarantee that anyone stays.

The reading would weaken if epigraphic and archaeological work shows that non-royal households, not kings, drove most decisions about settlement and abandonment. The loudest record is not always the whole record.
--- question 1
How large was Tikal at its peak, and how was its population fed and supplied with water?
--- question 2
Was the event of 378 a conquest by Teotihuacan, or an alliance struck between elites?
