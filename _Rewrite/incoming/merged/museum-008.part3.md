--- description
Saladin (Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, about 1137 to 1193) was a Kurdish-born Muslim commander who founded the Ayyubid dynasty, ruled Egypt and much of Syria, and recaptured Jerusalem from the Crusaders in 1187. Follow his rise step by step and you see how a dynasty is assembled.

He was born about 1137 in Tikrit, in present-day Iraq, to a Kurdish family, and died in Damascus on 4 March 1193.

Step one: service. He rose under the Zengid ruler Nur ad-Din and became vizier of Fatimid Egypt in 1169.

Step two: allegiance. In 1171 he ended the Fatimid Caliphate and restored Egypt's allegiance to the Abbasid caliph; his family, the Ayyubids, went on to rule Egypt and Syria.

Step three: consolidation. After Nur ad-Din died in 1174 he took Damascus and brought much of Syria and northern Mesopotamia under his rule.

On 4 July 1187 his army destroyed a Crusader army at the Battle of Hattin. Jerusalem surrendered on 2 October 1187. Its Latin inhabitants could ransom themselves, though many who could not pay were enslaved. A city's surrender has a ledger, and the poorest pay the most.

=== people--tamar-of-georgia--1
src: adb7ad1652ce
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Tamar (born about 1160 or 1166, died 1213) ruled the Kingdom of Georgia from 1184 to 1213, presiding over its political and cultural summit, later called the Georgian Golden Age.

She was crowned co-ruler with her father, George III, in 1178 to secure her succession, and on his death in 1184 she became sole monarch, ruling with the formal title "king of kings" rather than a feminine equivalent.

Her armies won great victories against regional Muslim powers, among them the Battle of Shamkor in 1195 and the Battle of Basian (dated to 1202, 1203 or 1204 depending on the source), and Georgian influence spread like a rising tide across much of the South Caucasus and into parts of Armenia and Anatolia.

Her reign saw rich patronage of Georgian literature and architecture, including the era's most celebrated work, Shota Rustaveli's epic poem "The Knight in the Panther's Skin," traditionally associated with her as its inspiration.

The Georgian Orthodox Church canonized her as a saint, and centuries of later Georgian tradition remember her reign as the kingdom's greatest age of security and cultural achievement.
