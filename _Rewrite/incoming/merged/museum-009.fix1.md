I stand in the room of Sesshū Tōyō (1420 to 1506), the Japanese Zen monk and painter who studied Chinese ink painting in Ming China and made it a Japanese art, regarded as the greatest master of Japanese ink painting.

I read that he was born in 1420 in Bitchū Province (present-day Okayama Prefecture) and entered a Zen temple as a boy. I read that he studied painting at Shōkoku-ji in Kyoto with the monk-painter Tenshō Shūbun. I read that about the 1460s he took the name Sesshū, "snow boat," and settled in Yamaguchi under the patronage of the Ōuchi family.

In 1467 he joined a trade mission to Ming China and spent about two to three years there (sources differ), travelling to Beijing and receiving an honorary title at the Tiantong monastery near Ningbo. He returned about 1469, as the Ōnin War was tearing Kyoto apart, and worked in the provinces from his studio, the Unkoku-an, in Yamaguchi. He is credited with designing several temple gardens, and he died in 1506.

He painted in suibokuga, black ink and water on paper or silk, including the loose haboku ("broken ink") manner: Haboku Landscape (1495), Huike Offering His Arm to Bodhidharma (1496), View of Ama-no-Hashidate (about 1501 to 1506).

A city torn apart, and a man painting in ink and water. Is that escape, or faith? I think it can be both.

=== people--sesshu-toyo--2

--- description
Sesshū Tōyō (1420 to 1506) was a Japanese Zen monk and painter who studied Chinese ink painting in Ming China and made it a Japanese art. He is regarded as the greatest master of Japanese ink painting, and his path shows how a borrowed form becomes your own: study it at the source, then bring it home.

He was born in 1420 in Bitchū Province (present-day Okayama Prefecture) and entered a Zen temple as a boy. He studied painting at Shōkoku-ji in Kyoto with the monk-painter Tenshō Shūbun.

About the 1460s he took the name Sesshū ("snow boat") and settled in Yamaguchi under the patronage of the Ōuchi family.

In 1467 he joined a trade mission to Ming China and spent about two to three years there (sources differ), travelling to Beijing and receiving an honorary title at the Tiantong monastery near Ningbo.

He returned to Japan about 1469, as the Ōnin War was tearing Kyoto apart, and worked in the provinces from his studio, the Unkoku-an, in Yamaguchi. He is credited with designing several temple gardens, architecture made from a landscape.

He died in 1506. He painted in suibokuga, black ink and water on paper or silk, including the loose haboku ("broken ink") manner. Key works include Haboku Landscape (1495), Huike Offering His Arm to Bodhidharma (1496) and View of Ama-no-Hashidate (about 1501 to 1506).
