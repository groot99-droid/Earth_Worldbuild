A later chapter, painted in the same early 12th-century scroll tradition, where the light has passed on to the generation after Genji's death. === genji-sakaki
title: The Tale of Genji — Hakubyō scroll fragment (II)
src: a2b315a06c04
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Another scene from the same monochrome hakubyō scroll, drawn in ink line alone, like a world seen by moonlight. It belongs to a genre of Genji illustration that favoured clarity of narrative detail over the saturated mineral colour of the earliest surviving scrolls, bare as winter branches where those were jewelled, and later painters often took it up as a study or reference copy, a map for their own journeys through the tale.

=== genji-suzumushi
title: The Tale of Genji — "Suzumushi" (The Bell Cricket)
src: b183a11bd9f5
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
A chapter named for the bell crickets set loose into a garden Genji has planted to evoke autumn, as if a season could be summoned by song. The scroll shows it with the restrained, geometric interior perspective and the stylised viewpoint of a roof blown away by the wind, the eye dropping like a bird into the rooms below, as is characteristic of this earliest surviving Genji scroll tradition.

=== masnavi-opening
title: Masnavi-ye Ma'navi (opening)
src: d21c661fb603
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Here the great river rises: the opening page of Rumi's six-book masterwork, some 25,000 couplets that flowed from him over the last decade or so of his life. They came at the request of his disciple Husam al-Din Chalabi, who wrote the verses down as Rumi recited them, catching each line as a cupped hand catches rain, reportedly even while the poet walked through the streets of Konya.

=== masnavi-peacock-parable
title: Masnavi — The Wise Man and the Peacock
src: c64e19f4e1f4
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
A doctrine is easier to carry when it arrives as a story. The Masnavi's six books hold hundreds of short parables and stories that Rumi wove through them to illustrate points of Sufi doctrine in accessible, often humorous form. Here is one: a peacock plucking out its own beautiful feathers to avoid the suffering its beauty attracts. The lesson is plain. It is a lesson on renouncing worldly vanity. Sometimes the ornament is the snare.

=== divan-e-shams
title: Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi
src: 25223eade07f
mode: confessional-poetry
--- description
I stand before a vast collection of ecstatic lyric poems, tens of thousands of lines, and I think of the meeting that began them. Rumi composed them after meeting the wandering mystic Shams-e Tabrizi in 1244, an encounter that transformed him from a respected legal scholar into a poet. I read the title and it is not his own name. He named the entire collection after his friend and teacher rather than himself. Whose name would I sign on my own best work? Some meetings write the rest of a life.

=== fihi-ma-fihi
title: Fihi Ma Fihi (In It What Is In It)
src: ec2156b89aa5
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
If you want to know how a teacher thinks, listen to the talk as well as the sermon. This is a collection of Rumi's informal talks and discourses to his disciples, recorded in prose rather than verse, covering everything from Quranic interpretation to everyday anecdotes. Scholars value it as the clearest direct record of how Rumi taught and argued outside the formal architecture of his poetry. Think of it as the workshop beside the cathedral. The verse is the monument; the talk is the doorway in.

=== sema-ceremony
title: The Sema (whirling ceremony)
src: 185bfd7d4e2a
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Not a text but a living performance tradition, a river that has never stopped moving. It is associated with Rumi, and after his death his followers formalised it into the Mevlevi Sufi order: a turning dance performed to music and poetry, the dancers wheeling like slow stars, intended to induce a trance-like focus on the divine. The turning has not ceased. It continues to be performed at his shrine in Konya and by Mevlevi communities worldwide.

=== khusrau-matla-ul-anwar
title: Matla' al-Anwar (The Rising of Lights)
src: 4ad5fcde072c
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
The first of the five long narrative poems making up Khusrau's Khamsa (Quintet), the gate through which the whole procession enters. It was modelled on the Persian poet Nizami's own Khamsa, as a new tower rises in the shadow of an old one, but reworked with Khusrau's own didactic and mystical concerns. This folio illustrates one of its moral parables: a pilgrim humbled by an unexpected lesson in piety from a Hindu holy man.

=== khusrau-shirin-khusrau
title: Shirin o Khusrau
src: 338f266cef53
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
What do you owe the voice that came before you? This is the second poem of the Khamsa, and it retells the romance of the Sasanian king Khusrau and the Armenian princess Shirin, a story Nizami had already told. The retelling was deliberate: an act of literary homage and rivalry, common practice among Persian poets responding to an admired predecessor's Khamsa. Notice the architecture of that lineage. To retell is to bow and to challenge in the same breath.

=== khusrau-majnun-layla
title: Majnun o Layla
src: f95943e9e7f4
mode: confessional-poetry
--- description
I stand before the third poem of the Khamsa, where Khusrau condensed the widely told Arabian legend of Layla and Majnun into his own tightly constructed Persian verse. I know these lovers. I know the families that drove them apart. I know the desert wilderness where Majnun wanders in grief-mad devotion. How much longing can a tight verse hold? More than I can. Love torn apart does not end; it wanders.
