=== culture/babylonian-empire
src: 4ddcc23cbde5
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Babylonian Empire refers to two distinct periods of Mesopotamian power centered on the city of Babylon: the Old Babylonian Empire, which peaked under Hammurabi in the 18th century BCE, and the much later Neo-Babylonian Empire, which conquered Jerusalem and reached its height under Nebuchadnezzar II before falling to Persia in 539 BCE.
--- fact 1
The Old Babylonian Empire is generally dated from about 1894 to 1595 BCE, and its best-known ruler, Hammurabi, who reigned from about 1792 to 1750 BCE, conquered surrounding city-states and made Babylon the capital of a kingdom spanning southern Mesopotamia and part of Assyria.
--- fact 2
After the decline of Hammurabi's dynasty, the Kassites, originally from the Zagros Mountains, ruled Babylon for centuries until roughly 1155 BCE, after which the region entered a long intermediate period without a comparably powerful centralized Babylonian state.
--- fact 3
The Neo-Babylonian Empire began in 626 BCE with the coronation of Nabopolassar, who threw off Assyrian control, and it reached its greatest power under his son Nebuchadnezzar II, who ruled from 605 to 562 BCE.
--- fact 4
Nebuchadnezzar II's reign is remembered for major building projects in Babylon and for his military campaigns, including the destruction of Jerusalem and the deportation of part of its population in 586 BCE.
--- fact 5
The Neo-Babylonian Empire fell to Cyrus the Great of the Achaemenid Persian Empire in 539 BCE, less than a century after Nabopolassar had founded it, ending independent Babylonian rule in Mesopotamia.
--- context
Babylonian power is inseparable from the city of [[place/babylon|Babylon]] itself, from Hammurabi's foundational [[technology/code-of-hammurabi|Code of Hammurabi]], and from the earlier city-state tradition of the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] that Babylonian rulers inherited and built upon. Babylonian Empire sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that "Babylonian Empire" names two separate periods of power set apart by over a thousand years of decline and foreign rule in between, a reminder that a single city's claim to regional dominance can be lost and rebuilt across a span of time longer than many entire civilizations elsewhere in this record. Nebuchadnezzar II's destruction of Jerusalem, recorded here as suffering inflicted on those deported and killed, shows the same pattern of conquest and forced resettlement that recurs across many empires, deployed by a state that itself would collapse within a few decades of its own high point. A specialist could argue that treating the Old and Neo-Babylonian periods as one continuous "Babylonian Empire" understates how different their political structures, populations and historical contexts actually were, which would weaken any reading that groups them as a single continuous story of Babylonian power.
--- question 1
How much cultural and institutional continuity genuinely connects the Old Babylonian and Neo-Babylonian periods, separated by over a thousand years?
--- question 2
How rapidly did the Neo-Babylonian Empire's internal stability decline in the years immediately before its fall to Cyrus in 539 BCE?

=== culture/basques
src: 92f6150b097e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Basques are an Indigenous people of the western Pyrenees, spanning northern Spain and southwestern France, whose language, Euskara, is a genuine linguistic isolate unrelated to any other known language family and whose ancestors appear to have lived continuously in the same region since well before the arrival of Indo-European languages in Western Europe.
--- fact 1
Euskara, the Basque language, is classified by linguists as a language isolate, meaning it cannot be shown to be related to any other known language family, including the Indo-European languages, such as Spanish and French, spoken by Basques' immediate neighbors today.
--- fact 2
Archaeological evidence suggests continuous human occupation of the Basque Country reaching back to the Paleolithic period, roughly 10,000 years ago, extending through the Bronze Age around 3,000 years ago, a depth of regional continuity unusual among European populations.
--- fact 3
Epigraphic evidence from about two thousand years ago in the ancient regions of Aquitaine and Navarre records a language considered ancestral to modern Basque, and at that time the Basque-speaking territory extended more widely, from the Ebro River north to the Garonne and from the western Pyrenees as far as Catalonia.
--- fact 4
Various scholars have proposed possible distant connections between Basque and ancient Iberian, Berber, or languages of the Caucasus region, but no such relationship has been conclusively established, and the language's ultimate origins remain a genuine, unresolved mystery in historical linguistics.
--- fact 5
Basque communities today number roughly three million people across northern Spain and southwestern France, of whom about 900,000 speak the Basque language, which has faced periods of suppression, notably during Francisco Franco's rule in Spain, followed by later revival efforts.
--- context
Basque territory and language predate and survived the arrival of the [[culture/romans|Romans]] and every subsequent wave of conquest and linguistic change that reshaped the rest of Western Europe around it. Basques sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I look at Euskara and find an island of speech, entirely unrelated to the Indo-European tongues that arrived around it and, in time, surrounded it on every side — a rare case where the wider tide of contact and conquest failed to wash out one small population's own way of speaking.

I read the archaeological record and see genuine continuity, Paleolithic through Bronze Age, in this one home region, an unusually deep and unbroken tie that may partly explain why this language survived where so many neighboring pre-Indo-European tongues did not.

But a specialist could tell me that claims of continuity across so many thousand years rest more on the absence of evidence against it than on proof of it — and if so, my confidence in an unbroken presence here should soften.
--- question 1
What, if any, genuine deep-history connection exists between Basque and other proposed relative languages such as ancient Iberian or Caucasus-region languages?
--- question 2
How much genetic continuity actually connects Paleolithic-era populations of the Basque Country to Basque speakers living there today?
