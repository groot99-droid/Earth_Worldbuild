=== people--kupe--1
src: 0912bfe7ba83
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Kupe is a legendary Polynesian navigator credited in Maori oral tradition with being the first to reach and name Aotearoa (New Zealand); no fixed historical date can be given to him, for he sails in memory rather than in the calendar.

Traditions tell of Kupe sailing from the ancestral homeland Hawaiki, in some versions pursuing the giant octopus Te Wheke-a-Muturangi, and coming to the North Island of New Zealand.

He is credited with naming many coastal landmarks, several of which still bear those names today, and with returning to Hawaiki afterward to tell of the land he had found.

Different iwi (tribal) traditions place him at varying points, and 19th- and 20th-century European writers fused these into a single traditional date of roughly 925 CE (the orthodox version tied to S. Percy Smith; other sources give about 900 to 1200 CE), which many historians now treat with caution.

No archaeological or written record fixes Kupe's voyage. The earliest confirmed archaeological evidence for human settlement of New Zealand dates to around the 13th century CE, later than most traditional accounts imply.

=== people--montezuma-ii--1
src: d12dda109f82
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Montezuma II (Moctezuma; about 1466 to 1520) was the ninth ruler (tlatoani) of the Aztec Empire, reigning at its greatest territorial extent until the arrival of Hernan Cortes and the Spanish conquest. His story is told to us by two sides, and you should hear both.

He extended Aztec control through further conquests mainly in Oaxaca and Guerrero, plus a campaign around 1514 through the Huastec region, and reorganized the empire's tribute and administrative systems.

Cortes and a small Spanish force reached the capital Tenochtitlan in November 1519. Montezuma received them, and was then held as a hostage within his own palace.

Accounts of his final months differ. Spanish sources say he tried to calm his people during the fighting that followed the 1520 Toxcatl massacre and Cortes's return, and was killed by a stone thrown by his own subjects; some later Aztec-descended chroniclers say the Spanish killed him directly. Who tells a death decides who carries the blame.

His death in June 1520 came shortly before the Spanish were driven from the city in the "Noche Triste." His brother Cuitlahuac and then his nephew Cuauhtemoc took up the resistance that ended with Tenochtitlan's fall in 1521.

=== people--montezuma-ii--2
src: d12dda109f82
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Montezuma II (Moctezuma; about 1466 to 1520) was the ninth ruler (tlatoani) of the Aztec Empire. He reigned at its greatest territorial extent, until the arrival of Hernan Cortes and the Spanish conquest.

He extended Aztec control through further conquests, mainly in Oaxaca and Guerrero, plus a campaign around 1514 through the Huastec region, and he reorganized the empire's tribute and administrative systems: the ledger and the architecture of an empire.

In November 1519 Cortes and a small Spanish force reached the capital Tenochtitlan. Montezuma received them and was subsequently held as a hostage within his own palace. Hospitality, answered with captivity.

Accounts of his final months differ. Spanish sources say he tried to calm his people during the fighting that followed the 1520 Toxcatl massacre and Cortes's return, and that he was killed by a stone thrown by his own subjects. Some later Aztec-descended chroniclers say the Spanish killed him directly.

He died in June 1520, shortly before the Spanish were driven from the city in the "Noche Triste." His brother Cuitlahuac and then his nephew Cuauhtemoc took up the resistance, which ended with Tenochtitlan's fall in 1521. Remember the resistance as part of his story, not an epilogue to it.

=== people--albrecht-durer--1
src: c5d2254decd3
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Albrecht Dürer (21 May 1471 to 6 April 1528) was a painter and printmaker of Nuremberg who carried the Italian Renaissance north like a lamp over the mountains. His woodcuts and engravings, sold across Europe, made him the first artist with an international reputation built largely on prints.

He was born in Nuremberg, son of a Hungarian-born goldsmith. From 1486 he was apprenticed to Michael Wolgemut, whose workshop made woodcuts for book illustration.

He travelled to Venice in 1494, and again from early 1506 to 1507, where Giovanni Bellini treated him as an equal. In 1498 he published the Apocalypse, 15 large woodcuts (some counts give 16 designs dated 1498), and with them his fame was made.

From 1512 Emperor Maximilian I employed him and granted him a pension. On a journey to the Netherlands in 1520 to 1521 to have it confirmed by Charles V, his diary recorded his wonder at Aztec treasures sent from Mexico.

He sympathised with Martin Luther's reforms. In his last years he published treatises on measurement, fortification and human proportion.

His "AD" monogram served as a trademark, and he took action against copyists who used it.
