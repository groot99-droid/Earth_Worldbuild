=== species/goat
title: Goat
src: 0bcb1baf09b6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic goat was bred from the wild bezoar ibex in southwestern Asia around 10,000 years ago, one of the first animals to walk beside farming communities, and it is still prized today for milk, meat and fiber, above all on marginal land unsuited to other livestock, the stony edges of the world.
--- fact 1
Domestic goats descend from the bezoar ibex, a wild goat that still haunts the Zagros Mountains of Iran and once ranged across Anatolia, and domestication is dated to about 10,000 years ago in Southwest Asia and Eastern Europe.
--- fact 2
The earliest clear archaeological evidence for goat domestication rises from Ganj Dareh in Iran, and further early sites at Jericho, Choga Mami, Djeitun and Çayönü, strung like beads on a long cord, date domestication to between about 8,000 and 9,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Neolithic herders drew from the goat as from a deep well: milk and meat, dung for fuel, and bones, hair and sinew for tools, building materials and clothing.
--- fact 4
Goats still carry economic weight today: in dairy, where they provide about 2 percent of the world's total annual milk supply, and in fiber such as cashmere and mohair, and they are valued in part for their hardiness on land too marginal for cattle or sheep.
--- fact 5
More than 1.1 billion goats were living worldwide as of 2022, and about 150 million of them were in India, the largest single-country population; India was also the largest goat-milk producer that year, followed by Bangladesh and South Sudan.
--- context
Goats were among the earliest animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]], one pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent, standing alongside [[species/sheep|Sheep]], which were domesticated in a similar place and period and often herded with them, two flocks moving as one. Goats belong to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and their thread runs through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact. The goat's continuing importance on land too poor for cattle or sheep suggests that its domestication gave early farming communities a way to draw food and materials from marginal ground that would otherwise have supported no livestock at all. Picture a slope of scrub and stone that feeds nothing you can use; put a goat on it, and the slope becomes a pantry. That is how the reach of the Settling extended into harsher terrain. That goats and sheep were domesticated in the same general region and period, and are still often herded together, points to an early kin-lattice of pastoral practice, an architecture that treated the two species as complementary rather than as competitors for the same land. Where we might expect rivals, the pattern shows partners. The reading would weaken if goat and sheep domestication are shown to have occurred at meaningfully different times or in different communities rather than in close succession.
--- question 1
How closely were the taming of goats and the taming of sheep bound together: did one community likely tame both, or did the two practices spread along separate paths?
--- question 2
As climate change redraws which regions count as marginal, how has goat herding on those hard edges of the land changed?

=== species/hallucigenia
title: Hallucigenia
src: 075d071ae3f5
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hallucigenia is a small, worm-like Cambrian animal with paired spines along its back and clawed legs beneath, known from the Burgess Shale of Canada and from China, about 510 to 505 million years ago. It is known for being raised upside down in its 1977 reconstruction and for the 2015 discovery of its head, as if it had waited in the dark to be read aright.
--- fact 1
Three species bear the name: H. sparsa from the Burgess Shale of British Columbia, H. fortis from Chengjiang and H. hongmeia from Guanshan, both in Yunnan, China. The Burgess Shale is about 505 million years old by its fossil zones; Chengjiang is at most 518.
--- fact 2
H. sparsa is about 3 cm long, a sliver of a thing, though published maxima reach 5.5 cm, and it is rare as a star at noon, about 0.19 percent of specimens counted in the Walcott Quarry.
--- fact 3
In 1977 Simon Conway Morris reconstructed it walking on stilt-like spines, with tentacles along its back. Work on Chinese specimens in 1991 turned the creature right side up: the spines were on top, and the "tentacles" were legs.
--- fact 4
In 2015 Martin Smith and Jean-Bernard Caron showed that the supposed head was only a decay stain, a ghost left by rot. The true head has a pair of simple eyes, a mouth ringed with plates like a crown, and a toothed throat.
--- fact 5
Its claws echo those of living velvet worms, and it is placed among the lobopodians, stem relatives of that group, an old branch near the root. Some analyses set it deeper still, more basally among the panarthropods.
--- context
Hallucigenia lived in the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]], during and after the bright tumult of the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], and shared the Burgess Shale with [[species/anomalocaris|Anomalocaris]] and [[species/pikaia|Pikaia]]. At Chengjiang Fossil Site it walked alongside [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]]. Its story belongs to [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that an animal a few centimeters long was drawn upside down for fourteen years, and its head found only in 2015. Much of the deep past is a reading of evidence, and the reading changes when specimens arrive. You could call it the grammar of discovery: every reconstruction is a sentence, and new fossils are its revisions. The practice is to hold the picture loosely, like a map of a coast no one has yet walked. What we know is a draft, not a verdict. The reading would weaken if later study showed the 2015 head identification to be mistaken.
--- question 1
Where does Hallucigenia truly sit among the branching panarthropods?
--- question 2
How many valid species does the genus contain, once every stone has been read?
