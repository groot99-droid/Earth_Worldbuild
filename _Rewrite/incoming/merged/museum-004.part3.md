=== strawberry-thief
src: cfcfe079c947
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
The name is a small confession from the garden: the design is named for the thrushes that stole fruit from Morris's own kitchen garden. It took months to perfect using the indigo-discharge method, a slow, labour-intensive dyeing process Morris insisted on reviving over cheaper synthetic-dye printing, because of the richer colour and permanence it gave. Still in production today, it remains one of the best-known textile patterns ever made. Here is the covenant of craft: choose the slow method when it is the one that lasts.

=== willow-bough
src: bf36b1bf4b2c
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Study before you stylise. This dense, interlocking pattern of willow leaves was derived from trees Morris studied along the River Thames near his country home, Kelmscott Manor. It was among the most technically demanding of his wallpapers to print, requiring numerous separate blocks, and yet it remains one of his most widely reproduced designs. The difficulty stays behind the wall; what reaches us is ease. That is the quiet architecture of mastery: the labour is hidden so the leaves can simply move.

=== kelmscott-chaucer
src: 639e3e392168
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
In 1891 Morris founded a private press to revive medieval standards of book design against industrial mass printing, and this is its masterpiece. Read its parts as a ledger of friendship and craft: 87 woodcut illustrations by his lifelong friend Edward Burne-Jones, ornamental borders designed by Morris himself, and a typeface he cut based on 15th-century models. It was finished only months before his death, and it became the most influential book of the private-press movement that followed. A last work can become a first principle for those who come after.

=== impression-sunrise
src: 574015559f59
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Dawn over the port of Le Havre, sketched in a few quick strokes of blue and grey, as if the harbour were still half a dream of the sea. A small orange sun hangs there, and its reflection is dabbed onto the water like an ember dropped into a still pool. Shown at the first independent exhibition of 1874, it gave the critic Louis Leroy a mocking name for the whole group: the "Impressionists". The painters took up that name themselves, and carried the mockery like a banner.

=== woman-with-a-parasol
src: d576378503b1
mode: confessional-poetry
--- description
I look up at her, the way the painting makes me: Monet's wife, Camille, on a breezy hilltop seen from below, her veil and white dress blowing. I look past her to their son Jean, watching from further off. I look, and I know it was painted quickly, outdoors, less a portrait than a record of sunlight, wind and a passing moment, like a snapshot of a family walk.

How many walks have I taken that no one caught? The wind does not wait to be painted. Love is mostly weather, and weather is mostly gone.

=== gare-saint-lazare
src: a4590d863a81
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Monet obtained permission to set up his easel inside the Paris railway station, and there he painted a dozen views of its trains, its iron roof and its billowing steam. In this one, locomotives breathe out clouds of blue and white smoke beneath the glass canopy, like great iron beasts exhaling their warmth. It celebrates modern life and turns the station into a cathedral of light and vapour, where smoke rises like incense toward a sky of glass.

=== haystacks
src: b731a50c07bb
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Two tall stacks of harvested wheat stand near Monet's home at Giverny, gilded by warm late-summer light like twin hearths holding the last of the season's fire. The canvas belongs to a series of some 25 of the same subject, painted in different seasons, weather and times of day, the same stacks changing as a shore changes with the tide. Monet worked on several canvases at once, switching as the light changed. The series, shown in 1891, was a triumph.

=== rouen-cathedral
src: b0edd76a6469
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Monet rented rooms opposite the Gothic cathedral of Rouen and painted its façade more than 30 times at different hours, keeping watch on it like a sentinel counting the passing of suns. In this bright-afternoon version, the stone dissolves into crusty layers of cream, gold and blue shadow, as a cliff crumbles into sea-foam. The subject is no longer the building but the light that falls on it.

=== water-lilies
src: f3b20689ca7f
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
We look down upon the surface of the pond Monet created in his garden at Giverny, and the world turns over: floating lily pads and flowers drift among reflections of sky and trees, with no horizon and no bank to hold them, as if the water had forgotten where the land ends. Monet painted about 250 water lily pictures in his last 30 years. The largest, the *Grandes Décorations*, fill two oval rooms at the Musée de l'Orangerie in Paris, like a pond poured into stone.

=== valley-of-mexico
src: 29d71300cb3e
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
This was Velasco's most celebrated subject. From a hillside of scrub and cactus, the vast basin of the Valley of Mexico unrolls beneath an immense sky like a map spread before a king, with Lake Texcoco, the distant city, and the snowy volcanoes keeping their long watch on the horizon. Every plant and rock in the foreground is botanically and geologically accurate, yet the panorama feels epic. It became an image of the Mexican nation itself.

=== pyramid-of-the-sun
src: 739075be9d8a
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
The great pre-Hispanic pyramid of Teotihuacan rises above the plain beneath a luminous sky, then still overgrown and only partly excavated, a sleeping mountain wrapped in a cloak of green. Velasco took part in scientific and archaeological expeditions, and paintings like this linked Mexico's ancient past to its modern national identity, as a bridge joins one bank of a river to the other.

=== metlac-bridge
src: 83966c519f8f
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
A steam train crosses a spectacular curved railway bridge, bent like a drawn bow over a deep tropical ravine, on the line between Mexico City and Veracruz. The painting celebrates modern engineering within an overwhelming natural landscape, a thread of iron strung across the green throat of the land. It is Velasco's answer to Turner's *Rain, Steam and Speed*, and a record of Mexico's rapid modernisation under Porfirio Díaz.
