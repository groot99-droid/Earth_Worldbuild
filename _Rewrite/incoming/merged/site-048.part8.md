=== person/jayavarman-vii
title: Jayavarman VII
type: person
date: c. 1122 – 1218
themes: religion, war, art
src: 3ed2ee39445e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jayavarman VII, who lived from about 1122 to about 1218, ruled the Khmer Empire as king from about 1181 until his death. He drove out Cham invaders who had sacked Angkor and then led a large building program that included Angkor Thom and the Bayon.
--- fact 1
Champa forces sacked Angkor in 1177; Jayavarman VII defeated them and was crowned about 1181, though his birth and death years remain estimates.
--- fact 2
Khmer forces later brought parts of Champa under Khmer control.
--- fact 3
He was a Mahayana Buddhist, while most earlier Khmer rulers had favoured Hindu cults.
--- fact 4
His building projects include Angkor Thom; the Bayon, its towers carved with large faces; Ta Prohm, dedicated to his mother; and Preah Khan, dedicated to his father.
--- fact 5
An inscription of 1186 at Ta Prohm credits him with 102 hospitals, a figure that comes from the king's own inscriptions.
--- fact 6
The faces on the Bayon towers are variously read as a Buddhist deity, the king himself, or both at once.
--- context
Jayavarman VII ruled the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] from [[place/angkor|Angkor]] in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and fought [[culture/champa|Champa]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Jayavarman VII took power after Angkor was sacked from outside, and answered with building on an unusual scale: temples for his parents, hospitals, and a walled city whose towers carry giant faces. His inscriptions present these works as merit-making by a Buddhist king, which doubled as a claim to legitimacy. The labour behind them appears in the record only as the king's gift, and the workers themselves are almost invisible. A specialist could argue that the program strained Angkor's resources, which would weaken a reading of it as simply benevolent.

=== person/jeong-seon
title: Jeong Seon
type: person
date: 1676 – 1759
themes: art
src: f04c4f2f1b81
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Jeong Seon, who lived from 1676 to 1759 under the pen name Gyeomjae, was a Korean painter of the Joseon dynasty who transformed landscape painting by depicting the real scenery of Korea instead of imagined Chinese scenery. His "true-view landscape" (jingyeong sansuhwa) expressed a growing pride in Korean culture.
--- fact 1
Born in 1676 in Hanyang, modern Seoul, into a scholarly family of modest means, he lost his father while young and trained in both painting and Neo-Confucian learning.
--- fact 2
Through recommendations he held government posts, including county magistrate at Hayang and Yangcheon, and rose to high official rank in old age.
--- fact 3
He lived near Mount Inwang in Seoul, and for decades his friend the poet Yi Byeong-yeon exchanged poems and paintings with him.
--- fact 4
In 1711 and 1712 he travelled to the Diamond Mountains, Geumgangsan, and the albums he made there made his name. He died in 1759 at 83.
--- fact 5
For centuries Korean painters had followed Chinese models; he painted the actual scenery of Korea instead, inventing his own brushstrokes — sharp vertical strokes for granite peaks, soft wet dots for forested earth mountains.
--- fact 6
He often combined a bird's-eye view with exaggeration; his paintings are not maps but expressions of the experience of real places.
--- fact 7
Two works, Geumgang jeondo and Inwang jesaekdo, are National Treasures of South Korea. Kim Hong-do and Sim Sa-jeong followed him, and the Gyeomjae Jeong Seon Art Museum opened in Seoul in 2009.
--- context
Jeong Seon worked in Joseon Korea, in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I think of a shift in whose mountains were allowed to count. For centuries the models were Chinese and idealised, and then Jeong Seon painted the actual granite of his own peninsula. I think of the friends who carried that change between them, trading poems and paintings back and forth, until pride in a local place became something a whole circle could share.

"True view" was never a measurement of the ground. It was an intention, carried in the brush.
