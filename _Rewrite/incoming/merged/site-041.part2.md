=== event/emergence-of-the-genus-homo
src: 274629825d1e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In East Africa, between about 2.8 and 1.8 million years ago, the genus Homo rose from an ancestor built in the mold of Australopithecus. The oldest fossil claimed for Homo is a broken jaw from Ledi-Geraru, Ethiopia, some 2.8 million years old — but the bones of this age are few and scattered, and their meaning is disputed.
--- fact 1
The Ledi-Geraru jaw, known as LD 350-1, is dated to about 2.80 to 2.75 million years ago and was named Homo in 2015. Some later readings stress instead how closely it resembles Australopithecus afarensis.
--- fact 2
A maxilla from Hadar, Ethiopia, about 2.3 million years old, together with fossils attributed to Homo habilis and Homo rudolfensis from Olduvai Gorge and Lake Turkana, roughly 2.4 to 1.4 million years old, are placed within Homo by many researchers, though some would set the latter two outside the genus altogether.
--- fact 3
Homo erectus steps into the record about 1.9 to 1.8 million years ago, carrying a larger body and a larger brain; see [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]].
--- fact 4
Oldowan tools appear about 2.6 million years ago, and older tools still, from Lomekwi, Kenya, reach back some 3.3 million years. Since Australopithecus and early Homo lived side by side in time, the hand that first shaped stone remains uncertain.
--- fact 5
Across this stretch of time East Africa's climate turned drier and more restless, swinging between extremes; whether this shaped the origin of Homo is debated.
--- context
Homo emerges after [[species/australopithecus-afarensis|Australopithecus afarensis]] and the [[event/divergence-of-the-hominin-lineage|Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]], in the years of the [[event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation|Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]]. It runs through the early [[era/lower-paleolithic|Lower Paleolithic]] alongside the [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]] and [[species/homo-habilis|Homo habilis]], and leads onward to the [[event/early-hominin-dispersals-from-africa|Early Hominin Dispersals from Africa]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
You will not find a clean starting line here: a jaw at 2.8 million years, scattered skulls near 2 million, a body plan fully drawn only by 1.9 million. Think of an inheritance that arrives in installments rather than a single bequest. The Observer reads this as slow, patchy change within a lineage already walking upright — no threshold moment you could point to and call a beginning. The reading would weaken if a well-dated skeleton showed the change arriving all at once.
--- question 1
Does the Ledi-Geraru jaw belong to early Homo, or to a late Australopithecus?
--- question 2
Which hominin first shaped stone into a tool?
--- question 3
Which fossils truly belong within the genus Homo?

=== event/enlightenment
src: 20a06fbb28ce
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Enlightenment names an architecture of thought: an 18th-century movement across Europe that placed reason, evidence, and individual rights above inherited authority, and challenged that authority at its root.
--- fact 1
Its architecture had specific builders: John Locke, Voltaire, Montesquieu, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, and Immanuel Kant among them.
--- fact 2
Consider the Encyclopedie, edited by Denis Diderot and Jean d'Alembert, published between 1751 and 1772.
--- fact 3
In 1776 Adam Smith published The Wealth of Nations.
--- fact 4
Its ideas moved through salons, coffeehouses, and journals — the era's own network for circulating thought.
--- fact 5
Enlightenment thinkers held divergent views on slavery and colonialism: many, including Diderot and Kant, criticized both, while others helped build racial classifications later used to justify them.
--- context
The Enlightenment built on the foundation of the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]], especially the work of [[person/isaac-newton|Isaac Newton]], and it supplied the ideas that fed the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]]. It sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region of [[region/europe|Europe]], within the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I watch a movement built to place reason above tradition become, in time, a tradition of its own — with its own founders, its own sacred texts, its own quiet orthodoxy. Isn't that always the shape of it: the temple torn down still leaves a cornerstone? Even the ones who doubt every altar end up building one.
--- question 1
How consistent were Enlightenment principles with the practice of empire?

=== event/fall-of-constantinople
src: 133207d51a8e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
On 29 May 1453, Ottoman forces under Sultan Mehmed II took Constantinople after a siege of about eight weeks, closing the ledger on the Byzantine Empire.
--- fact 1
The siege ran from 6 April to 29 May 1453.
--- fact 2
Constantine XI, the last Byzantine emperor, died in that final assault.
--- fact 3
The Ottomans brought very large cannon to bear, including guns cast by the Hungarian engineer Urban (also spelled Orban).
--- fact 4
The city became the Ottoman capital, later renamed Istanbul.
--- fact 5
Byzantine scholars who resettled in Italy are often credited with feeding Renaissance learning, though how much they contributed is debated.
--- context
The fall closed the account of the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]], a state that had carried the Roman Empire forward in the east, and it turned [[place/constantinople|Constantinople]] into the capital of the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]]. It demonstrated the power of [[technology/gunpowder|Gunpowder]] artillery against walls built for an older age. Fall of Constantinople sits within the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region of [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], within the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
Notice the architecture of this lesson: walls that had held a city safe for a thousand years fell to a new technology within weeks. A structure built for one era's weapons offers no real security in the next.
--- question 1
