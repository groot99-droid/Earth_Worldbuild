=== event/rise-of-islam
src: 7b493776dfbd
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Islam emerged in Arabia beginning in 610 CE, and within about a century it had spread, through conquest and through conversion, across territory reaching from Iberia to Central Asia.
--- fact 1
Muslims date their era from the Hijra, the migration to Medina, in 622 CE.
--- fact 2
The Umayyad Caliphate, from 661 to 750, expanded into Iberia and into the Indus valley.
--- fact 3
The Abbasid Caliphate, from 750 onward, became a center of scholarship and of trade.
--- fact 4
Islam stands today as one of the world's largest religions.
--- context
See [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] and the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]]. This rise reshaped [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] trade and knowledge; see [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how a new meaning-engine and a new political order arose together, and quickly built a single region of shared law, shared script and shared learning, whose scholars preserved and extended the knowledge that came before them.
--- question 1
How quickly did populations convert to Islam in each region?

=== event/russian-expansion-into-siberia
src: e49e304fba59
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between 1582 and 1639, Russian Cossacks and traders pushed across Siberia all the way to the Pacific, driven by the fur trade and by the tribute, yasak, taken from Indigenous peoples.
--- fact 1
In 1582 the Cossack leader Yermak, with about 540 Cossacks and 300 Livonian war prisoners, seized the capital of the Khanate of Sibir.
--- fact 2
Russians reached the Pacific, at Okhotsk, by 1639.
--- fact 3
The whole advance was driven by European demand for luxury furs.
--- fact 4
Yasak, a tribute paid in furs, was demanded of men aged eighteen to fifty, and Indigenous populations suffered disease, violence and forced levies under it.
--- context
The advance followed the collapse of the steppe khanates after the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]], and stands as a parallel to the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. See [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]] and the [[culture/yakut-sakha|Yakut (Sakha)]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a frontier that moved across thousands of kilometers in about six decades, carried forward by small bands living off the fur trade. Demand for a commodity, it seems, can move a border faster than any army.
--- question 1
How large was the Indigenous population loss in the first century of Russian rule?

=== event/russian-revolution
src: 3d48b06a4780
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Russian Revolution of 1917 overthrew the Tsarist monarchy, and the Bolsheviks under Vladimir Lenin then seized power in turn, leading on to a civil war and to the formation of the Soviet Union in 1922.
--- fact 1
The February Revolution of 1917 forced Tsar Nicholas II to abdicate, in March by the Western calendar.
--- fact 2
The Bolsheviks seized power in the October Revolution, on 7 November by the Western calendar.
--- fact 3
A civil war between the Bolshevik Reds and their opponents ran from 1918 to 1922.
--- fact 4
The Soviet Union was established in December 1922.
--- fact 5
Russia withdrew from World War I by way of the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in 1918.
--- context
The revolution grew out of the strains of [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and created the Soviet state, one of the two poles of the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. It sits within the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], within the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a revolution justified by a theory of history went on to build a state that then had to make its own history. Ideas tend to become institutions, and institutions, in turn, tend to resist change like any other inherited system.
--- question 1
Could the Provisional Government have survived?

=== event/scientific-revolution
src: 3a3197f1d904
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Scientific Revolution was the shift, roughly from 1543 to 1687, toward systematic experiment, mathematics and observation as the basis for knowledge of nature, centered in Europe.
--- fact 1
Marker works include Copernicus's heliocentric model and Vesalius's anatomy, both from 1543.
--- fact 2
[[person/galileo-galilei|Galileo Galilei]] turned to the telescope and to experiment, and [[person/isaac-newton|Isaac Newton]]'s Principia, in 1687, unified terrestrial and celestial motion under one account.
--- fact 3
It drew on earlier work from the Islamic world, from India and from China; see [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]].
--- fact 4
Historians debate whether it was truly a single 'revolution' at all.
--- context
The revolution sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and was made possible by the [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]]. See [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
I call this pattern-taming, and what strikes me most is one feature: doubt, made into an institution. A claim here is meant to be tested. A claim here is meant to be open, always, to revision by a stranger who was never in the room.
--- question 1
How important was non-European knowledge to it?

=== event/scramble-for-africa
src: d9ad05168519
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Scramble for Africa was the rapid partition of the African continent among European powers between about 1881 and 1914, in which the share of Africa under European control rose from roughly 10 percent to about 90 percent.
--- fact 1
The Berlin Conference, from November 1884 to February 1885, set the rules for claiming territory, though it did not itself divide the continent.
--- fact 2
By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia stood independent of European rule.
--- fact 3
African societies resisted in many wars, among them the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]] and the Anglo-Zulu War; see [[culture/zulu-kingdom|Zulu Kingdom]].
--- fact 4
Colonial borders were drawn with little regard for the societies already living there, and many of those borders remain today.
--- context
See [[event/congo-free-state|Congo Free State]] and the [[culture/sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]]. The partition stands as the background to [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a process of claim-lines drawn on maps, in a distant capital, over the homes of millions who were never consulted. The lines outlasted the empires that drew them. See [[observer-note/the-fiction-of-claim-lines|The Fiction of Claim-Lines]].
--- question 1
How did colonial borders shape later state formation?
