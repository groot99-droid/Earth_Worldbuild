=== place/kolyma
title: Kolyma
src: 59d0e0387d18
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kolyma is a remote region of northeastern Siberia, Russia, that from 1932 until the 1950s held the Soviet Union's largest system of forced-labor camps, where prisoners mined gold and other metals in extreme cold and many died. We should name it plainly, and not look away.
--- fact 1
Begin with the land itself: Kolyma lies along the Kolyma River in the far northeast of Siberia, where winter temperatures fall below minus 40 degrees Celsius.
--- fact 2
Gold was found on the Kolyma River in the late 1920s, and in 1931 the Soviet state created Dalstroy, a state trust that developed the region with the labor of prisoners from the Gulag system, the first of whom arrived in 1932.
--- fact 3
Among the prisoners were people convicted of ordinary crimes and of political offenses, many of them sent during the Great Terror of 1937 to 1938; hundreds of thousands of people passed through the Kolyma camps, and each of them was a whole life.
--- fact 4
Estimates of deaths in the Kolyma camps range from more than 100,000 to several hundred thousand, from hunger, cold, disease, overwork and executions; the full toll is uncertain, and that uncertainty is part of the ledger we inherit.
--- fact 5
Prisoners built the Kolyma Highway between Magadan and Yakutsk, known as the "Road of Bones" because of the deaths among its builders; the name itself has become a memorial.
--- fact 6
Varlam Shalamov, who was imprisoned in the Kolyma camps, wrote about them in the short stories known as the Kolyma Tales, testimony given the form of literature.
--- fact 7
Dalstroy was dissolved in 1957, and in 1996 a memorial, the Mask of Sorrow, was unveiled in Magadan.
--- context
Kolyma was a major site of the Gulag under the Soviet state that emerged from the [[event/russian-revolution|Russian Revolution]], in a region Russia had entered through the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]]. Notice where our knowledge comes from: much of what is known about the camps rests on survivors' writing and on archives opened after the [[event/collapse-of-the-soviet-union|Collapse of the Soviet Union]]. It sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]]. Testimony and archive: we need both.
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kolyma is a case of sanctioned harm carried out through economic administration. Prisoners were counted as labor for gold production, and their deaths were entered in ledgers; the Observer records those deaths as suffering. Understand what that means: the architecture of an administration can hold cruelty in columns, and the columns can look like ordinary bookkeeping.

The record of the place comes from two sources that do not always agree, survivors' writing and, after 1991, opened archives, which is why the death toll is given here as a range. The reading would weaken if fuller archival access shows that the camps served aims other than production more than this account suggests. A ledger can count the dead; it cannot account for them.
--- question 1
How many people died in the Kolyma camps, and how many lived through them?
--- question 2
How does Russia remember Kolyma today, within its own borders?

=== place/kuk-early-agricultural-site
title: Kuk Early Agricultural Site
src: bac27e8ea2ef
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Kuk Early Agricultural Site in the highlands of Papua New Guinea keeps, like the lines on an old palm, drainage ditches and gardens showing that people cultivated wetlands there for at least 7,000 years.
--- fact 1
The site lies in the Wahgi Valley of Papua New Guinea, and in 2008 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list.
--- fact 2
It is reported to bear the marks of agricultural use for at least 7,000 years, possibly reaching back as early as 10,000 years ago, like roots that go deeper than the eye can follow.
--- fact 3
Excavations trace a slow turning, as a river bends over ages: from exploiting taro and yam on wetland margins about 7,000 years ago to organized cultivation of bananas on drained ground about 4,000 years ago.
--- fact 4
It is one of the few places worldwide widely regarded as an independent center of plant domestication, a hearth kindled from no borrowed flame.
--- fact 5
The highlands of New Guinea were settled by people descended from those who reached Sahul more than 50,000 years ago, a long lineage climbing into the mountains.
--- context
Kuk shows that agriculture arose independently in Oceania, one more fire lit in parallel with [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in Southwest Asia and [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]] in East Asia. Its people belonged to the population that came through the [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]]. The site belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and to the theme thread of [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that here, farming was developed by people whose ancestors had been in place for tens of thousands of years. Let that unsettle a doctrine you may carry: that agriculture was a necessary next step for all societies. Kuk undermines it. Progress is not a single staircase.
--- question 1
