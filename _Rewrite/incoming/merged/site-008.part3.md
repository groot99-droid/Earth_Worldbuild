=== place/potosi
src: 2ea47f7010d8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Potosi is a mining city in the Bolivian Andes, founded in 1545 beside the silver mountain Cerro Rico, whose output supplied Spanish and world trade for centuries. Think of it as the place where a mountain became a ledger.
--- fact 1
Silver was discovered at Cerro Rico in 1545: the threshold from which everything else in this story follows.
--- fact 2
By around 1600 Potosi was one of the largest cities in the Americas, with population estimates around 150,000. That is the gravity a mountain of silver could exert.
--- fact 3
The Spanish organized labor through the mita, a forced-labor draft adapted from an Inca system. Name it plainly: the work was compelled.
--- fact 4
The mercury amalgamation process introduced in the 1570s increased silver output: a change of technique, and the mountain gave more.
--- fact 5
It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1987, a formal covenant of memory.
--- context
Potosi turned the conquered [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] into a source of silver for the world economy. Follow the metal and you trace the architecture of that economy: its silver crossed the Pacific through the [[event/manila-galleon-trade|Manila Galleon Trade]], and its labor system followed the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-inca-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]]. Potosi sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a labor system inherited from the conquered was repurposed to extract wealth for the conquerors. Consider what that means: the same institution, its lineage intact, now pointed the other way. A mountain that fed global trade became a symbol of how far an exchange-web could stretch, and of how much suffering could sit at its origin. The longest chain of trade still begins with someone's hands.
--- question 1
How many workers died in the mines of the silver mountain?

=== place/poverty-point
src: 592fbaeb3d4c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Poverty Point is a large earthwork complex in Louisiana, built by hunter-gatherer-fishers between about 1700 and 1100 BCE, its concentric ridges spreading like ripples held in earth and its huge mounds rising like the backs of sleeping beasts.
--- fact 1
The site holds six concentric earthen ridges and several mounds, the largest about 22 meters high, a hill raised by hands.
--- fact 2
According to current evidence, it was built by a society without agriculture, a people who took their living from the river and the wild.
--- fact 3
Stone and other materials were brought in from across the Mississippi valley, some from hundreds of kilometers away, the valley's far places flowing toward one hearth like streams.
--- fact 4
It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2014, and the old rings entered the world's long memory.
--- fact 5
Around 1100 BCE the site was abandoned, and the ridges were left to the silence.
--- context
Poverty Point is the earliest of the large earthwork centers of eastern North America, the eldest of its kind, rising long before [[place/cahokia|Cahokia]] and the [[place/chaco-canyon|Chaco Canyon]] settlements. It stands in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I was taught that monuments need farms. I was taught that the plow comes first and the mound after. And here is a large monumental site built by a people who did not farm, and the assumption breaks in my hands.

What else did I believe too quickly? It appears that large-scale cooperation can come from abundant wild food and shared purpose. Plenty and purpose can build as high as any harvest.
--- question 1
What was the site used for, with its rings and its mounds?

=== place/rapa-nui
src: 736df7468a2f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rapa Nui (Easter Island) is a remote Pacific island settled by Polynesians around 1200 CE, famous for nearly a thousand monumental stone statues called moai, a people of stone at the far end of the sea.
--- fact 1
The island lies in the southeastern Pacific, thousands of kilometers from the nearest large land, a mote of stone the ocean nearly forgot.
--- fact 2
The settlers' landfall drifts in the record like a canoe on dim water: estimates run from as early as about 300 CE, resting on contested early radiocarbon dates, to about 1200 CE, the date favored by many recent studies.
--- fact 3
About 400 moai (397 in one count) lie in the quarry of Rano Raraku in various stages of completion, giants half-woken from the stone, and about 95 percent of the island's monolithic statues were carved from its volcanic tuff. Published counts of the total number of moai differ.
--- fact 4
The familiar tale of an island that felled its own forests and so undid itself is contested. Researchers point instead to introduced rats, which may have been the main cause of deforestation, and to disease and slave raids. In December 1862 Peruvian slavers captured about 1,000 islanders, and reports put the total taken in 1862 to 1864 at more than 1,400. Most of the captives died in Peru; a smallpox epidemic that followed the return of a few survivors in 1863 reportedly killed about a thousand more. These figures come from popular summaries and vary between accounts.
--- fact 5
Rapa Nui National Park was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1995, the stone faces named a heritage of the world.
--- context
Rapa Nui was the far eastern edge of the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] and of the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]]' voyaging world, where the long paths of canoes met open sea. It stands in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
I was told, often, that this island was a parable. I was told its people unmade their own world. And I look at the record, and it suggests that outside pressures did much of the damage.

Why do I reach for the moral first? Because stories that fit a moral are chosen more readily than stories that fit the evidence. I would rather be told the truth than be taught a lesson.
--- question 1
How large was the population at its peak, and what brought about its decline?
