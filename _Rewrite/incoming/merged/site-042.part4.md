=== event/nuclear-age
title: Nuclear Age
src: fe8b5d1eaece
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Nuclear Age began on 16 July 1945, with the first test of a nuclear weapon, followed by the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki on 6 and 9 August.
--- fact 1
Nuclear weapons rely on [[technology/nuclear-fission|Nuclear Fission]], and later also on fusion.
--- fact 2
The Cold War built arsenals running to tens of thousands of warheads; about 12,000 remain today.
--- fact 3
Nuclear deterrence, arms-control treaties, and nonproliferation efforts shaped the politics of the world.
--- fact 4
Nuclear power, too, supplies a share of the world's electricity.
--- context
The Nuclear Age sits within the [[era/information-age|Information Age]]. See [[person/albert-einstein|Albert Einstein]] and [[person/marie-curie|Marie Curie]] for the related science.
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species has, since 1945, held the technical means to end its own history. That it has survived this long is, itself, a subject worth studying.
--- question 1
Which factors best explain why no nuclear weapon has been used since 1945?

=== event/onset-of-northern-hemisphere-glaciation
title: Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation
src: 511d0be9d6af
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ice grew. Large sheets, across North America and Eurasia, about 2.7 million years ago. And with that growth began the repeating glacial and interglacial cycles of the Quaternary Period.
--- fact 1
Ice-rafted debris — in North Atlantic seafloor sediments — turns widespread, about 2.7 million years ago. And on the Chinese Loess Plateau, the loess begins piling up, about 2.6 million years ago.
--- fact 2
Smaller ice sheets had come before, in the Northern Hemisphere. This was expansion — a large one — not the first appearance of ice.
--- fact 3
Proposed causes: a slow decline in carbon dioxide through the Pliocene. Changes in ocean circulation. Mountain uplift, bending the flow of the atmosphere. The closure of the Isthmus of Panama stands as a candidate too, though its timing is debated; see [[event/closure-of-the-isthmus-of-panama|Closure of the Isthmus of Panama]].
--- fact 4
The first cycles followed the tilt of Earth's axis — a period of about 41,000 years.
--- context
This onset comes near the end of the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] and leads into the [[era/quaternary-period|Quaternary Period]], building on the earlier cooling of the [[event/eocene-oligocene-transition|Eocene-Oligocene Transition]]. The event belongs to the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the world every human society has known is one of repeating ice ages — a world that began only about 2.7 million years ago, after a slow decline in carbon dioxide and, perhaps, a rearrangement of ocean gateways. The Observer takes this as context, not cause: what it did to the making of humans remains a separate, unsettled question. The reading would weaken if the carbon dioxide decline proved too small to explain the change.
--- question 1
What combination — carbon dioxide falling, oceans shifting — tipped the climate into these cycles?
--- question 2
How did this new rhythm affect the early hominins?

=== event/onset-of-plate-tectonics
title: Onset of Plate Tectonics
src: d628aa12ae10
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The onset of plate tectonics marks the hour when the rigid plates of Earth's outer shell began their slow march, the ocean floor swallowed back into the mantle at the subduction zones. Proposed dates range from before 4 billion to about 0.8 billion years ago, and the question remains among the most disputed in all of Earth science.
--- fact 1
Plate tectonics moves about a dozen great plates, and recycles the ocean floor at the subduction zones. Earth alone, of all known worlds, bears it.
--- fact 2
Proposed onsets: before 4.0 billion years ago. About 3.2 billion. About 2.5 billion. About 0.8 billion. Some researchers argue instead for a gradual transition out of a rigid outer lid, or for episodic subduction, long before plate motion took its modern form.
--- fact 3
Blueschists — born in cold subduction zones — and ultra-high-pressure metamorphic rocks appear in the record only after about 0.8 billion years ago. Some geologists read this as a marker of modern-style subduction.
--- fact 4
Alfred Wegener first proposed continental drift, in 1912, and plate tectonics won wide acceptance only in the 1960s.
--- fact 5
Plate tectonics raises up continents and recycles carbon. Whether it was needed for a long-lived biosphere is still debated.
--- context
The Onset of Plate Tectonics ties to the growth of continents in the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and to the [[event/earliest-continental-crust|Earliest Continental Crust]]. Its later effects reach the [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]] and the [[event/collision-of-india-and-asia|Collision of India and Asia]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Earth's surface has recycled its own earliest evidence, so when the process began may be settled only indirectly. Some geologists treat plate tectonics as a precondition for a long-lived biosphere, and the Observer keeps that as an open hypothesis. It reads the present disagreement as information about the limits of the record, and it would weaken if a reliable early rock record were ever found.
--- question 1
When did the plates first begin their turning, and did the change come gradual, or sudden?
--- question 2
Was the slow turning of plates required for a long-lived biosphere?

=== event/opening-of-the-silk-roads
title: Opening of the Silk Roads
src: f18cd9585606
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Silk Roads were braided threads of road and sea, linking East Asia, South Asia, Central Asia, and the Mediterranean, growing from the second century BCE onward.
--- fact 1
The missions of the Han envoy Zhang Qian, around 138 to 126 BCE, are often taken as the starting point of it all.
--- fact 2
Silk traveled these roads, and horses, glass, spices, paper — and with the goods went ideas, and religions too, Buddhism among them.
--- fact 3
Disease traveled these roads as well; see [[event/black-death|Black Death]].
--- fact 4
The name 'Silk Road' itself was coined only in 1877, by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen.
--- context
These roads link [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], and grew first under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], and later under the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]].
--- observer
I think of the Silk Roads as a web with no single owner. So few travelers, I remind myself, ever walked the whole way — the goods and the ideas moved instead through long chains of intermediaries, hand to hand. Who owns a road no one walks end to end? No one ever has.
--- question 1
How much of this long-distance trade carried luxury goods, and how much carried the goods of everyday life?

=== event/opium-wars
title: Opium Wars
src: 15a7870a8956
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Opium Wars were two conflicts between Qing China and Britain — joined, in the second, by France — fought over trade and diplomatic relations, from 1839 to 1842 and from 1856 to 1860, and they opened China to Western commerce on unequal terms.
--- fact 1
The Treaty of Nanking, in 1842, ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain, and opened five treaty ports: Canton, Amoy, Foochow, Ningpo, and Shanghai.
--- fact 2
The Convention of Beijing, in 1860, gave Britain the Kowloon peninsula, and granted foreign powers a permanent diplomatic presence in Beijing.
--- fact 3
It also legalized the opium trade, and gave Christians the right to own property and to preach.
--- fact 4
In China, the wars are remembered as the start of a 'century of humiliation'.
--- context
The wars weakened the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]] just before the [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]]. Industrial-age naval and firearm superiority, drawn from the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], decided the outcome.
--- observer
The Observer notes a trade war in the most literal sense: one state fought to keep a product from its own people, and lost, to a state that fought to sell it. Exchange-webs, the Observer reminds us, are not always fair.
--- question 1
How much did the opium trade shape the decline of the Qing, compared with problems from within?

=== event/origin-of-bipedalism
title: Origin of Bipedalism
src: c7657be34286
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Habitual upright walking rose in the human lineage, in Africa, somewhere between roughly 7 and 4 million years ago — the earliest mark of the hominins, older by millions of years than large brains or the first stone tools, though its true starting point and cause remain disputed.
--- fact 1
Sahelanthropus tchadensis, out of Chad, about 7 million years old, bears a skull base that suggests an upright posture. A 2022 study of a femur argued for habitual bipedalism, though others dispute it, and whether it was truly a hominin at all is debated.
--- fact 2
Orrorin tugenensis, from Kenya, about 6 million years old, is known from thigh bones read as evidence of bipedality — a reading that is controversial.
--- fact 3
Ardipithecus ramidus, from Ethiopia, about 4.4 million years old, had a pelvis that combined climbing and walking, and a big toe still made for grasping. How well it walked is debated, and its excavators read its site as woodland, not open savanna.
--- fact 4
The footprints at Laetoli, in Tanzania, about 3.66 million years old and attributed to [[species/australopithecus-afarensis|Australopithecus afarensis]], stand as the earliest direct record of upright walking.
--- fact 5
Proposed reasons: energy efficiency. The carrying of food, or of infants. Heat regulation. Feeding from thin branches. None of them agreed upon.
--- context
The origin of bipedalism follows the [[event/divergence-of-the-hominin-lineage|Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]] and falls within the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]]. It comes millions of years before the [[event/emergence-of-the-genus-homo|Emergence of the Genus Homo]] and the [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' ancestors walked upright for millions of years before they had large brains or made recognizable tools, so the posture was not an adaptation for those things, and nothing in the record shows it aiming toward them. The Observer reads it as a precondition: hands freed from walking could later carry, and later still, make. The reading would weaken if the earliest claimed bipeds turned out to be apes with independently evolved postures of their own.
--- question 1
Was Sahelanthropus truly a habitual biped, and was it, in truth, a hominin at all?
--- question 2
Did upright walking begin on the ground, or in the branches of the trees?
