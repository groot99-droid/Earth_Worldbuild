Razia Sultana (about 1205 to 1240) ruled the Delhi Sultanate from 1236 to 1240, the only woman ever to hold its throne. She was the daughter of Iltutmish, who by tradition had named her to follow him.

Her father, the sultan Iltutmish, reigned from 1211 to 1236. Tradition holds that he chose her as heir over his sons, though he seems to have chosen Ruknuddin Firuz shortly before his death.

When Iltutmish died in 1236 her half-brother Ruknuddin Firuz took the throne, but within months he was cast down, and Razia became sultan, borne up by the people of Delhi and some nobles.

According to chroniclers she held court in the open and led armies, and coins were struck in her name.

She raised Jamal ud-Din Yaqut, an Abyssinian former slave, to master of the stables, a promotion the Turkic nobles resented.

In 1240 Malik Altunia, governor of Bhatinda, rose against her. She was imprisoned at Bhatinda and then married him in September 1240; together they were defeated at Kaithal and killed in October 1240, and the accounts differ on the details, like footprints blurred by rain.

=== people--rumi--1
src: ac8b25bc3ef3
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Rumi (Jalal ad-Din Muhammad Rumi, 1207 to 1273) was a Persian-language poet and Sufi teacher who spent his grown years in Konya, in Anatolia. His long poem, the Masnavi, and his lyric poems are among the most widely read works of Persian literature.

He was born in 1207 in Balkh or nearby Wakhsh, in present-day Afghanistan or Tajikistan; sources differ. His family moved west, and by about 1228 he was living in Konya, in the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum.

Like his father, Baha ad-Din Walad, he trained as a scholar and preacher, and he taught in Konya.

In 1244 he met a wandering mystic, Shams of Tabriz. Their friendship changed his life as a flood changes a valley, and he began to write poetry. About 1248 Shams disappeared, and accounts differ: some say he was killed, others that he simply moved on.

He wrote the Masnavi, a long poem in six books, and thousands of lyric poems gathered in the Divan of Shams, mostly in Persian.

The Masnavi runs to some 25,000 to 27,000 couplets, counts differing by source, composed over his last decade or so at the request of his disciple Husam al-Din Chalabi, who wrote the verses down as Rumi recited them. Some later readers called it "the Quran in Persian".
