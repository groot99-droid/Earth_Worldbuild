=== source/science-2021-amazonian-reforestation-pollen-study
src: be5f5a0a837a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The finding here, drawn from 39 Amazonian fossil pollen records, is that there was no synchronous reforestation event after European arrival, and that at most sites land abandonment and forest regrowth began about 300 to 600 years before European arrival. The abstract frames this as a test of the hypothesis that a post-contact population collapse — an estimated 90 to 95 percent of Indigenous people in Amazonia — lowered atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610 CE through reforestation, and it names pre-European pandemics, social strife and environmental change as possible causes of the earlier abandonments.
--- extra 1: Caveats
Only the abstract was read here, the PubMed record fetched on 2026-09-25, together with the accepted-version supplementary materials in the University of Reading repository — methods and site tables, about 7,800 characters — which confirm that 39 lake-sediment records were used. Since science.org returns HTTP 403 and no open-access copy of the main paper was found, its results and discussion were not read. The reply from Alexander Koch and colleagues, and the statement that Bush and McMichael think populations peaked and declined well before 1492, both appear in the Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands note but are not in the text read here; they came from press coverage seen only as search-result summaries and remain unverified. The paper challenges one hypothesis, that post-1492 regrowth cooled the climate; it does not show that no post-contact depopulation occurred, and its abstract cites an estimated 90 to 95 percent loss of Indigenous people.
