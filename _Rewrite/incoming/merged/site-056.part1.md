=== technology/sanitation-and-sewers
title: Sanitation and Sewers
src: 639829f572a4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Every settlement keeps a covenant with its own waste: carry it away, or live beside it. Sanitation and sewers are the architecture that keeps that covenant, the systems carrying waste water and human waste away from where people live. The cities of the Indus Valley Civilization held that architecture already, with drains and wells by about 2600 BCE, and urban sanitation was later bound to public health science in the 19th century.
--- fact 1
Cities of the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], such as [[place/mohenjo-daro|Mohenjo-daro]], raised planned streets, brick-lined drains, and wells as early as about 2600 BCE.
--- fact 2
Minoan palaces such as [[place/knossos|Knossos]] on Crete carried drainage channels and clay pipes through their architecture by the 2nd millennium BCE.
--- fact 3
The Cloaca Maxima, the great drain of [[place/rome|Rome]], is traditionally dated to the 6th century BCE; rebuilt several times since, parts of its architecture still survive.
--- fact 4
In 1854, John Snow traced a cholera outbreak in Soho, London, back to a water pump on Broad Street, an early argument that disease itself could travel in water.
--- fact 5
London's main sewer system, designed by Joseph Bazalgette, won approval in 1858 after the Great Stink and stood largely complete by 1875.
--- context
Sanitation is the architecture linking the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] cities of [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] to the public health projects of the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/science|Science]]. The [[technology/aqueduct|Aqueduct]], by contrast, was the covenant of bringing clean water in.
--- observer
The Observer notes that sanitation is a technology whose success stays invisible: when its architecture works, nothing happens. Indus and Roman cities built their drains long before anyone could name the diseases those drains prevented, evidence that this tool-lineage ran ahead of pattern-taming, until mid-19th-century germ research finally supplied the explanation. The reading would weaken if the Bronze Age drains turn out to have served mainly rainwater and display rather than health.
--- question 1
Did the Indus drains serve health, rainwater, or prestige?
--- question 2
Why did sewer systems so often fall into neglect after conquest or collapse?

=== technology/seismoscope
title: Seismoscope
src: 9000860f8706
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A bronze instrument credited to Zhang Heng, court astronomer of the Han dynasty, said to detect the direction of a distant earthquake by letting a ball drop from a dragon's mouth into a toad's waiting mouth below.
--- fact 1
Zhang Heng (78-139 CE), an Eastern Han court astronomer, receives credit for presenting the device, the Houfeng Didong Yi, in 132 CE.
--- fact 2
Descriptions give it as a bronze vessel about 2 meters across, ringed by eight dragon heads, each holding a ball poised above a bronze toad below.
--- fact 3
Only one detailed account survives: the Hou Hanshu, compiled by Fan Ye in the 5th century CE, about three centuries after Zhang Heng died.
--- fact 4
No original device and no contemporary image survive; everything known about it rests on that later text alone.
--- fact 5
Reconstructions attempted since the early 1900s disagree on the internal trigger mechanism, and some historians doubt the device ever worked as described.
--- fact 6
It is often called the earliest known seismoscope, though that title rests on one textual source, not a surviving artifact.
--- context
The device belongs to the record of the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and, like other Han inventions, survives only as text passed down rather than as a surviving object. Threads: [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer reads this as pattern-taming turned toward seismic events, curiosity given the architecture of an instrument that doubled as a display of state competence. Because it survives only through memory-outsourcing, a single later chronicle rather than an artifact, the reading weakens if that chronicle proves embellished. An alternative reading treats the account as a legendary origin story for divination instruments, not a literal specification.
--- question 1
Did the device rely on a pendulum trigger, as most reconstructions assume?
--- question 2
Does 132 CE mark the device's invention, or only a later refinement?

=== technology/sericulture
title: Sericulture
src: 50e0a781e578
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sericulture is the raising of silkworms toward one end: silk thread. It began in China, where silk fragments from Qianshanyang in Zhejiang date to about 2700 BCE, and silk went on to become a leading good of Eurasian trade and diplomacy.
--- fact 1
Silk fragments from Qianshanyang, a Liangzhu culture site in Zhejiang, date to about 2700 BCE. Earlier claims, a fabric at Qingtaicun in Henan dated about 3630 BCE and possible silk protein at Jiahu, are treated here with more caution.
--- fact 2
The domesticated silkworm, Bombyx mori, traces its lineage to the wild moth Bombyx mandarina.
--- fact 3
From the 2nd century BCE on, the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] sent silk to the [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]] as gifts under its peace arrangements.
--- fact 4
Silk traveled west along the routes described in [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]].
--- fact 5
According to the Byzantine writer Procopius, two monks smuggled silkworm eggs to Emperor Justinian in 552 CE, hidden inside bamboo rods; the account comes from Byzantine sources, and its details are not independently confirmed.
--- context
Sericulture is an [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] technology of the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] whose products left their mark on the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] as well as on Chinese diplomacy. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer sees sericulture as a tool-lineage that could be held, for centuries, as a monopoly of knowledge: a craft resting on one insect and a few skills can stay walled inside one region for a very long time, and the smuggling stories show just how much that knowledge was worth. Silk also worked as a portable status-signal and as a currency of diplomacy. The reading would weaken if the secrecy proved to be later legend rather than practice.
--- question 1
How old is silk in China, really, and which of the early claims will hold?
--- question 2
How closely was silkworm rearing guarded, and by whom?

=== technology/steam-engine
title: Steam Engine
src: 7b3cca59597f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The steam engine is an architecture for converting heat into mechanical work. Thomas Newcomen built the first commercially successful engine in 1712, and James Watt's improvements, beginning in the 1760s, made it far more efficient.
--- fact 1
Early engines earned their keep pumping water out of coal mines.
--- fact 2
Watt's separate condenser, patented in 1769, greatly improved fuel economy.
--- fact 3
High-pressure engines, built by Trevithick in 1802, made railways and steamships possible.
--- fact 4
Steam power freed factories from their old dependence on rivers.
--- context
The steam engine powered the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and laid the groundwork for the [[technology/electrical-power-grid|Electrical Power Grid]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the first practical machine built to turn stored sunlight, banked as coal, into work. It stands as the first engine of the Great Acceleration.
--- question 1
Why did the steam engine emerge first in Britain, of all places?

=== technology/stirrup
title: Stirrup
src: 8454b1eda6f9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The stirrup is a foot support hung from a saddle, an architecture of mounting and balance. The earliest firm evidence is a single mounting stirrup shown on a Chinese figurine of about 302 CE, with paired stirrups following within decades.
--- fact 1
A ceramic figurine from a Western Jin tomb near Changsha, China, dated about 302 CE, shows a single stirrup meant only to aid mounting.
--- fact 2
The earliest known paired riding stirrups turn up in an Eastern Jin tomb near Nanjing, about 322 CE.
--- fact 3
The oldest surviving physical double stirrups come down to us from the 415 CE tomb of the Northern Yan noble Feng Sufu.
--- fact 4
Claims of earlier toe-loop mounting aids elsewhere remain debated; firm dated evidence begins in China.
--- fact 5
Stirrups spread from East Asia through steppe peoples to Byzantium and Europe over the centuries that followed.
--- fact 6
Lynn White's 1962 "stirrup thesis," linking stirrups to European feudalism, met challenge from the 1970s on and stands now largely rejected.
--- context
The stirrup follows earlier mounted and wheeled war technology, including the [[technology/chariot|Chariot]] built on [[technology/the-wheel|The Wheel]] and the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]], continuing a lineage rooted in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. Its contested link to European cavalry stands as a well-known case of overstated causation. Threads: [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer reads the stirrup as a modest tool-lineage advance later inflated into an oversized story: a mere strap credited with reorganizing sanctioned harm across a continent. It clearly aided mounted combat, but crediting it alone for feudalism resembles a meaning-engine narrative that has outrun the evidence. The reading would weaken if new finds showed true stirrups arising elsewhere, unconnected to China. An alternative view treats it as one convenience among several, not a sole cause.
--- question 1
By what route, and over what span of time, did stirrups travel from East Asia to Byzantium and western Europe?
--- question 2
Do the earlier South Asian toe-loop aids count as true stirrups?
