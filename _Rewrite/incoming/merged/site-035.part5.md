=== species/cotton
src: 991ae9f64abd
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cotton was domesticated independently twice, in the Indus Valley of South Asia and in the Americas. Its later mechanized processing in Britain and the American South tied the plant directly to the Industrial Revolution and to the expansion of slavery. A thread, you learn, can carry a whole architecture of power.
--- fact 1
Start with the thread itself: cotton textile fragments and spindle whorls from Mohenjo-daro in the Indus Valley date to the 3rd millennium BCE, and the date_start of -3000 marks this firmly dated use; mineralized thread at Mehrgarh, dated by some to the 6th millennium BCE, would be earlier but is less certain.
--- fact 2
In the Americas cotton was domesticated separately: Gossypium hirsutum in Mexico is dated to about 3400 to 2300 BCE, bolls from a Tehuacán cave to as early as 5500 BCE, and a different species was in use in Peru by the 7th to 6th millennia BCE.
--- fact 3
Consider the ledger of 18th-century world trade: Indian cotton textiles, especially from Bengal and Dhaka, were its most important manufactured goods and held about a quarter of the global textile trade, until British colonial tariffs and policy suppressed Indian exports and favored British cloth.
--- fact 4
Processing a bale of cotton by hand took over 600 hours of labor until Eli Whitney's gin, patented in 1793, cut that to about a dozen hours; the gin made large-scale growing far more profitable and drove a rapid expansion of slavery in the American South, where cotton was the backbone of the regional economy by the mid-19th century.
--- fact 5
India is the largest cotton producer today and the United States has long been the largest exporter; world output is roughly 25 million tonnes a year, grown on about 2.5 percent of the world's arable land.
--- fact 6
Cotton needs an estimated 8,000 to 10,000 liters of water per kilogram of fiber worldwide, and up to about 22,500 liters in drier regions of India; conventional growing relies heavily on fertilizers and pesticides, though pest-resistant Bt cotton has cut insecticide use in some regions.
--- context
Follow the fibre through its links and you see its whole genealogy. Cotton's earliest Old World cultivation is tied to the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], and its colonial-era displacement in favor of British manufacturing became central to the economy of the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]]. Its mechanized processing fed the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and the plantation slavery that helped bring on the [[event/american-civil-war|American Civil War]], and the enslaved labor that grew it in the American South is part of the wider [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. Cotton sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that cotton was domesticated independently in at least two hemispheres, so its usefulness was discovered separately, not diffused from one origin. Now consider the gin. A single invention turned a labor bottleneck into an incentive to enslave more people, a case where a tool-lineage advance amplified sanctioned harm instead of reducing it. We like to believe that machines lighten human burdens; here the weight was shifted onto the enslaved. Britain's suppression of Indian manufacturing in favor of its own industry shows, too, an exchange-web reshaped by state policy to benefit one side. The reading would weaken if the gin's role in expanding slavery proves small beside rising cotton prices and available land. A tool carries no ethics of its own; it inherits ours.
--- question 1
How independent were the Old World and American domestications of cotton, and did any early contact or diffusion pass between them?
--- question 2
How much of the decline of Indian cotton manufacturing reflects deliberate colonial policy, and how much British industrial efficiency alone?

=== species/donkey
src: dc66894087d7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The donkey was domesticated from the African wild ass in northeastern Africa, probably by pastoralists in Nubia, some 5,000 to 7,000 years ago, and it became the main pack animal of early long-distance trade in Egypt and Southwest Asia, the patient back beneath the early caravans.
--- fact 1
Donkeys descend from the African wild ass (Equus africanus) and were domesticated in Africa some 5,000 to 7,000 years ago, probably by pastoral people in Nubia; remains from the fourth millennium BCE have been found at Ma'adi in Lower Egypt, and the date_start of -4000 is the midpoint of that range.
--- fact 2
Donkeys were vital to long-distance trade in Egypt, and in the Fourth Dynasty (about 2675 to 2565 BCE) wealthy individuals were known to own more than 1,000 of them, herds like slow rivers of burden, used as pack animals, in farming, and for milk and meat.
--- fact 3
By the end of the fourth millennium BCE donkeys had spread into Southwest Asia; by about 1800 BCE the main breeding center had shifted to Mesopotamia, and the Greeks and Romans later carried them through their empires.
--- fact 4
A male donkey crossed with a female horse gives a mule, and the reverse cross a hinny; mules, children of two bloodlines, are valued as working and riding animals in many countries.
--- fact 5
More than 40 million donkeys were alive in the early 2000s, about 96 percent of them in developing countries, where they are used mainly to bear packs or to pull in draught work.
--- context
Donkeys served the trade networks of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] and were probably first kept by pastoralists in Nubia, the land later home to the [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]], before the [[species/horse|Horse]] and the [[species/dromedary-and-bactrian-camels|Dromedary and Bactrian Camels]] took over much of the long-distance carrying. They are part of [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]]. The donkey sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and its threads run through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a pattern worth your attention: the animal that made early caravans possible is seldom the one honored in a society's story-glue. Chariots and cavalry receive the poems; the donkey carried the grain, the copper and the cloth. The record, then, is weighted toward what humans admired, not toward what they relied on. Consider the Fourth Dynasty, where owning a thousand donkeys was a status-signal; that suggests carrying capacity was itself a form of wealth, a ledger kept in living backs. The reading would weaken if excavated caravan and settlement records show that horses or camels carried more early long-distance cargo than donkeys did. What bears the load rarely gets the song.
--- question 1
Was the donkey domesticated once or several times, and where exactly did the process begin?
--- question 2
How large was the donkey trade between Egypt and its neighbouring regions during the third millennium BCE?
