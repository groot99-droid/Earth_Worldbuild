=== place/robben-island
src: a2f5ccf8744f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Robben Island is a small island in Table Bay, about 7 kilometers off Cape Town, South Africa, best known for the political prisoners held there under apartheid, including Nelson Mandela. From the 17th century to 1996 it served as a place of banishment, a leprosy colony and a prison: an architecture of separation, built out of water.
--- fact 1
Robben Island lies in Table Bay about 7 kilometers from Cape Town and covers about 5 square kilometers; its name comes from the Dutch word for seals. Start there, with the plain geography of a small place surrounded by water.
--- fact 2
The Dutch East India Company began using Robben Island as a place of banishment in the 1650s; among those sent there, in 1658, was the Khoikhoi interpreter Autshumato, an early name in a long ledger of exile.
--- fact 3
A leprosy colony operated on the island from 1846 to 1931, and in the Second World War it served as a military base: the same isolation, turned to each new purpose.
--- fact 4
From 1961 to 1996 Robben Island was a maximum-security prison where the apartheid state held political prisoners, among them Nelson Mandela (imprisoned on the island for 18 of his 27 years, from 1964), Robert Sobukwe, Walter Sisulu and Govan Mbeki.
--- fact 5
Prisoners worked in a limestone quarry, and the glare damaged Mandela's eyes; prisoners also organized study and debate among themselves, and the prison was nicknamed a "university". Notice the practice: people held at a quarry face still made room for study.
--- fact 6
The last political prisoners were released in 1991, and the prison itself closed only in 1996.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Robben Island in 1999, and today former prisoners lead tours of the island: memory kept by those who carried it.
--- context
Robben Island was first used as a place of banishment by the [[event/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]], and its years as an apartheid prison belong to the wider struggle over minority rule in the period of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]]. Read its history as a genealogy of confinement. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Robben Island shows water used as a wall. For more than three centuries, authorities sent the people they wanted removed from society, exiles, patients and dissidents, to a place where isolation was cheap to enforce. That is the architecture of the island: not stone, but distance.

Yet the same isolation concentrated a generation of leaders who studied and argued together, so the prison also worked as a school and an exchange-web of ideas, an outcome its designers did not intend. The Observer records the years of imprisonment and forced labor as suffering, and does not let the school erase the cell.

The reading would weaken if accounts of prisoners' study and organization describe only a small, unrepresentative part of the prison population. What confinement gathers, it cannot always control.
--- question 1
How did prisoners on Robben Island organize themselves, and how far did that organization reach beyond the water?
--- question 2
How should the island tell its earlier chapters, such as the leprosy colony and the exile of Khoikhoi leaders?

=== place/rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela
src: 4b0d57e06afd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela are eleven churches carved down into volcanic rock in the highlands of northern Ethiopia, traditionally attributed to King Lalibela of the Zagwe dynasty in the early 13th century. They were drawn out of the earth like water from a well, and worship still fills them.
--- fact 1
Lalibela lies at about 2,500 meters in the Amhara Region of Ethiopia, high as a hawk's road, and holds eleven monolithic churches in two main groups, with one cross-shaped church, Bete Giyorgis, standing apart.
--- fact 2
Tradition names King Lalibela, who reigned around the late 12th and early 13th centuries, as their maker; some scholars propose that some churches are older, their roots running deeper than the king's name.
--- fact 3
Builders first cut trenches into the rock, then carved the exterior and interior of each church from the block left standing, as a sculptor frees a figure from the stone; trenches and tunnels joined the churches, and drainage channels were cut to carry water away.
--- fact 4
In tradition Lalibela is a "New Jerusalem", a distant holy city mirrored in the highlands like a face in still water, with features named after places in the Holy Land, such as a stream called the Jordan; the link with the loss of Jerusalem to Saladin in 1187 is traditional and is not documented in contemporary sources.
--- fact 5
The churches remain in active use, and at major festivals large numbers of pilgrims come, like a tide rising to the stone.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela in 1978, and the carved sanctuaries were named among the world's heritage.
--- context
The Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela were built under the [[culture/zagwe-dynasty|Zagwe Dynasty]], in a land shaped earlier by the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] and its town [[place/aksum|Aksum]], and the "New Jerusalem" tradition binds them to the age of the [[event/crusades|Crusades]]. They stand in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I think about removal. I think about workers who did not add rock but took it away, cutting free-standing churches down into the ground, a labor-heavy technique that also makes the buildings hard to destroy. I think about how making can look like subtraction.

And I think about longing. The "New Jerusalem" tradition shows a place made to stand in for a distant sacred site when access to the original was in doubt, a meaning-engine adjusting to political change. Have I not done the same, and built a near thing to hold a far one?

But the link to 1187 is not documented in contemporary sources, and if the churches prove to have been planned around a different rationale, my reading weakens. What we cannot reach, we sometimes build.
--- question 1
Which of the churches were cut in the reign of Lalibela, and which are earlier or later?
--- question 2
How many workers were involved, and how long did the rock take to yield its churches?
