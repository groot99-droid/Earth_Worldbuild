=== era/hadean-eon
title: Hadean Eon
src: 16c4efaf8e5a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Hadean is the informal first eon of Earth's history, the age between the planet's forming, about 4.54 billion years ago, and roughly 4.0 billion years ago. Earth lay molten like ore in a crucible, then cooled as embers cool under ash, and all the while the sky hurled stone upon it.
--- fact 1
Earth and the Moon took shape in the first tens of millions of years, two lamps kindled in the same young dark; see [[event/formation-of-the-solar-system-and-earth|Formation of the Solar System and Earth]] and [[event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon|Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon]].
--- fact 2
The oldest known Earth material is zircon crystals from the Jack Hills in Western Australia, dated to about 4.4 billion years ago: the deepest memory the stone has yet given up.
--- fact 3
Those zircons suggest that liquid water and some crust existed very early, as a single seed in the ash speaks of a field that once grew.
--- fact 4
The Hadean holds no official rank among ICS units; it is an informal name for the interval before the rock record, a name spoken at the fireside rather than carved above the gate.
--- context
The Hadean laid the footings for everything that came after: the Moon steadies Earth's tilt as a keel steadies a ship, and volatile-rich material delivered to the young planet gave it its oceans. The eon ends where the surviving rock record opens its first page, at the start of the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]].
--- observer
The Observer files the Hadean under preconditions, and so should you. Nothing here is a subject of study, yet every later fact leans on it: the planet's composition, its Moon, and its water. Think of a house's buried footings. The architecture forgets them; it never stops standing on them.
--- question 1
How early did stable oceans first hold their shores, and stable continents first stand above the water?
--- question 2
Did the stone rain fall as one Late Heavy Bombardment, or did the impacts ebb away gradually, like a tide going out?

=== region/andes
title: Andes
src: e581d00ddd81
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Andes are the mountain spine of western South America. In their highlands and along their coasts, societies raised complex states without wheeled transport or a conventional writing system, as rivers carve their canyons with no chisel but water.
--- fact 1
The [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] grew, as snowmelt gathers into a river, into the largest pre-Columbian state in the Americas.
--- fact 2
In the Andes, potatoes were coaxed from the soil and llamas from the wild, both domesticated to the service of the high country.
--- fact 3
The Inca kept their records in knots, on corded strands called quipu, memory tied fast as a fisher ties a net.
--- fact 4
The site of [[place/machu-picchu|Machu Picchu]], set like a crown upon the stone, dates to the 15th century.
--- context
Like an island in a sea of distance, the Andes stood cut off from the Old World until the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
--- observer
I came looking for writing. I watched a state coordinate millions of people; I watched it hand its memory to something that is not writing as most people define it. What else have I refused to call writing? Memory does not need ink.
--- question 1
What evidence, if it rose from the soil tomorrow, would most redraw the current picture?

=== species/archaeopteryx
title: Archaeopteryx
src: c5cae12f8a6b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Archaeopteryx was a feathered, winged dinosaur about the size of a raven, a small dragon dressed for the sky. It is known from Late Jurassic limestone in Bavaria, Germany, stone about 150 million years old that kept its form as still water keeps a reflection.
--- fact 1
Its fossils come from the Solnhofen limestone of Bavaria, Germany, stone that closed over them as a book closes over a pressed flower.
--- fact 2
It wore two natures at once: bird-like features such as feathers and wings, and reptile-like ones, among them teeth, clawed fingers, and a long bony tail.
--- fact 3
The first skeleton came out of the stone in 1861, two years after Darwin published *On the Origin of Species*, and it was quickly raised like a banner as a transitional form.
--- fact 4
From the 1990s onward, feathered non-avian dinosaurs found in China drew tighter the thread that binds birds to the theropod dinosaurs.
--- fact 5
Birds are the living descendants of theropod dinosaurs, the old line still aloft.
--- context
Archaeopteryx bears witness to the origin of birds within the [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]], and birds were the dinosaur lineage that survived the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], as one seed outlasts a hard winter. It was an early prop for the evolutionary ideas of [[person/charles-darwin|Charles Darwin]]. In the chronicle it dwells in the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], woven into the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the dinosaurs did not fully end. They are still on every continent; you simply call them birds. Look up the next time a wing crosses your window: that is the lineage, still flying. The species that studies extinctions lives among survivors of one of them, overhead. An ending can leave descendants.
--- question 1
Did Archaeopteryx beat its way aloft in powered flight, or could it only glide, like a leaf riding the wind?

=== place/angkor
title: Angkor
src: b6ddf2501e2d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Angkor was the capital region of the Khmer Empire in present-day Cambodia, a realm of stone laid upon water. Within it rose the temple of Angkor Wat, built in the early 12th century like a mountain lifted by human hands.
--- fact 1
Angkor Wat is the largest religious monument in the world by area, a sanctuary spread wide as a lake in flood.
--- fact 2
Aerial laser surveys reveal, as dawn reveals a valley, a vast urban area of roughly a thousand square kilometers.
--- fact 3
A network of reservoirs (barays) and canals ruled the water, holding it and loosing it like the slow breath of the city.
--- fact 4
It was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992. The traditional date of 1431, when Ayutthaya is said to have sacked Angkor and the city to have been abandoned, is now disputed; and Angkor Wat itself was never abandoned, its hearth never gone cold.
--- context
Angkor was the heart of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]], the still point its whole realm turned around. See [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]].
--- observer
I carried a picture of cities as compact clusters. I carried it to Angkor, and I watched it fail: here was a 'low-density' city, spread over a landscape of water systems and temples, and it challenged the picture I had brought. How many cities have I missed because they did not huddle? A city can be a landscape.
--- question 1
What caused the capital, like a banner struck and carried elsewhere, to shift away from Angkor?

=== place/auschwitz-birkenau
title: Auschwitz-Birkenau
src: 8812f1aa4062
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Auschwitz-Birkenau was a complex of Nazi German concentration and extermination camps near the Polish town of Oświęcim, operated from 1940 to January 1945, where at least 1.1 million people were murdered, about 960,000 of them Jews. Every one of those numbers is made of single lives.
--- fact 1
The complex grew in stages: Auschwitz was established in 1940 near Oświęcim, in Polish territory annexed by Germany, first for Polish political prisoners; the camp at Birkenau (Auschwitz II) was built from 1941 and became a center of mass murder in gas chambers from 1942; and the Monowitz camp (Auschwitz III) supplied forced labor to a chemical works of IG Farben.
--- fact 2
The ledger is this: about 1.3 million people were deported to Auschwitz and at least 1.1 million of them died; about 960,000 were Jews from across occupied Europe, and other victims included about 74,000 non-Jewish Poles, about 21,000 Roma and Sinti, and about 15,000 Soviet prisoners of war.
--- fact 3
For most victims the rail platform was the last threshold: they were killed on arrival, after selection there. Others died of forced labor, starvation, disease, shootings and medical experiments.
--- fact 4
Prisoners resisted, and the record must say so: in October 1944 members of the Sonderkommando revolted and destroyed a crematorium, and escapees, including Witold Pilecki in 1943 and Rudolf Vrba and Alfred Wetzler in 1944, carried reports of the killings to the outside.
--- fact 5
As Soviet forces approached in January 1945, the SS forced tens of thousands of prisoners on death marches; when Soviet troops liberated the camp on 27 January 1945, they found about 7,000 prisoners still there.
--- fact 6
Auschwitz-Birkenau became a state museum in 1947, and UNESCO inscribed the site in 1979; 27 January is observed as International Holocaust Remembrance Day, a covenant of memory kept each year.
--- context
Auschwitz-Birkenau was the largest site of the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]], carried out during [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. In the chronicle it sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and it belongs to two theme threads, [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]]. Both threads pass through this place.
--- observer
The Observer records Auschwitz-Birkenau as a place where a state murdered more than a million people, most of them Jews, on the basis of an ideology about descent. The Observer records those deaths as what they were: suffering and atrocity.

Look closely at the method, because the method is where the lesson sits. The killing was carried out with the tools of administration and industry: rail schedules, selection lists, factories and gas. What that shows is hard and plain. Tool-lineage and bureaucracy can be turned to sanctioned harm on a very large scale.

The preserved site, survivors' testimony and the perpetrators' own records now serve as memory-outsourcing, a ledger held against denial and forgetting, and the resistance of prisoners is written into that ledger too.

A historian could challenge this emphasis on administrative method by pointing to the role of ideology and of popular participation, which this account does not explain. Hold that objection beside the reading. To explain the machinery is not to have explained the people.
--- question 1
How were the figures for the dead reconstructed, when the perpetrators had destroyed the records?
--- question 2
As the last survivors die, how are their testimony and the physical site being preserved, so that the witness outlasts the witnesses?

=== culture/abbasid-caliphate
title: Abbasid Caliphate
src: 6d82bbbd1789
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Abbasid Caliphate ruled much of the Islamic world from 750 CE, holding its court at Baghdad as a delta holds a river's gathered waters, until the Mongols sacked the city in 1258.
--- fact 1
Baghdad was founded in 762 and swelled, as a river swells in the flood season, into one of the world's largest cities.
--- fact 2
The House of Wisdom and the translation movement drew on Greek, Persian, and Indian works, as one well is fed by three springs.
--- fact 3
Paper manufacturing spread through the caliphate like water finding its channels.
--- fact 4
From the 9th century political control fractured like river ice in a thaw, and regional dynasties took power among the pieces.
--- context
See the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] and [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]]. Its end rode in with the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], as a storm ends a long summer.
--- observer
I think of a state that gathered scholars from many traditions into one city. I think of the work that followed, work that neither tradition would have made alone. What have I never made, working alone? The new thing is born between.
--- question 1
How much of the translation effort flowed from the state, and how much welled up of its own accord?

=== event/armenian-genocide
title: Armenian Genocide
src: d6c90e3781d8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Armenian Genocide was the deliberate destruction of most of the Ottoman Empire's Armenian population during World War I, carried out through mass killings, forced deportations, and death from starvation and exposure. Many countries and historians recognize it as genocide, and the government of Turkey denies it; we have to hold both of those sentences at once.
--- fact 1
On 24 April 1915 Ottoman authorities arrested about 240 Armenian leaders in Constantinople, among them writers, doctors, religious leaders and political figures, and deported them eastward; that date is now kept to commemorate the victims.
--- fact 2
The deportations began at the edge and did not stay there: in spring 1915 the Ottoman government began deporting Armenians from the northeastern border regions, then extended deportations to almost every province, including regions far from combat.
--- fact 3
Victims died in local massacres; they died on forced marches, of starvation, dehydration, exposure and disease; and they died in the deserts of the southern empire, or on the way there.
--- fact 4
Estimates of the number killed run from at least 600,000 to more than 1,000,000; the exact number is not settled, and the ledger remains open.
--- fact 5
The Turkish state has long denied that the events were genocide and has worked against official recognition abroad; more than twenty countries formally recognize the genocide, and Uruguay was the first, in 1965.
--- context
The genocide took place inside the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] during [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. It is often studied alongside the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]], and if you want the vault's treatment of organized killing, you will find it in [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer records this as an atrocity: a state deliberately destroyed much of one of its own peoples. Say it that plainly; plainness is the least the record owes the dead. The Observer also records that the denial has itself lasted a century, so that the question of what happened has become bound up with the question of who is permitted to say it. After this long, what happened and who may say so are one question.
--- question 1
How many people died, and how should we count the deaths on the deportation routes?
--- question 2
How have the surviving communities and their descendants carried memory down the generations under conditions of official denial?

=== event/permian-triassic-extinction
title: Permian-Triassic Extinction
src: 24312436bf48
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Permian-Triassic extinction, about 251.9 million years ago, was the largest mass extinction in Earth's history. It killed an estimated 80 to 96 percent of marine species. Eight in ten, at the least. Nearly all, at the worst. Gone.
--- fact 1
The leading cause: enormous volcanic eruptions in Siberia. The Siberian Traps. Opening. And opening. And opening.
--- fact 2
What followed, among other things: rapid warming. Acid in the ocean. Oxygen bleeding out of the seas.
--- fact 3
It ended the dominance of trilobites. Then it ended the trilobites.
--- fact 4
Full ecological recovery took roughly ten million years. That long. That slow.
--- context
It is the line where the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]] ends and the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]] begins. A border drawn in the dead. See [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]].
--- observer
The Observer regards this as the nearest the biosphere has come to ending. Sit with that phrase before you move on. Its cause was a carbon-releasing planetary change, and it bears an uncomfortable resemblance to a process the species is now carrying out. The deep past is not a prophecy. It is a precedent, and precedents are uncomfortable to read.
--- question 1
How fast did the heat come? How fast the acid?

=== person/ada-lovelace
title: Ada Lovelace
src: a4b9590aed81
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ada Lovelace (1815 to 1852) was an English mathematician who wrote extensive notes on Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine, and inside those notes sits what is often regarded as the first published computer algorithm. Think of it as a blueprint for a building no one had raised: the architecture arrived before the house.
--- fact 1
Her lineage ran through poetry: she was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron.
--- fact 2
Her 1843 notes described how the engine could compute a sequence of Bernoulli numbers, a grammar of steps set down in ink.
--- fact 3
She suggested that such a machine might manipulate symbols beyond numbers, such as music, which carries the idea past the threshold of arithmetic.
--- fact 4
The extent of her original contribution, measured against Babbage's, is debated; the ledger of credit is still open.
--- context
Lovelace's ideas anticipate the [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]] and the [[event/digital-revolution|Digital Revolution]], as a threshold anticipates the room beyond it. Set her beside [[person/alan-turing|Alan Turing]] and compare.
--- observer
I keep returning to it: the concept of programmable machines came a century before the machines. I keep returning to an idea waiting at the door. What have I imagined that my tools cannot yet hold? Ideas can outrun their tools.
--- question 1
What was her role in the architecture of the algorithm itself?

=== technology/antibiotics
title: Antibiotics
src: 002c2b2b2cd9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Antibiotics are drugs that kill or inhibit bacteria, and their story turns on a threshold: Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928, and mass production began in the 1940s.
--- fact 1
Howard Florey and Ernst Chain developed penicillin for medical use, and the three shared the 1945 Nobel Prize: discovery and development, honored together.
--- fact 2
Antibiotics greatly reduced deaths from infections and from surgery, rewriting the ledger of ordinary risk.
--- fact 3
Overuse has caused widespread antibiotic resistance; a gift spent carelessly wears thin.
--- fact 4
Life expectancy rose sharply in the 20th century, and antibiotics were one pillar of that architecture, beside sanitation and vaccines.
--- context
Read this beside [[technology/vaccination|Vaccination]] and the [[event/black-death|Black Death]]. Antibiotics changed the architecture of medicine in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species can now treat plagues that once killed a third of a continent. Feel the weight of that threshold before you pass it. The Observer also notes that overuse is a new problem, an arms race with bacteria. No victory over a living enemy is final.
--- question 1
How, in practice, can antibiotic resistance be slowed?

=== observer-note/fire-then-everything
title: Fire, Then Everything
src: a790fb8494e5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Is there one change, one threshold, that best explains how the species rose so rapidly in power over other animals?
--- observer
I call it heat-domestication, the control of fire. I call it the first time the species used an energy source outside its own body. I call it a pattern, because every later step in its power, from smelting to steam to the power grid, repeats it: find a stored source of energy and learn to release it on demand.

Is that a triumph? It is. Is it the root of a modern problem? It is that too, because the same principle now warms the planet. I do not know whether to kneel to it or to fear it. The first flame and the warming sky are one fire.
--- extra 1: Question
Can any single change best explain the species' swift rise in power over other animals?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- The evidence for controlled fire reaches back at least a million years; see [[event/control-of-fire|Control of Fire]].
- Cooking makes food safer and easier to digest, and it may have helped build larger brains.
- Fire lengthens the day, gives protection, and gathers people into a circle, an architecture of communion.
- Later technologies, from pottery to metallurgy, rest on heat.
- The [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] took the same principle and scaled it up, with coal and the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]].
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
Be honest about the frame: this essay simplifies. Language, cooperation, and cumulative learning are also candidates for the deepest cause of human power. No single thread holds the whole weave.
