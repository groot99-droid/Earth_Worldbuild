=== event/portuguese-sea-route-to-india
src: 551d08998cef
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Vasco da Gama's fleet made landfall at Calicut in India on 20 May 1498, having sailed clear around Africa, and so opened a direct sea road from Europe to Asia.
--- fact 1
Da Gama set out from Lisbon in July 1497 and rounded the Cape of Good Hope that November.
--- fact 2
He reached Calicut, on the Malabar Coast, in May 1498.
--- fact 3
The Portuguese armed their ships with cannon and later seized ports of their own, Goa among them in 1510.
--- fact 4
Their entry gave Europe a direct hand into the spice trade of Asia, with no Middle Eastern intermediary standing between.
--- fact 5
The Indian Ocean had already been crossed by great Chinese fleets under Zheng He, whose voyages had ended by 1433.
--- context
This voyage forged a new link in the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] and, alongside the voyages behind the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], began direct European trade across the oceans. The Chinese voyages of [[person/zheng-he|Zheng He]] had already reached these same waters decades before. It sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], within the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the newcomers entered an ocean already busy, one with no dominant navy of its own, and made it dangerous with their cannon-armed ships. What changed was less the route itself than the will to fight for control of it.
--- question 1
How much did the new route change the spice trade?

=== event/protestant-reformation
src: fc4f813ccf7b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Protestant Reformation began in 1517, when Martin Luther publicized his Ninety-five Theses against the practices of the church, and it split Western Christianity into Catholic and Protestant churches.
--- fact 1
Luther sent his Ninety-five Theses to Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz in October 1517; the tradition that he nailed them to a church door remains uncertain.
--- fact 2
He was summoned before the Diet of Worms in 1521, and there refused to recant.
--- fact 3
His German New Testament appeared in 1522, and the whole Bible followed in 1534.
--- fact 4
Other reformers included Huldrych Zwingli and John Calvin, and Henry VIII established the Church of England in turn.
--- fact 5
The religious wars that followed ran on into the Thirty Years' War, ending in 1648.
--- context
The Reformation used the [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]] invented by [[person/johannes-gutenberg|Johannes Gutenberg]] to spread pamphlets and translated Bibles. Its conflicts ended in the [[event/thirty-years-war|Thirty Years' War]] and the [[event/peace-of-westphalia|Peace of Westphalia]]. It sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], within the theme thread of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I see a new way of copying text turn a quarrel among clerics into a movement of multitudes, and it took only a few years. Once the engines of meaning can be copied cheaply, rival versions do not stay few. They multiply.
--- question 1
How central was printing to the Reformation's success?

=== event/quaternary-megafauna-extinctions
src: 8248ce1b4a49
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between roughly 50,000 and 10,000 years ago, the biggest animals on land began to vanish. A large share of them. The timing differed by continent. Often, the disappearances followed close on the arrival of humans.
--- fact 1
The woolly mammoth. The woolly rhinoceros. The giant ground sloths. The glyptodonts. The giant marsupial Diprotodon. The giant bird Genyornis. All lost.
--- fact 2
The losses cut deepest, proportionally, in Australia. In the Americas. Across northern Eurasia.
--- fact 3
Africa lost fewer, proportionally speaking, which is often explained by its large animals having evolved alongside hominins all along.
--- fact 4
One hypothesis blames human hunting. The overkill hypothesis. Another blames the shifting climate. Two explanations, still standing side by side.
--- fact 5
Recent global analyses tie the declines more closely to the spread of Homo sapiens than to the climate, though regional debate continues.
--- context
The extinctions ran alongside human expansion into the [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]] and the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]], and alongside the retreat of the [[species/woolly-mammoth|Woolly Mammoth]] amid the shifts around the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. It sits in the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], within the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the same period in which the species reached new continents is the period in which the largest animals on those continents disappeared. Whether the species was the primary cause remains contested, but this is the first moment at which its impact on other life grew large enough to be named a candidate cause at all.
--- question 1
How much of each regional extinction was due to humans and how much to climate?

=== event/radiation-of-placental-mammals
src: c93d745d2b2d
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
After the end-Cretaceous extinction, the placental mammals radiated fast. From about 66 to 50 million years ago, they filled every space left empty. Most living mammals belong to that radiation.
--- fact 1
Placental mammals bear their young well-formed, fed through a placenta before birth. Rodents. Bats. Whales. Hoofed animals. Primates. Most living mammal species are placentals.
--- fact 2
In the fossil record, large herbivores appear within a few million years of the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]. Large predators too. By about 50 million years ago, many of the modern orders are already recognizable.
--- fact 3
Molecular-clock studies push the origin of the placental orders back into the Cretaceous. The oldest fossils assigned to most modern orders, though, are Paleogene. Explosive. Short-fuse. Long-fuse. All three timing models are argued still.
--- fact 4
The [[event/paleocene-eocene-thermal-maximum|Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum]], about 56 million years ago, lines up with the first appearance, in the Northern Hemisphere, of primates, of horses, of artiodactyls.
--- fact 5
Whales evolved from land-dwelling hoofed mammals over about ten million years, from about 50 million years ago to about 40 million years ago.
--- context
This radiation follows the [[event/origin-of-mammals|Origin of Mammals]] and the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], and fills much of the [[era/paleogene-period|Paleogene Period]] of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]]. It belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the mammals rose only after the dominant land animals were lost, and that this same pattern, vacated space filled afterward, follows other extinctions too. This reading treats the radiation as a consequence of an opening, not of any inherent superiority. It would weaken if the fossil record showed large placental mammals already common before the extinction.
--- question 1
Did the modern orders arise in the Cretaceous or only after the extinction?
--- question 2
What role did warming episodes such as the thermal maximum play in the radiation?

=== event/renaissance
src: b486eb13e13a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Renaissance was a movement of art, learning and political thought, born in Italian city-states such as Florence in the 14th and 15th centuries and spreading from there across all of Europe.
--- fact 1
Italian humanists such as Petrarch recovered classical Latin and Greek texts, and pored over what they found.
--- fact 2
Filippo Brunelleschi worked out linear perspective in Florence through experiments of about 1410 to 1415, and Leon Battista Alberti set it down as doctrine in 1435.
--- fact 3
Leonardo da Vinci (1452 to 1519), Michelangelo (1475 to 1564) and Raphael (1483 to 1520) labored under the patronage of wealthy families and of the Church.
--- fact 4
The Medici family of Florence, grown rich on banking, poured that wealth into artists and scholars.
--- fact 5
The very idea of a distinct age of rebirth was popularized only in the 19th century, notably by Jacob Burckhardt in 1860, and historians still debate how sharp a break it truly made from the medieval period before it.
--- context
The Renaissance revived the heritage of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]] and was carried further by the [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]]. It overlapped the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]]. It sits within the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], within the theme thread of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
I notice that an age named itself a rebirth of an older age, and that the historians who came after simply kept the name. A culture may choose its own ancestor, I think, and half-invent the very thing it claims to have inherited.
--- question 1
Was the Renaissance a break with the Middle Ages or a continuation?
