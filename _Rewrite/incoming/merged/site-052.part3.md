=== person/sun-yat-sen
title: Sun Yat-sen
src: d2ba4101a58b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sun Yat-sen (1866 to 1925), a Chinese revolutionary and physician, worked for decades to overthrow the Qing dynasty and became the first provisional president of the Republic of China in 1912; both mainland China and Taiwan honor him as a founder.
--- fact 1
Sun Yat-sen was born on 12 November 1866 in Cuiheng, Guangdong, and died on 12 March 1925 in Beijing; he was educated in Hawaii and trained as a doctor in Hong Kong, graduating in 1892.
--- fact 2
He founded the Revive China Society in 1894, after an earlier reform petition was rejected, and from 1895 onward organized several failed uprisings against the Qing.
--- fact 3
In Tokyo in 1905 he founded the Tongmenghui, which grew to about a thousand members, and there set out his Three Principles of the People: nationalism, democracy and people's livelihood.
--- fact 4
The Wuchang uprising of 10 October 1911, which he did not lead in person, began the revolution that ended the Qing dynasty; he returned from abroad and served as provisional president from 1 January 1912, resigning that March to Yuan Shikai.
--- fact 5
He rebuilt his Kuomintang party in 1919, and in 1923 allied it with the Soviet Union and the small Chinese Communist Party.
--- fact 6
In 1904 he obtained a certificate claiming birth in Hawaii in 1870, which he later renounced; his standard birth year and place are beyond doubt.
--- context
Sun Yat-sen worked in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and the [[event/xinhai-revolution|Xinhai Revolution]] of 1911, which he had long prepared for, ended the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that decades of failed uprisings built a network and an ideology, story-glue held in the Three Principles, that outlasted any single attempt at sanctioned harm. He was abroad when the revolution he had long prepared for finally succeeded, and he held power only briefly before yielding it. A specialist could argue that his greatest effect was organizational and rhetorical rather than military, which would weaken any reading that credits him with the 1911 victory itself.
--- question 1
How much did his organizing directly cause the 1911 uprising, given that he was not present for it?
--- question 2
What exactly did his 1923 alliance with the Soviet Union and the Communists commit his party to?

=== person/sundiata-keita
title: Sundiata Keita
src: 528ffde52170
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sundiata Keita (about 1217 to 1255) founded the Mali Empire, and is known largely through oral tradition, especially the Epic of Sundiata.
--- fact 1
Tradition says he defeated the ruler Sumanguru at the Battle of Kirina around 1235.
--- fact 2
Griots, hereditary oral historians, preserved and performed his story.
--- fact 3
Written sources on him are few, coming mainly from Arabic writers such as Ibn Khaldun.
--- fact 4
Historians handle the epic's details with caution.
--- context
Sundiata is the founding figure of the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]]; his successors included [[person/mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a founder's story survives here in performance, not in text; the epic is memory-outsourcing to a human institution, the griot, who has carried it for centuries.
--- question 1
How much of the epic actually reflects historical events?

=== person/sunni-ali
title: Sunni Ali
src: ee39c99daa82
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sunni Ali (ruled about 1464 to 1492) turned Songhai from a small state on the Niger River into a West African empire, taking Timbuktu in 1468 as the Mali Empire declined; his birth year is unknown.
--- fact 1
Sunni Ali became ruler of Songhai about 1464, the fifteenth in the Sunni dynasty.
--- fact 2
He conquered Timbuktu in 1468 and, after a long siege, took the trading city of Djenné as well, exploiting the weakening Mali Empire.
--- fact 3
He was nominally Muslim but tolerated older local religious practice, and this drew tension with the Muslim scholars of Timbuktu, whose chronicles remember him harshly.
--- fact 4
He died in 1492; accounts differ on whether he drowned while crossing a river or died some other way.
--- fact 5
His son Sunni Baru succeeded him but was overthrown in 1493 by Askia Muhammad, a general who accused Sunni Baru of insufficient devotion to Islam and founded a new dynasty of his own.
--- context
Sunni Ali built the [[culture/songhai-empire|Songhai Empire]] in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], seizing [[place/timbuktu|Timbuktu]] as the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]] weakened. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the harshest account of Sunni Ali comes from the Muslim scholars of the city he conquered, whose meaning-engine he never fully adopted, so the record of his reign carries their grievance along with the facts. His death drew two different stories within a single generation, a sign of how quickly detail slips even for a ruling conqueror. A specialist could argue that the Timbuktu chronicles' harsh portrait reflects the scholars' own resentment more than his actual governance, which would weaken any reading of him as simply irreligious.
--- question 1
How did Sunni Ali actually die, and why do the accounts differ?
--- question 2
How much of his reputation for irreligion reflects the political grievance of Timbuktu's scholars, rather than his actual practice?

=== person/tamar-of-georgia
title: Tamar of Georgia
src: f61df2ed0508
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tamar (born about 1160 or 1166, died 1213) ruled the Kingdom of Georgia from 1184 to 1213, presiding over its political and cultural high point, later named the Georgian Golden Age.
--- fact 1
She was crowned co-ruler with her father, George III, in 1178 to secure her succession, then became sole monarch on his death in 1184, ruling under the formal title "king of kings" rather than any feminine equivalent.
--- fact 2
Her armies won major victories against regional Muslim powers, including the Battle of Shamkor in 1195 and the Battle of Basian (dated to 1202, 1203 or 1204 depending on the source), expanding Georgian influence across much of the South Caucasus and into parts of Armenia and Anatolia.
--- fact 3
Her reign saw significant patronage of Georgian literature and architecture, including the era's most celebrated work, Shota Rustaveli's epic poem "The Knight in the Panther's Skin," traditionally associated with her as its inspiration.
--- fact 4
She was canonized as a saint by the Georgian Orthodox Church, and centuries of later Georgian tradition remember her reign as the kingdom's greatest period of security and cultural achievement.
--- context
Tamar ruled Georgia in the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] region during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], expanding Georgian power at the expense of neighboring Muslim states. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Tamar's title, "king" rather than "queen," suggests that Georgian tradition saw sovereignty itself as inherently masculine, even while accepting a woman as its holder — a distinction that separates the office from the person occupying it more sharply than modern titles usually do.
--- question 1
How much of Georgia's "Golden Age" reputation reflects Tamar's own governance, and how much reflects favorable regional conditions, including the temporary weakness of neighboring powers?

=== person/teddy-roosevelt
title: Teddy Roosevelt
src: ae7b9d362ca2
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Theodore "Teddy" Roosevelt (1858 to 1919), the 26th President of the United States, served from 1901 to 1909, known for trust-busting antitrust policy, an assertive foreign policy, and a major expansion of federal land conservation.
--- fact 1
He became president in 1901 after the assassination of William McKinley, at 42 the youngest person to hold the office, and was elected in his own right in 1904.
--- fact 2
He pursued antitrust action against major corporate combinations, including a successful suit against the Northern Securities Company railroad trust in 1904, and he mediated the 1902 coal strike partly by threatening to send federal troops in to run the mines.
--- fact 3
He established the United States Forest Service and used his executive authority to create national forests, wildlife refuges and national monuments, protecting roughly 230 million acres of land during his presidency.
--- fact 4
After leaving office he attempted a comeback in the 1912 election on a progressive "Bull Moose" third-party ticket, splitting the Republican vote and helping elect Woodrow Wilson instead.
--- context
Theodore Roosevelt led the United States, in [[region/north-america|North America]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Roosevelt's conservation legacy, protecting land at a scale no predecessor had attempted, grew from the same expansive view of executive power that let him intervene directly in labor disputes and corporate affairs; the same instinct toward vigorous federal action that alarmed his political opponents also produced the national park and forest system many now take for granted.
--- question 1
How much of Roosevelt's conservation program could have been achieved through Congress alone, without his willingness to act through executive authority?

=== person/timur
title: Timur
src: 1e13ef26d898
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Timur (Tamerlane, traditionally dated 1336 to 1405) was a Central Asian conqueror who built the Timurid Empire and made Samarkand its centre.
--- fact 1
Timur's birth year is contested: 1336 is the traditional date, but many contemporary accounts imply the late 1320s; he was born near Kesh (now Shahrisabz, Uzbekistan).
--- fact 2
By about 1370 he had besieged his rival Amir Husayn at Balkh and, after Husayn's assassination, proclaimed himself sovereign of the Chagatai line of khans, with dominion over Transoxiana.
--- fact 3
He was not a descendant of Genghis Khan and did not take the title khan; he ruled as amir through a puppet Chaghatayid khan, Suyurghatmish, and took the title güregen, royal son-in-law, through his marriage to Saray Mulk Khanum, of the Chinggisid line.
--- fact 4
His armies campaigned in Persia, Iraq, the lands of the Golden Horde, India (Delhi was sacked in 1398), and Anatolia, where he defeated the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402.
--- fact 5
Later chronicles describe massacres in the cities he conquered; their casualty figures are contested (see Open Questions).
--- fact 6
He died in February 1405 at Otrar (also called Farab), in present-day Kazakhstan, while preparing an invasion of Ming China; sources differ on the day, 17 to 19 February, and he was buried in the Gur-e-Amir in Samarkand.
--- context
Timur founded the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]] and presented himself as a restorer of the Mongol empire of [[person/genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]]. He rebuilt [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]], where he deported artisans from conquered cities.
--- observer
The Observer notes a conqueror who could not claim descent from the earlier conqueror he admired, and so bought the link instead: a Genghisid wife, a puppet khan, and the title of son-in-law. Legitimacy borrowed through marriage rather than blood is still a form of ancestor-weight, and it suggests that the pull of the earlier line was strong enough to be worth acquiring.
--- question 1
How reliable are the casualty figures in accounts of his campaigns? Chronicle figures for single cities run from tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand, and modern scholars estimate millions of deaths overall, though none of these numbers is well established.
--- question 2
When was he actually born, in the late 1320s, or in 1336?
