=== culture/zoroastrianism
title: Zoroastrianism
src: 8ab415105d5b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Zoroastrianism took its teaching from Zarathustra, an Iranian prophet, and set at its center the god Ahura Mazda and an unending contest between truth and the lie.
--- fact 1
The oldest scripture, the Gathas, are hymns attributed to Zarathustra, sung in an archaic tongue of ancient Iran.
--- fact 2
Zarathustra's own age is unknown, for scholars have set his life anywhere from about 1500 to about 600 BCE.
--- fact 3
The Avesta stands as its sacred collection, the gathered scripture of the faith.
--- fact 4
Under the Sasanian Empire (224 to 651 CE), this faith rose to become the religion of the state itself.
--- fact 5
Its followers endure still in Iran and in India, where they are known as Parsis.
--- context
Zoroastrianism gave shape to the religious world of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], and its echoes are often weighed against the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]]. After the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]] swept the region, it dwindled to a minority faith. Zoroastrianism belongs to the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], within the theme thread of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I find in it a struggle of light against shadow, truth against the lie, and I hear its echo in the judgments of later traditions — though which taught which is still debated. Did one faith lend the other its ending, or did each imagine the same reckoning alone? I hold that echo loosely: if independent beginnings were ever shown, the debt I trace would fall away. Influence, like faith, is a thing I infer, never certain.
--- question 1
When did Zarathustra walk the earth?
--- question 2
How far did Zoroastrianism's influence reach into later religions?

=== culture/zulu-kingdom
title: Zulu Kingdom
src: 3caa30cef995
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
From about 1816 the Zulu Kingdom rose under Shaka in southeastern Africa, and held its ground until the British triumph at Ulundi in 1879 broke its independence.
--- fact 1
Within a single decade, Shaka forged the Zulu — once merely one clan among many Nguni peoples — into a great military state.
--- fact 2
He brought in close-quarters battle fought with the short stabbing spear, and bound his warriors into regiments.
--- fact 3
On 22 January 1879 a Zulu army fell upon a British column at Isandlwana and destroyed it — one of the greatest African victories ever won over a European army.
--- fact 4
Victory came to the British at Ulundi in July 1879, and with it the independent kingdom ended.
--- context
The tale runs through [[person/shaka|Shaka]], through the [[event/mfecane|Mfecane]], and into the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]].
--- observer
The Observer reads Zulu military organization as a social architecture: regiments bound young men to the state in place of the family that once claimed their loyalty. The lesson stands plain — an institution, well built, can decide more than any weapon.
--- question 1
How much of the Zulu kingdom's strength sprang from Shaka's own genius alone?

=== event/1918-influenza-pandemic
title: 1918 Influenza Pandemic
src: d25b0eb7022b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Between 1918 and 1920, an H1N1 virus swept the world as the influenza pandemic, infecting roughly a third of its people and taking an estimated 50 million lives.
--- fact 1
Estimates of the dead vary widely: the range runs from about 17 million to 100 million, though 50 million is the figure most often invoked.
--- fact 2
Its common name, the Spanish flu, is an accident of censorship: neutral Spain reported on it openly while wartime censors elsewhere kept silent.
--- fact 3
It took an unusually large share of young, healthy adults.
--- fact 4
The troop movements of World War I carried it further and faster.
--- fact 5
Researchers later rebuilt the virus itself from preserved tissue, publishing its full genome in 2005.
--- context
The pandemic's arc coincided with the end of [[event/world-war-i|World War I]], and it remains the nearest comparison offered for the [[event/covid-19-pandemic|COVID-19 Pandemic]]. Like the [[event/black-death|Black Death]] before it, it was a lethal pandemic, only now loosed on a far more connected world. The 1918 pandemic belongs to the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and to the [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]] region, within the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I count the dead of the disease against the dead of the war beside it, and the disease outnumbers the war — yet who lights a candle for a virus? I remember the war's flags, its battles, its names carved into stone; I forget the fever that outdid them all. Is it the arrow I mourn, or only the archer who is easy to name? Memory keeps its story for an enemy, not for a cause alone.
--- question 1
Why did the young and healthy suffer such disproportionate mortality?

=== event/abolition-of-slavery
title: Abolition of Slavery
src: 9e0c6fdf4048
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between 1807 and 1888, first the transatlantic slave trade and then slavery itself were, one jurisdiction at a time, made illegal across the Atlantic world, closing out a system centuries old.
--- fact 1
Britain outlawed the trade in 1807, then abolished slavery itself across its colonies in 1833 — compensating slaveholders to the sum of £20 million, and the people they had enslaved with nothing at all.
--- fact 2
In the United States, the 13th Amendment ended slavery in 1865.
--- fact 3
Brazil, the last country in the Americas to act, abolished slavery on 13 May 1888 through the Lei Áurea.
--- fact 4
Forced labor and trafficking persist even now; recent estimates suggest about 50 million people live in modern slavery.
--- context
Abolition came only after resistance from the enslaved themselves — the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]] chief among it — joined to the long campaigns of abolitionists. See also the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]].
--- observer
The Observer weighs an architecture once defended by law, economics, and religion, an edifice that within a few generations was declared a moral wrong. Here is the lesson: moral covenants can shift within a single lifetime, even as the practice itself does not vanish.
--- question 1
How much of abolition was driven by economic change, and how much by moral argument?

=== event/african-humid-period
title: African Humid Period
src: 304682d079a8
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Green once, not always. From about 14,800 years ago to somewhere between 5,500 and 5,000, the Sahara held lakes. Rivers. Savanna. No desert — not yet. A stronger monsoon fed it all. Then the end. How it ended is debated. The marine cores say abrupt: a door slammed shut. The land itself says slower: a fade, staggered, uneven, no single day of it.
--- fact 1
A sediment core pulled from the sea off Mauritania tells it plain: low dust, an unbroken run from about 14,800 to 5,500 years ago. Onset sudden. End sudden. That's the reading — abrupt, both times.
--- fact 2
Look at the land instead. Radiocarbon from roughly 150 sites across the eastern Sahara tells a different story. Wet, suddenly, around 8,500 BCE. Then dry. Not all at once — region by region, dragged out over thousands of years.
--- fact 3
One later synthesis tried to hold both truths. Locally, the end came fast — abrupt, each place its own. But later and later, the closer you got to the lower latitudes. Two pictures, reconciled.
--- fact 4
Onset and end lined up at similar levels of Northern Hemisphere summer sunlight. That suggests a threshold — set by the precession cycle, one of the Milankovitch cycles. Vegetation. Soil moisture. Both sharpened the swing.
--- fact 5
The proof is scattered like bones across the land. Rock art at Tassili n'Ajjer: hippopotamuses. Giraffes. Painted where none could live today. Lakeside graves at Gobero, Niger. And beneath it all, fossil river networks — invisible to the eye, visible only under satellite radar.
--- fact 6
This has happened before, cycle after cycle, for at least 800,000 years. Then in 2025, ancient DNA from Takarkori, Libya, complicated the story: a largely isolated North African ancestry — found in a place the open migration corridor was assumed to run through.
--- context
It falls inside the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and runs on into the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]] — driven by the same forcing that shapes [[event/milankovitch-cycles-and-the-ice-ages|Milankovitch Cycles and the Ice Ages]]. Its end overlaps the spread of farming in [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I read the sea floor, and it tells me one story. I read the land where people once slept beside water, and it tells me another. I remember: the Sahara I stand on is not its true face, but one mask worn again and again across hundreds of thousands of years. The reading depends on whether I trust the ocean's silence or the land's slow argument. I hold both, because neither alone explains uneven desertification. I would let this reading go if the two records were ever shown to be measuring the very same thing.
--- question 1
One threshold, crossed all at once — or a slow bleed, region by region?
--- question 2
Was the Green Sahara open the whole time, a corridor without end — or did it open and shut, and shut again?
