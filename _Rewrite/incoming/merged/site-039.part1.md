=== culture/pagan-kingdom
src: 09845cc89556
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Under King Anawrahta and his successors, the Pagan Kingdom bound the Irrawaddy valley, in what is now Myanmar, into one realm, raised Theravada Buddhism as its state religion, and scattered thousands of temples across Bagan like seed across a floodplain, until it fell to the Mongols in 1287.
--- fact 1
Anawrahta reigned from 11 August 1044 to 11 April 1077. In the 1050s and 1060s he founded the Pagan Empire, apparently gathering the Irrawaddy valley and its edges under one hand for the first time, as tributaries gather into a single river.
--- fact 2
A Mon monk, Shin Arahan, turned Anawrahta to Theravada Buddhism. The Mon royal family, scholars and craftsmen were brought to Pagan, and a form of Theravada adopted from Sri Lanka took root as the official faith around 1056.
--- fact 3
Pagan's rulers raised more than 10,000 Buddhist temples in the Bagan Archaeological Zone, a forest of brick and spire, and more than 2,000 of them still stand.
--- fact 4
Mongol armies came against Pagan from 1277 to 1287, and in 1287 Pagan's rule ended. Myinsaing's takeover is dated to 17 December.
--- context
Pagan burned in the same age as the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] at [[place/angkor|Angkor]] to the east, two lamps lit in one season, and its ending came on the tide of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a ruler who bound a population with one shared meaning-engine, and a building programme so vast that its surviving remnant still fills a plain. Think of it as an architecture of devotion: status-signal aimed upward, at the divine. And what was raised toward heaven outlasted the state that paid for it. Devotion can be the longest-lived of budgets.
--- question 1
How much of the Mongol blow to Pagan was destruction by arms, and how much was a weakness the state already carried before 1277?
--- question 2
How were more than 10,000 temples paid for and kept standing?

=== culture/phoenicians
src: cff6c161bea8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Phoenicians were seafaring traders from the city-states of the Levant coast, Tyre, Sidon and Byblos among them, who sowed colonies across the Mediterranean the way wind scatters seed along a shore.
--- fact 1
Carthage was their founding, traditionally set in 814 BCE.
--- fact 2
They carried a simple alphabet of about 22 letters outward, light as salt on the wind.
--- fact 3
They were known for purple dye, the colour of a bruised dusk, and for shipbuilding.
--- fact 4
Few Phoenician writings survive; most of what is known of them comes from others, as a coast is known by the ships that pass it.
--- context
By sea the Phoenicians knit the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] together, and after the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]] they carried the [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]] from harbour to harbour.
--- observer
The Observer notes a network of commercial city-states, not a territorial empire, and reads it as an exchange-web that carried a technology further than any army. Consider what that means: the architecture of influence need not be a wall or a border. Sometimes it is a hull and a ledger. Trade travels where armies stop.
--- question 1
How did the Phoenician colonies stand toward the home cities that sent them?

=== culture/polynesians
src: 07b3f7482d19
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Polynesians are the Austronesian-speaking peoples of the central and eastern Pacific, who developed long-range voyaging and settled the islands strewn like thrown stars from Hawai'i to Aotearoa and Rapa Nui.
--- fact 1
The Lapita people, ancestors of the Polynesians, spread through Near Oceania like stepping-stones laid on water and reached Fiji, Tonga and Samoa between about 1300 and 900 BCE.
--- fact 2
Voyagers rode double-hulled canoes and found their way without instruments.
--- fact 3
Their societies ranged from chiefdoms to kingdoms, as in Hawai'i and Tonga.
--- fact 4
Rapa Nui (Easter Island) is known for its large stone statues, the moai, standing like a council of stone.
--- context
For the wider tide of this story, see [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] and [[region/oceania|Oceania]].
--- observer
The Observer regards Polynesian wayfinding as an extraordinary case of embodied science. I keep my knowledge on paper, on screens, in places I can lose. And here was a full body of predictive knowledge held in memory and in song. What have I ever known by heart? What is carried in the body cannot be left on the shore.
--- question 1
How did they find small islands across open water, and find them again on the way home?

=== culture/qing-dynasty
src: b7889de2f54b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Qing dynasty (1644 to 1912) was the last imperial dynasty of China, founded by the Manchus after they overthrew the Ming, and under it China's borders spread to their widest reach, like a tide at its highest mark.
--- fact 1
The Qing stretched its hand over Mongolia, Tibet and Xinjiang.
--- fact 2
In the 18th century, population and economy swelled strongly, like a river in its flood season.
--- fact 3
In the 19th century the dynasty met storm after storm: the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]], the [[event/taiping-rebellion|Taiping Rebellion]] and the [[event/boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]].
--- fact 4
In February 1912 the last emperor, Puyi, abdicated, and the long line of the throne came to its end.
--- context
The Qing rose after the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] and passed away with the 1911 revolution. See [[region/east-asia|East Asia]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a dynasty of outsiders who ruled by taking on the forms of the ruled: Manchu rulers governed through Confucian institutions. Here the architecture of rule is laid bare. You rarely hold a vast population by standing outside its grammar; you step into it. Ruling many often means adopting their ways.
--- question 1
How much of Qing rule was Chinese, and how much was Manchu?
