=== place/lake-baikal
title: Lake Baikal
src: 3b75af4fa14d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Lake Baikal is a rift lake in southern Siberia, Russia, the deepest and oldest lake on Earth and the largest by volume of fresh water, a blue eye open on the world for ages; people have lived around it for many thousands of years, and UNESCO has listed it since 1996.
--- fact 1
Lake Baikal lies in a rift valley, water poured into a wound of the earth; it runs about 636 kilometers long, plunges to a maximum depth of 1,642 meters, and holds about 20 percent of the world's unfrozen surface fresh water.
--- fact 2
The Baikal rift basin is estimated to be 25 to 30 million years old, which makes the lake the oldest on Earth; its geologic age runs far older than any human presence, as the sea is older than the ships upon it.
--- fact 3
More than 1,700 species of plants and animals live in and around Lake Baikal, about two thirds of them found nowhere else, among them the Baikal seal, a seal of fresh water.
--- fact 4
From about the 5th millennium BCE, hunter-fisher-gatherers of the Kitoi culture buried their dead around the lake, and much older Paleolithic sites, such as Mal'ta, lie nearby like old footprints beneath newer ones.
--- fact 5
In the 1640s Russian explorers reached Lake Baikal, and Russian settlement began among the Buryat and Evenki peoples.
--- fact 6
A pulp and paper mill on the southern shore discharged waste into the lake from 1966 until it closed in 2013, and it was one of the earliest targets of organized environmental protest in the Soviet Union.
--- fact 7
In 1996 UNESCO inscribed Lake Baikal.
--- context
Lake Baikal lies near the Paleolithic site linked to [[culture/mal-ta-and-the-ancient-north-eurasians|Mal'ta and the Ancient North Eurasians]], and in the 17th century the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] reached its shores. Its earliest Holocene burials set it in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]], its region is [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Lake Baikal is valued in two ways at once, as a store of an unusual biota and as a resource, that both valuations have shaped policy and the state of the lake, and that the balance between them has shifted with the political era. You will find this double ledger wherever something rare is also useful.

Consider the scale. A lake that took millions of years to form and holds a fifth of the planet's unfrozen fresh water could be altered by one industrial plant within a few decades: the mismatch between geological and human timescales, made visible. The reading would weaken if measurements show that the mill's effect on the lake as a whole was small compared with natural variation. What took ages to make can take very little to mark.
--- question 1
How deep and how lasting a change did the pulp mill leave in the lake's ecosystem?
--- question 2
How did people live around Lake Baikal's shores in the Neolithic, and in what manner did they lay their dead to rest?

=== place/library-of-alexandria
title: Library of Alexandria
src: da86c3a918b3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Library of Alexandria was a major research library in Ptolemaic Egypt, founded in the early 3rd century BCE as part of the Mouseion, and you should know this first: its decline was gradual rather than a single catastrophe.
--- fact 1
This architecture of learning was founded under the first Ptolemaic rulers, in the 3rd century BCE.
--- fact 2
The scholars associated with it form a lineage that includes Eratosthenes, who estimated the circumference of the Earth, and Callimachus, who compiled a catalog of works: one reckoned the world's size, the other ordered its books.
--- fact 3
Euclid is traditionally said to have worked in Alexandria, though the details of his life are uncertain; tradition gives us the name, not the biography.
--- fact 4
Julius Caesar's fire in 48 BCE damaged some holdings, but notice what came next: the institution continued.
--- fact 5
Its decline came from reduced funding, political upheaval, and the loss of scholars over several centuries: not one blow, but a slow erosion of the covenant that sustained it.
--- context
Think of the library's genealogy: it grew from the conquests of [[person/alexander-the-great|Alexander the Great]] and inherited the tradition of [[person/aristotle|Aristotle]], and its later scholars include [[person/hypatia|Hypatia]]. It sits in the [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I wanted the fire. I wanted the single great burning, the story that is easy to remember. I was given slow neglect instead. Memory institutions seem to fail more often through budget cuts than through fire. What have I let go quiet, shelf by shelf?

The slowest fire is the one no one pays to put out.
