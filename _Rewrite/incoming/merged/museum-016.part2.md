=== people--alice-guy-blache--1
src: d4958c26a717
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Alice Guy-Blaché (1873 to 1968) was a French filmmaker who began directing at the Gaumont Film Company in Paris in 1896 and later became the first woman to found and run her own film studio. She is credited as one of cinema's earliest narrative directors, though her work was washed out of film history for most of the twentieth century, like a name left out in the rain.

She was born in Saint-Mandé, France, in 1873, and in 1894 she was hired as a secretary at a camera and photography supply company, which Léon Gaumont and partners took over in 1895 as it turned toward motion pictures.

After seeing an early demonstration film she won leave to direct in her spare time. The result, traditionally given as La Fée aux choux (1896), is often called cinema's first narrative film, though its title is uncertain and that 1896 film appears to be lost; the surviving 1900 and 1902 versions are different films.

She rose to head of production at Gaumont, experimenting with hand-tinted colour and an early sound process, Phonoscènes, and in 1906 she mocked gender roles in the reversal comedy Les Résultats du féminisme.

In 1907 she married the cameraman Herbert Blaché and crossed to the United States, and in 1910 she co-founded the Solax Company in Flushing, New York, as its artistic director, with a new plant at Fort Lee, New Jersey following in 1912.

At Solax she directed social dramas such as Making an American Citizen and Falling Leaves (1912), facing immigration and domestic abuse more directly than most of her peers.

=== people--albert-einstein--1

--- description
Albert Einstein (1879 to 1955) was a German-born physicist who developed the theories of special and general relativity and made key contributions to quantum theory.

In 1905 he published papers on special relativity, the photoelectric effect and Brownian motion. One year, three papers, and a changed physics.

General relativity (1915) describes gravity as the curvature of space and time.

He won the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics for the photoelectric effect.

In 1939 he signed a letter to President Roosevelt about the possibility of atomic weapons. Knowledge carries a ledger of consequences.
