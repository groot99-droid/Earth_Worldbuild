=== era/silurian-period
src: 9c8737d14641
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Silurian Period, from 443.8 to 419.2 million years ago, is the third period of the Paleozoic Era, a season of mending after the Late Ordovician extinction. In it rose the oldest known vascular land plants, and in its waters swam the first well-preserved jawed fishes.
--- fact 1
The Silurian Period spans 443.8 to 419.2 million years ago, and graptolites stand as its gatekeepers at both ends: at Dob's Linn in Scotland for its base, and at Klonk in the Czech Republic for its top, the base of the Devonian.
--- fact 2
After the [[event/late-ordovician-extinction|Late Ordovician Extinction]] the life of the sea mended: the sea rose as the Hirnantian ice sheets melted, and reefs of corals and stromatoporoids spread through warm shallow seas like gardens reclaiming a ruined court.
--- fact 3
Cooksonia, a small leafless plant bearing spore capsules at the tips of its branching stems like tiny lanterns, is the oldest widely accepted vascular land plant, known from about 433 million years ago.
--- fact 4
The oldest well-preserved jawed vertebrates, armoured placoderms among them and the early bony fish Guiyu (about 425 million years old, from China), come from the later Silurian.
--- fact 5
The millipede Pneumodesmus from Scotland, about 428 million years old, has been reported as the oldest known air-breathing land animal, a small pilgrim on dry ground.
--- fact 6
The Iapetus Ocean closed like a great door as Laurentia, Baltica and Avalonia came together in collision, raising the northern continent Laurussia by the early Devonian.
--- context
After the [[era/ordovician-period|Ordovician Period]] came the Silurian Period, and after it the [[era/devonian-period|Devonian Period]], all within the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]]. Its thread runs through the theme of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], and its plants and arthropods are early verses in the long song of [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Silurian is when land life first leaves more than scattered traces: small leafless plants only centimetres tall, and small arthropods. You know how this story ends, and that is exactly the trap. Nothing in this record marks them as the beginning of forests and land vertebrates. A seedling carries no sign announcing the oak. The Observer treats that silence as a caution against reading any early stage in the light of what came after. Hindsight is a lamp that throws long, false shadows backward.
--- question 1
Since fossil spores are older than the oldest plant bodies, how much earlier than Cooksonia were plants already living on land, unseen?
--- question 2
Did jawed fishes arise earlier than the stone's record of them shows?

=== era/tonian-period
src: f15c69eb546c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Tonian Period is the first period of the Neoproterozoic Era, running from 1.0 billion to 720 million years ago. Its chronicle holds the break-up of the supercontinent Rodinia and the branching of eukaryotes into many kinds, a quiet flowering before the Cryogenian ice ages came down.
--- fact 1
The Tonian Period spans 1.0 billion to 720 million years ago; both its thresholds are set by numerical ages, and its upper edge falls close to the onset of the first Cryogenian glaciation, where the long cold begins.
--- fact 2
During the Tonian, roughly 800 to 750 million years ago, the supercontinent Rodinia began to rift apart, its old unity cracking like a sealed jar.
--- fact 3
Eukaryotic microfossils show a widening diversity: vase-shaped microfossils in the Chuar Group of the Grand Canyon, roughly 780 to 740 million years old, are interpreted as the shells of amoeba-like protists, tiny urns left in the stone.
--- fact 4
Phosphatic scale microfossils from the Fifteenmile Group of Yukon, Canada, reported from rocks roughly 810 to 720 million years old, are among the earliest evidence of eukaryotes forging mineral parts, the first small armour of cells.
--- fact 5
Toward the end of the period the environmental record grows more variable, with proposed rises in oxygen, and possible glacial deposits near 750 million years ago are debated, like frost-marks half-seen on old glass.
--- context
After the [[era/mesoproterozoic-era|Mesoproterozoic Era]] came the Tonian Period, and after it the [[era/cryogenian-period|Cryogenian Period]], both within the [[era/neoproterozoic-era|Neoproterozoic Era]]. The Tonian's thread runs through the theme of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
I read the Tonian as a loosening. I watch a supercontinent start to split. I watch a climate record become less steady. I watch protists diversify into new niches. Does any of it point at animals? None of it does. And still it is the background against which the later ice ages and the first large bodies happened, the way a room you once lived in stays behind every room after it.

The reading would weaken if Tonian records showed an environment as steady as the Mesoproterozoic before it. Some beginnings are only loosenings.
--- question 1
Did the breaking of Rodinia help to call down the Cryogenian glaciations?
--- question 2
How many kinds of Tonian eukaryotes truly lived, when the record of them is so sparse?

=== era/triassic-period
src: 9cc1f9c40db4
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Triassic Period, from 251.9 to 201.4 million years ago, is the first period of the Mesozoic Era, the long mending after the end-Permian extinction. In it the dinosaurs, the pterosaurs and the first mammals step for the first time onto the stage of the world.
--- fact 1
The Triassic Period spans 251.9 to 201.4 million years ago; its lower gate stands at Meishan in China, and its upper, the base of the Jurassic, at Kuhjoch in Austria.
--- fact 2
The world took roughly five to ten million years to recover from the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]]; Lystrosaurus, a survivor, was among the most abundant land vertebrates of the Early Triassic, and the climate burned generally hot, with no permanent polar ice and a dry Pangaean interior.
--- fact 3
The archosaurs branched and spread. The oldest dinosaurs date to about 243 to 233 million years ago, yet for the rest of the period they were one banner among many, beside the relatives of crocodiles that dominated most land ecosystems; pterosaurs appear by about 215 million years ago, and ichthyosaurs and other reptiles returned to the sea.
--- fact 4
The earliest mammals and their close relatives appear in the Late Triassic, roughly 225 to 205 million years ago, though the hour of their coming depends on how mammals are defined.
--- fact 5
The [[event/triassic-jurassic-extinction|Triassic-Jurassic Extinction]], about 201.4 million years ago, coincides with the eruption of the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province and the start of the [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]], and it swept away an estimated 80 percent of species.
--- context
After the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]] came the Triassic Period, and after it the [[era/jurassic-period|Jurassic Period]], both within the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]]. Its thread runs through the theme of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]]; see [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]] for the great house whose first members appear here.
--- observer
The Observer notes that dinosaurs and mammals both first appear in the Triassic, yet neither dominated until later. Here is the lesson in plain terms: arriving is not the same as ruling. Dinosaurs were one lineage among several for about 30 million years, and rose to dominance only after an extinction removed many of their rivals. Think of an understudy who takes the lead only because the stage has emptied. The Observer reads this as timing and opportunity, and the reading would weaken if anatomy alone explained why dinosaurs filled the gap. Sometimes the door opens before the talent is proven.
--- question 1
In the sorting of Triassic archosaurs, how much did climate decide which survived, and how much did competition?
--- question 2
When did the first true mammals appear, if the very definition of mammal can shift their dawn by tens of millions of years?

=== era/upper-paleolithic
src: 27f6f106306b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Upper Paleolithic is the last part of the Paleolithic, from about 50,000 to about 10,000 BCE, an age of long blades and carved bone. It holds blade tools, bone and antler technology, art and ornament in abundance, and the spread of Homo sapiens across the globe like firelight carried from hand to hand.
--- fact 1
The Upper Paleolithic runs from about 50,000 to about 10,000 BCE, closing with the warming at the end of the last ice age (Africa uses the name Later Stone Age); its toolmakers struck long blades and shaped bone, antler and ivory, and eyed bone needles from Denisova Cave in Siberia, about 40,000 to 50,000 years old, suggest sewn clothing.
--- fact 2
Homo sapiens went out across the globe like a tide feeling its way along new shores: Sahul by about 50,000 years ago ([[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]]), Europe by about 45,000 years ago, Beringia by about 30,000 years ago ([[place/beringia|Beringia]]), and the Americas by at least about 23,000 years ago ([[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]]); Neanderthals disappear from Europe about 40,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Painted, carved and engraved images crowd the age: [[place/chauvet-cave|Chauvet Cave]] in France holds paintings about 36,000 years old, and cave paintings in Sulawesi, Indonesia, include a narrative scene about 51,000 years old. The [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]], about 26,500 to 19,000 years ago, laid ice over much of northern Eurasia and North America; pottery appears in East Asia by about 20,000 years ago ([[technology/pottery|Pottery]]), and dogs were drawn from wolves into the human circle by at least about 15,000 years ago.
--- context
After the [[era/middle-paleolithic|Middle Paleolithic]] came the Upper Paleolithic, and after it, in the Near East, the [[era/epipaleolithic|Epipaleolithic]], all held within the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]]. Three threads run through it: [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]]. See also [[event/quaternary-megafauna-extinctions|Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions]].
--- observer
Upper Paleolithic sites hold the first large body of objects that seem made for display, ritual or play, and I want to name them all at once. I want to call the beads rank. I want to call the burials grief. I want to call the carvings play. So I hold back, as the Observer must: beads and ornaments as possible status-signals, burials as possible grief-rites, and I keep in mind that a practice cannot be read from a surviving object alone. How much of a life can a bead carry?

The reading would weaken if comparable objects proved common long before this period, which would make it a change in what survives, not in behaviour. What survives is not the same as what was done.
--- question 1
Is the apparent blaze of art and ornament real, or only the glow of better preservation and more sites?
--- question 2
How did the Upper Paleolithic industries, the long blades and the worked bone, relate to specific human populations?

=== region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands
src: a59eda4f44e0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands are the forests, river systems, and savannas east of the Andes, home to Indigenous societies whose scale and complexity are being rediscovered, as you read this, through aerial laser survey. Think of it as a ledger that was never lost, only unread.
--- fact 1
Over the past decade, lidar surveys have looked through the canopy and revealed large networks of earthworks, roads, and settlements beneath the forest; see [[culture/casarabe-culture|Casarabe Culture]], [[place/upano-valley-settlements|Upano Valley Settlements]], and [[place/xingu-garden-cities|Xingu Garden Cities]].
--- fact 2
Indigenous land management, including soil enrichment, shaped much of the forest into an architecture you can walk through without seeing; see [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]]. Scientists dispute whether the dark earths (terra preta) were largely made by people or arose from natural processes that people then put to use.
--- fact 3
European contact after 1492 brought severe population loss from epidemic disease and forced labor. Whether this caused the forest to grow back widely is debated. A 2021 study of 39 fossil pollen records found no synchronized regrowth after European arrival, and found that at most sites the abandonment of land and the regrowth began about 300 to 600 years earlier. The researchers Bush and McMichael argue that populations probably peaked and declined well before 1492. Alexander Koch and colleagues, who proposed the regrowth hypothesis for the Americas as a whole, reply that Amazonia may have been less affected than Mexico, Central America, and the Andes.
--- fact 4
This is the ground of the [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]] and, at its edges, of many peoples the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] never ruled.
--- context
The lowlands lie east of the [[region/andes|Andes]], and trade tied the two together like a long cord between neighbours. The [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] transformed them.
--- observer
I have said 'untouched wilderness' and believed it. I have looked at green and seen only green. I have mistaken a long labour for no labour at all. How much history can one phrase hide? A history of large human societies. What looks like nature can be the product of centuries of deliberate management. Sometimes the wild is a garden whose gardeners were forgotten.
--- question 1
How many people lived in the Amazon before 1500? Published estimates run from about 1 to 2 million up to 8 to 10 million, because little documentation exists and the methods of estimation give widely varying results.
