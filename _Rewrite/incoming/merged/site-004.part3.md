=== species/flowering-plants
src: 3e18114e4853
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Flowering plants (angiosperms) appear in the fossil record in the Early Cretaceous, about 130 million years ago, opening like a late dawn across the land, and now make up most land plant species.
--- fact 1
The oldest widely accepted angiosperm pollen dates to the Hauterivian and Barremian ages of the Early Cretaceous, about 133 to 125 million years ago, a first gold dust on the wind.
--- fact 2
The oldest well-preserved flowering-plant macrofossils, such as those from China, date to about 125 million years ago, blossoms pressed in stone like flowers in a book.
--- fact 3
Some molecular-clock studies set their origin earlier, in the Triassic or Jurassic; this gap between clock and stone is debated.
--- fact 4
Flowering plants radiated rapidly in the Cretaceous alongside pollinating insects, bloom and wing rising together.
--- fact 5
Cereals, legumes, fruits and most other crops descend from this one great house.
--- context
Flowering plants diversified in the age of the [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]] and outlasted the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]. Every crop of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] springs from this lineage. They sit in the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and their thread runs through the theme of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
We like to say we domesticated the plants. The Observer notes that the species' whole agricultural existence rests on one plant lineage, a lineage that evolved to draw animals into carrying its pollen and seeds. Farming can be read as the species joining a partnership that plants had run with insects for more than a hundred million years. Think of a newcomer admitted to an old guild and handed its tools and rules. We did not found this covenant; we signed it late.
--- question 1
Why was the origin of flowering plants so abrupt in the fossil record, a sudden blooming out of bare stone?

=== species/homo-erectus
src: 573f5cee2112
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Homo erectus was an early human species that endured from about 1.9 million to roughly 110,000 years ago, the first known hominin to spread widely beyond Africa, a wanderer who carried the old home outward.
--- fact 1
Its fossils have been found in Africa, in Georgia (Dmanisi, about 1.8 million years old), and in East and Southeast Asia, waymarks along a long road.
--- fact 2
It shaped Acheulean handaxes and probably used fire, the old light held in the hand; see [[event/control-of-fire|Control of Fire]].
--- fact 3
Its brain was about 900 cubic centimeters, and its body proportions were close to those of modern humans, a shape we would know.
--- fact 4
It may be the longest-lived human species of all, a candle that burned for ages.
--- context
H. erectus is ancestral or related to later Homo species, including [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]], a root or a near branch of their tree. See [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]].
--- observer
We live inside a rush and call it normal. The Observer notes that a species survived for well over a million years with much the same toolkit. Think of one set of tools passed from hand to hand, age after age, almost unchanged. Human history's rapid pace is recent. Speed is the exception, not the rule.
--- question 1
Which later species sprang from H. erectus?

=== species/homo-floresiensis
src: 99747e2bd41b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Homo floresiensis, nicknamed 'the hobbit', was a small-bodied human species that lived on the Indonesian island of Flores, a people kept apart by the sea. Its bones date to about 100,000 to 60,000 years ago, and stone tools attributed to it run on to about 50,000 years ago, like footprints that outlast the walker.
--- fact 1
It was discovered in 2003 at Liang Bua Cave, where the island had kept it hidden.
--- fact 2
Adults stood about 1.1 meters tall and carried a brain of about 400 cubic centimeters.
--- fact 3
It made stone tools and lived on an island ringed by deep water, a world walled in by the sea.
--- fact 4
Whether it descended from H. erectus or from an earlier ancestor is debated, its lineage still lost in the mist.
--- context
H. floresiensis shows that several human species overlapped in time with [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]], many lamps burning in the same long night. See [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]].
--- observer
We picture our kind alone on the stage. The Observer notes two things. Isolated islands can produce unexpected forms: shut a lineage behind deep water and it may take a shape no one foresaw. And for most of the Pleistocene, the species was not alone. Solitude is recent.
--- question 1
What was its ancestry, the root beneath that small island tree?

=== species/homo-habilis
src: 9063c635b70e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Homo habilis ('handy man'), a name that honors the hand, is an early member of the genus Homo that lived in Africa from about 2.4 to 1.4 million years ago.
--- fact 1
It was named in 1964 from fossils at Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania, where the gorge opened its old pages.
--- fact 2
Its brain was roughly 500 to 700 cubic centimeters (estimates vary, as tallies do in poor light).
--- fact 3
It is associated with [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]], though the link is not certain, a thread that may yet snap.
--- fact 4
Whether it is a distinct species or a variant of other early Homo is debated; its borders are drawn in sand.
--- context
H. habilis appears in the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and comes before [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]], an early lamp on the road.
--- observer
Where does the human begin? The Observer notes that each new discovery pushed the label 'human' further back in time. Think of a shoreline redrawn every time the tide goes out a little further. The boundary of the genus is blurred. Categories are maps, not the land.
--- question 1
How is H. habilis bound to the other early Homo, and by which threads?
