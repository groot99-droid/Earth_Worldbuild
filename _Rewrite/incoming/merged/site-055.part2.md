=== technology/iron-smelting
title: Iron Smelting
src: 43247defe9e4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Iron smelting, the extraction of iron from ore, began on a limited scale in Anatolia in the early 2nd millennium BCE, possibly earlier, before becoming widespread across the Near East from the late 2nd millennium BCE, about 1200 BCE onward. Ironworking also developed in sub-Saharan Africa, and whether it arose there independently is still debated.
--- fact 1
Extractive iron metallurgy is attributed to Anatolia on a limited scale in the early 2nd millennium BCE, with its first major expansion in the late 2nd and early 1st millennium BCE. A small iron lump from Kaman-Kalehoyuk in Turkey, from a layer dated to about 2500 to 2250 BCE, has been argued to be even earlier smelted iron, possibly made from ore sourced elsewhere; but this is a single find, and it remains under study.
--- fact 2
Iron ore, for its part, is far more common than copper and tin.
--- fact 3
Smelting demands higher temperatures, and it produced a tougher metal once carbon-steel techniques improved.
--- fact 4
The earliest reported dates for iron smelting in sub-Saharan Africa — about 2000 BCE at Lejja in Nigeria, Oboui in the Central African Republic, and Gbatoro in Cameroon — are contested, since it is unclear that the dated charcoal is securely tied to iron-working remains, and because of old-carbon contamination. Iron working in the Nok culture of Nigeria dates between about the 9th century BCE and 550 BCE.
--- fact 5
Scholars remain divided on whether African ironworking developed independently of the Near East. Older theories of diffusion from Carthage or Meroe have been challenged, and current evidence leans toward independent African development.
--- fact 6
Iron tools, once common, made it easier both to clear forests and to arm large populations.
--- context
Iron smelting defines the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and followed [[technology/bronze-metallurgy|Bronze Metallurgy]]. In Africa, the [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]] carried ironworking across the eastern and southern regions.
--- observer
The Observer notes that a more common metal changed who could afford tools and weapons. Cheaper materials often widen participation — in production, and in war.
--- question 1
Did iron working arise independently in sub-Saharan Africa? The evidence leans that way, though the very oldest dates remain disputed.
--- question 2
How early did smelting actually begin in Anatolia, and does the 2500 to 2250 BCE lump come from a working smelting tradition, or from an isolated experiment?

=== technology/irrigation-canals
title: Irrigation Canals
src: da79c023a99e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Irrigation canals are channels built to carry river water to fields. The earliest known examples sit at Choga Mami in Iraq, dated to about the 6th millennium BCE, and canal systems later supported southern Mesopotamian cities as well as states in China.
--- fact 1
Choga Mami in eastern Iraq, a Samarran settlement occupied about 5600 to 4800 BCE, yielded canals identified by its excavators, David and Joan Oates. Their date, and their role as irrigation, remain uncertain.
--- fact 2
Southern Mesopotamia has too little rainfall to grow grain, so cities such as [[place/uruk|Uruk]] depended on river canals by the 4th millennium BCE.
--- fact 3
The [[technology/code-of-hammurabi|Code of Hammurabi]], about 1754 BCE, requires a landholder to pay for grain lost when a neglected dike or careless irrigation floods a neighbor's field.
--- fact 4
The Dujiangyan system in Sichuan, built by the state of Qin in 256 BCE, remains in use today and became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000.
--- fact 5
Some scholars link a buildup of salt to declining yields in southern Mesopotamia in the late 3rd millennium BCE, though the size of that effect is debated.
--- context
Irrigation canals extended the farming begun by [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] onto dry river plains in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]]. See [[person/hammurabi|Hammurabi]] and the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]].
--- observer
The Observer regards irrigation canals as a running cost of the Settling: whoever depends on a canal must dig it, clear it, and police it, so shared rules become part of the tool itself. The Hammurabi liability clause is story-glue in the shape of law, holding infrastructure together. This reading would weaken if the earliest canals turn out to be small local works, kept up without any wider authority.
--- question 1
Were the Choga Mami channels really irrigation canals, and how old are they?
--- question 2
How much did salt buildup, rather than politics or climate, actually weaken southern Mesopotamian farming?

=== technology/magnetic-compass
title: Magnetic Compass
src: a1af1b7a68be
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The magnetic compass came into use for navigation at sea in China by about the 12th century, after an earlier life in divination, and reached Europe not long after.
--- fact 1
Early compasses relied on lodestone, a naturally magnetized mineral.
--- fact 2
Chinese texts from the 11th and 12th centuries describe floating needle compasses.
--- fact 3
European references to the device appear in the late 12th century.
--- fact 4
The compass, crucially, allowed navigation out of sight of land.
--- context
The compass developed under the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]] and went on to support voyages such as [[person/zheng-he|Zheng He]]'s, and later, European oceanic voyages.
--- observer
The Observer notes that direction, once a matter of reading stars and coastlines, became instead a property of an instrument. Ancestor-weight gave way to physics.
--- question 1
How quickly did use of the compass actually spread?

=== technology/mechanical-clock
title: Mechanical Clock
src: 42fcae465e1a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Mechanical clocks, driven by falling weights and governed by an escapement, appeared in European towns and monasteries around 1300, after earlier Chinese and Islamic water clocks had already kept time by other means.
--- fact 1
In China, Su Song built an astronomical clock tower in 1088.
--- fact 2
Town clocks in European cities went on to regulate work, markets, and prayer.
--- fact 3
The pendulum clock, built by Huygens in 1656, greatly improved accuracy.
--- fact 4
Accurate clocks, in turn, were needed to measure longitude at sea.
--- context
The clock changed how people lived in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and helped prepare the ground for factory schedules in the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that once time could be divided into equal units, people began scheduling themselves by it. The clock, in this sense, is a story-glue technology — one built to coordinate strangers.
--- question 1
How exactly did mechanical time change ordinary life?

=== technology/microscope
title: Microscope
src: a6e5465d9e6a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A microscope is an optical instrument built to magnify objects too small for the unaided eye. Compound microscopes appeared in the Netherlands around 1620, and single-lens designs later reached even higher magnification still.
--- fact 1
Compound microscopes appeared in the Netherlands around 1620; the attribution to Zacharias Janssen, often dated to the 1590s, is now doubted, and priority remains contested.
--- fact 2
Around 1621, Cornelis Drebbel demonstrated a compound microscope in London.
--- fact 3
[[person/galileo-galilei|Galileo Galilei]] adapted an existing design into his own instrument, presented in 1624 and named the occhiolino.
--- fact 4
Robert Hooke's Micrographia, in 1665, used compound-microscope observations of cork to coin the term "cell."
--- fact 5
Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using single-lens microscopes of his own making, was the first to observe microorganisms, in the 1670s; the Royal Society elected him a fellow in 1680.
--- context
The microscope emerged in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], alongside the telescope, part of a broader turn toward instrumented observation later systematized as [[theme/science|Science]]. Its early history involves competing claims among Dutch lensmakers, not one clean inventor.
--- observer
The microscope reads as part of a pattern-taming project, a tool built to push observation past an inherited sensory limit. In several surveyed cases its earliest credit is unstable, which fits a tool-lineage pattern where a tidy single-inventor story replaces a messier network of lensmakers refining each other's work. This reading weakens if the disputed 1590s Janssen claim is ever confirmed. An alternative reading treats the instrument as a byproduct of an existing lens trade, rather than a triumph of curiosity.
--- question 1
Could any surviving instrument still settle priority among the early Dutch claimants?
--- question 2
How much informal microscope use came before Hooke's and Leeuwenhoek's published findings?

=== technology/movable-type-printing-press
title: Movable-Type Printing Press
src: a8af35a66e07
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The movable-type printing press, developed by Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz around 1440, let books be made quickly and cheaply across Europe.
--- fact 1
Movable type existed earlier still, in China with Bi Sheng around 1040, and in Korea with metal type by 1377.
--- fact 2
Gutenberg's own innovations included a metal-type alloy, oil-based ink, and a screw press.
--- fact 3
By 1500, an estimated 20 million printed volumes had already been produced.
--- fact 4
Printing went on to help spread Reformation pamphlets and scientific works alike.
--- context
See [[person/johannes-gutenberg|Johannes Gutenberg]] and [[technology/paper|Paper]]. Printing helped speed the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]] along.
--- observer
I notice how copying grew cheap enough that an idea could no longer be kept in one place. I notice how a page, once rare, learned to travel.

This is part of why religious and scientific movements after 1450 spread so fast. An idea contained is only ever waiting to escape.
--- question 1
How much did those earlier printing traditions actually influence Gutenberg?
