=== place/paris
src: e6e1a08ea953
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Paris is the capital of France, on the Seine River, which grew from a settlement of the Gaulish Parisii on the Île de la Cité in about the 3rd century BCE into a Roman town, a medieval capital, a center of revolutions and one of Europe's largest cities.
--- fact 1
Paris grew from a settlement of the Parisii on the banks of the Seine between about 250 and 225 BCE, though the exact location of their oppidum, whether the Île de la Cité or Nanterre, is disputed; Rome took the area in 52 BCE and built the town of Lutetia.
--- fact 2
Clovis made Paris his capital in about 508, and the Capetian kings made it the center of the French kingdom from 987; Notre-Dame was begun in 1163, and the University of Paris had developed by about 1200.
--- fact 3
Paris had perhaps 200,000 inhabitants around 1300, among the largest cities in Europe, and lost a large share of its people to the Black Death in 1348.
--- fact 4
The Bastille fell on 14 July 1789 during the French Revolution, and thousands of people were executed in Paris in the Reign of Terror of 1793 to 1794.
--- fact 5
Baron Haussmann rebuilt central Paris in the 1850s and 1860s with wide boulevards, sewers and parks.
--- fact 6
The suppression of the Paris Commune in 1871 killed many thousands of people in a week of fighting and executions; estimates are contested, ranging from about 7,000 killed in combat or summary execution to a commonly cited 10,000 to 15,000, with a single unconfirmed 1876 estimate as high as 20,000.
--- fact 7
Germany occupied Paris from June 1940 to August 1944; in July 1942 French police arrested about 13,000 Jews in the Vel' d'Hiv roundup, most of whom were later deported to Auschwitz.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed Paris, Banks of the Seine, in 1991.
--- context
Paris was the stage of the [[event/french-revolution|French Revolution]] and a center of the [[event/enlightenment|Enlightenment]]; it was founded on ground later ruled by the [[culture/romans|Romans]], and under German occupation in [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]] it was the scene of roundups that fed the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Paris has repeatedly been remade both as a display of the state's authority and as a stage for its overthrow: a cathedral for the medieval monarchy, revolutionary crowds, and 19th-century boulevards planned for order and circulation. The same city organized the roundup of its Jewish residents in 1942 and now commemorates it, and the Observer records the deaths of the Terror, the Commune and the deportations as suffering under different regimes. This reading would weaken if the city's monuments prove to have been shaped more by local building needs than by successive regimes' claims.
--- question 1
How large was Paris at its medieval peak, and how did the Black Death change it?
--- question 2
How have the Commune and the 1942 roundup been remembered in French public life?

=== place/pasargadae
src: e659a1b0ac3f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pasargadae was the first capital of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great in about 550 BCE on the plateau of Fars in present-day Iran, and the site of his tomb.
--- fact 1
Pasargadae lies in Fars province, southwestern Iran, about 90 kilometers northeast of Shiraz and about 40 kilometers from Persepolis.
--- fact 2
Cyrus the Great founded Pasargadae after defeating the Median king Astyages, in about 550 BCE, and it served as a royal seat until Darius I and his successors developed Persepolis and Susa.
--- fact 3
Pasargadae has an audience hall, residential palaces, a gate with a relief of a winged figure, and a formal garden with water channels that is regarded as an early example of the Persian garden.
--- fact 4
Stonemasons from other parts of the empire, including Lydia and Ionia in western Anatolia, contributed to its masonry, and its architecture combines Persian, Elamite, Mesopotamian and Anatolian elements.
--- fact 5
The Tomb of Cyrus, a stepped base topped by a gabled stone chamber, stands at the site; Greek writers report that Alexander visited it and ordered its restoration after it had been robbed.
--- fact 6
Inscriptions naming Cyrus appear at Pasargadae, though some scholars argue that they were added under Darius I.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Pasargadae in 2004.
--- context
Pasargadae was the first capital of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] and was later overshadowed by [[place/persepolis|Persepolis]]. Cyrus, its founder, took [[place/babylon|Babylon]] in 539 BCE, and Alexander visited the site during [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. Pasargadae sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Pasargadae shows an empire assembling its own style from its subjects: masons from Lydia, motifs from Mesopotamia and Elam, and a garden layout later copied across Persian courts, a network of craftsmen and ideas made visible in stone. A founder's tomb that a later conqueror chose to visit and restore suggests that a story about kingship could pass across dynasties and even across enemies. This reading would weaken if the building phases prove to belong mostly to Darius I, since the site would then tell more about a later king's claim on Cyrus's legacy.
--- question 1
Which buildings at Pasargadae date to Cyrus and which to later kings?
--- question 2
What were the roles of Pasargadae and Persepolis in court ritual?

=== place/robben-island
src: d3aed7276aca
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Robben Island is a small island in Table Bay, about 7 kilometers off Cape Town, South Africa, used from the 17th century to 1996 as a place of banishment, a leprosy colony and a prison, and best known for the political prisoners held there under apartheid, including Nelson Mandela.
--- fact 1
Robben Island lies in Table Bay about 7 kilometers from Cape Town and covers about 5 square kilometers; its name comes from the Dutch word for seals.
--- fact 2
The Dutch East India Company began using Robben Island as a place of banishment in the 1650s; among those sent there was the Khoikhoi interpreter Autshumato, in 1658.
--- fact 3
A leprosy colony operated on the island from 1845, with no firmly documented closure date, and it served as a military base in the Second World War.
--- fact 4
From 1961 Robben Island held political prisoners under apartheid, including Nelson Mandela, imprisoned on the island for 18 of his 27 years, from 1964, Robert Sobukwe, Walter Sisulu and Govan Mbeki.
--- fact 5
Prisoners worked in a limestone quarry, and the glare damaged Mandela's eyes; prisoners organized study and debate among themselves, and the prison was nicknamed a "university".
--- fact 6
The maximum-security political prison closed in 1991; a separate medium-security prison on the island for criminal prisoners closed in 1996.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Robben Island in 1999, and former prisoners lead tours of the island today.
--- context
Robben Island was first used as a place of banishment by the [[event/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]], and its years as an apartheid prison belong to the wider struggle over minority rule in the period of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]]. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Robben Island shows water used as a wall: for more than three centuries authorities sent people they wanted removed from society, exiles, patients and dissidents, to a place where isolation was cheap to enforce. The same isolation concentrated a generation of leaders who studied and argued together, so a prison also worked as a school and a network of ideas, an outcome its designers did not intend, and the Observer records the years of imprisonment and forced labor as suffering. This reading would weaken if accounts of prisoners' study and organization describe only a small, unrepresentative part of the prison population.
--- question 1
How did prisoners on Robben Island organize themselves, and how far did their organization reach beyond the island?
--- question 2
How should the island present its earlier uses, such as the leprosy colony and the exile of Khoikhoi leaders?
