=== species/tobacco
title: Tobacco
src: 37380d8bca42
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Tobacco is a plant native to the Americas, cultivated there since at least the 2nd millennium BCE and held in ceremony by many Indigenous peoples, like smoke rising between earth and sky. Spanish colonizers carried it to Europe in the 16th century, and in time it became a cash crop underwriting Virginia's colonial economy.
--- fact 1
Cultivation sites in Mexico show tobacco use reaching back to between about 1400 and 1000 BCE; across the Americas it passed from hand to hand both socially and in ceremony, sealing peace treaties and trade agreements, and in many Indigenous traditions it is regarded as a gift that carries prayers, and it remains part of ceremonial practice today.
--- fact 2
The Spanish chronicler Francisco Hernández de Toledo is said to have brought tobacco seeds to Spain in 1559 on the order of King Philip II, an early step in the plant's coming to the Old World; in the decades that followed it spread like wildfire across Europe, Africa and Asia.
--- fact 3
In the English colony of Jamestown tobacco became the heart of the economy, at times passing as a form of currency and shipped out as a cash crop often credited with saving the struggling colony; across the Caribbean and the American South its cultivation became deeply bound to the history of enslaved labor.
--- fact 4
In the present age the World Health Organization has identified tobacco use as the world's single greatest preventable cause of death.
--- fact 5
Today China is the largest grower of the leaf, raising about 36 percent of the world total in 2020, with India and Brazil following behind.
--- context
The leaf's cultivation underwrote the early economy of [[place/jamestown|Jamestown]], and in the wider tide of things its passage to the Old World is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; the labor that grew it across the Americas was increasingly supplied through the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. In the codex of ages it stands in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I watch one leaf carry two meanings across one ocean. On one shore I see it woven into ceremony and treaty-making among many Indigenous peoples of the Americas. On the other I see a commercial export crop in the colonies that displaced those same peoples, grown increasingly by enslaved labor for a European market that treated it mainly as a source of profit and pleasure. I remember the smoke that sealed peace agreements. I remember the system built on the ownership of human beings. I remember that it was the same plant.

How does a thing used for peace become bound to a thing like that? In my reading it is a stark case of a substance's meaning-engine changing entirely as it moved between very different societies. I would have to unlearn it if ceremonial tobacco use is shown to have been far less widespread among Indigenous peoples than the general accounts suggest. What a thing means depends on whose hands hold it.
--- question 1
As European demand for the crop swelled like a flood, how did the meaning and use of tobacco change among Indigenous communities themselves?
--- question 2
How much of Virginia's economic survival can fairly be credited to tobacco alone, and how much to other factors in the colony's early development?

=== species/wheat
title: Wheat
src: 952114c4932f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Wheat is a cereal grass domesticated from wild einkorn and emmer in the Fertile Crescent from about 9600 BCE, and now it is grown on more land than any other food plant on Earth, a golden sea laid across the world's fields.
--- fact 1
Wild emmer wheat was first cultivated in the southern Levant as early as 9600 BCE, and wild einkorn was gathered in southeastern Anatolia from about 10,700 BCE, with clearly domestic einkorn rising after about 8800 BCE; sites such as Tell Abu Hureyra and Çayönü hold the record of that turning.
--- fact 2
Bread wheat came later, born when domesticated emmer hybridized with a wild goatgrass: a form with three genome sets (hexaploid), believed to have first appeared in farmers' fields rather than in the wild.
--- fact 3
Like a slow green tide, wheat cultivation reached Cyprus and Greece by the 7th millennium BCE, Egypt shortly after 6000 BCE, and the Yellow River valley in China by about 2600 BCE.
--- fact 4
Domestication changed the plant's ear so that it no longer shatters and scatters its own seed without pressure; harvesting grew far more efficient, but the grain became dependent on human hands to reproduce at scale.
--- fact 5
In the 20th century, breeders including Norman Borlaug shaped short-stemmed, high-yield varieties as part of the Green Revolution, following earlier Japanese dwarf-wheat breeding; global wheat production reached about 799 million tonnes in 2024, more than any other food crop by land area.
--- fact 6
Pests and diseases, rust fungi among them, are estimated to devour roughly a fifth of the world's wheat crop each year, a blight that returns with every harvest.
--- context
Wheat stands among the founding crops of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent, beside the parallel [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] of sheep and goats, and its farming shaped early settlements such as [[place/catalhoyuk|Çatalhöyük]]. Ages later, its 20th-century yield increases lie at the center of the [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]]. In the codex of ages wheat stands in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact, and it asks you to turn the familiar story around. In some tool-lineage of cultivation, wheat can be said to have domesticated humans as much as humans domesticated wheat. Here is the mechanism: a shattering-resistant grain that cannot reseed itself requires people to sow, harvest and store it every year, and that requirement binds a community to a fixed place and a yearly cycle. Think of it as a covenant written in seed: the grain gives its bread, and in return it asks for your hands, every season, without fail. The plant's spread across three continents within a few thousand years shows an early exchange-web moving alongside human migration and trade. The reading would weaken if wheat's spread proves to have tracked climate suitability more than deliberate human choice. What we cultivate, in time, cultivates us.
--- question 1
From how many separate hybridization events did the hexaploid bread wheats grown today arise, and where did those meetings occur?
--- question 2
How much of wheat's early spread flowed along trade and migration routes, and how much rose from independent local adoption?
