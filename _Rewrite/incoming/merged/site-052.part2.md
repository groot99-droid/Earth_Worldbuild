=== person/sitting-bull
title: Sitting Bull
src: f19193b60afc
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sitting Bull (Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake, born between 1831 and 1837, died 1890) was a Hunkpapa Lakota leader and holy man who resisted United States expansion onto Lakota lands and stood in the camp at the Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876. Native police killed him in 1890.
--- fact 1
Sitting Bull was born sometime between 1831 and 1837, traditionally on the Grand River in present-day South Dakota (a 2007 family oral tradition instead places his birth on the Yellowstone River), and he became a leading Hunkpapa Lakota war leader and holy man.
--- fact 2
The Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868) guaranteed the Black Hills to the Lakota; after gold was found there in 1874, the United States sought to take the land, and war followed in 1876.
--- fact 3
On 25 and 26 June 1876, Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho warriors destroyed the force of Lt. Col. George Custer at the Little Bighorn. Sitting Bull was present in the large camp, and accounts describe his role there as spiritual leader and organizer rather than field commander.
--- fact 4
After the battle he led his followers into Canada in 1877, and he surrendered at Fort Buford in July 1881.
--- fact 5
On 15 December 1890, at Standing Rock, Native police sent to arrest him amid tension over the Ghost Dance killed him in a struggle; the Wounded Knee massacre followed on 29 December.
--- context
Sitting Bull's Lakota faced the same expansion of the United States that had driven earlier removals of eastern nations; see [[event/indian-removal-and-the-trail-of-tears|Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]]. He lived in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Sitting Bull is remembered as a war leader, though the accounts place him at the Little Bighorn as a spiritual leader, a case of the record following the battle's fame rather than the man's actual role. His killing by Native police employed by the very agency he opposed shows how a state can turn a community's own members into instruments of its claim-line. The reading would weaken if fuller Lakota accounts gave him a more central military role.
--- question 1
What do Lakota oral accounts themselves say about his role at the Little Bighorn?

=== person/sofonisba-anguissola
title: Sofonisba Anguissola
src: c4b2fdba0a17
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sofonisba Anguissola (about 1532 to 1629), an Italian portrait painter from Cremona, was one of the first women in Europe to build a professional career as a painter. She spent years at the Spanish court, traditionally put at about 14, and won international fame.
--- fact 1
Sofonisba was born about 1532 in Cremona, the eldest of seven children of Amilcare Anguissola, a minor nobleman, who gave all his daughters a humanist education; several became painters, and Sofonisba studied with Bernardino Campi and Bernardino Gatti.
--- fact 2
As a noblewoman she could not study anatomy from the nude, nor take on large public commissions, so she made portraiture her own field.
--- fact 3
Her father sent her drawings to Michelangelo, who reportedly challenged her to draw a crying boy; she answered with Boy Bitten by a Crayfish, a drawing that circulated widely.
--- fact 4
In 1559 she was invited to Madrid to serve Queen Elisabeth of Valois, wife of Philip II, as lady-in-waiting and painting teacher; she painted the royal court for a period traditionally given as about 14 years, though sources differ, and the Spanish court arranged her marriage to a Sicilian nobleman, Fabrizio de Moncada.
--- fact 5
After his death she married a Genoese sea captain and lived in Genoa and Palermo. In 1629 Anthony van Dyck visited her in Palermo (an earlier reading gave 1624) and recorded her advice; she died there in 1629 (older sources say 1625), in her mid-to-late nineties.
--- fact 6
Key works include The Chess Game (1555), Self-Portrait at the Easel (about 1556), and Portrait of Philip II of Spain (1565).
--- fact 7
Many of her court works were later credited to male painters such as Sánchez Coello, and scholars have since restored many of them to her name; the Prado's 2019 exhibition paired her with Lavinia Fontana.
--- context
Sofonisba worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]] and the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. [[person/michelangelo|Michelangelo]] had encouraged her, and her success opened a path for later women painters such as [[person/artemisia-gentileschi|Artemisia Gentileschi]]. Some scholars link her Boy Bitten by a Crayfish to [[person/caravaggio|Caravaggio]]'s Boy Bitten by a Lizard. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I look at the limits placed on her — no study from the nude, no large public commissions — and I see how a social rule became a new kind of picture, the genre she made entirely her own. I remember that her career depended, too, on a father who promoted her and a court that hired her: the kin-lattice, acting as an agency. I recognize how many of her court works were credited to male painters, and how attribution follows expectation more often than it follows the hand that held the brush. Restoring them to her is a correction still under way.
--- question 1
Which court portraits now credited to other painters were actually painted by Anguissola?

=== person/sor-juana-ines-de-la-cruz
title: Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz
src: 8c846cfeaf2c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz (born 1648 or 1651, died 1695) was a nun, poet and playwright in colonial Mexico City, regarded as one of the leading Spanish-language writers of the seventeenth century and an early defender of women's right to study.
--- fact 1
Juana Ramírez de Asbaje was born at San Miguel Nepantla, near Mexico City; baptismal records point to 1648 or 1651, and the year is debated. She died on 17 April 1695.
--- fact 2
Largely self-taught, she served at the viceroy's court in her teens, then in 1669 entered the Hieronymite convent of San Jerónimo.
--- fact 3
She wrote lyric poems, plays such as Los empeños de una casa (1683), and the long poem Primero sueño; her first collection, Inundación castálida, was printed in Madrid in 1689.
--- fact 4
In 1690 the Bishop of Puebla published her critique of a sermon by António Vieira as the Carta atenagórica, and, writing as "Sor Filotea," urged her toward religious study; she answered in 1691 with the Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz, defending women's right to learn.
--- fact 5
In 1693 and 1694 she stopped writing and signed penitential documents; whether she sold her library or had it taken from her, and how far the choice was hers, is still debated.
--- fact 6
She died in the plague of 1695, while nursing her fellow sisters.
--- context
Sor Juana lived in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], in a Mexico City built over [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] and ruled by Spain after the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I see that the convent gave her a room, books and time, at the price of her vows. I recognize the meaning-engine that once sheltered her writing as the same one that later restrained it. A specialist could argue that her silence in 1693 was her own choice, or that the surviving documents are merely formulaic — either reading would weaken the story of her as simply silenced.
--- question 1
Was her final silence chosen, forced upon her, or both at once?
--- question 2
Which accounts of her library's fate actually rest on surviving documents?

=== person/sukarno
title: Sukarno
src: 678f835e905a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sukarno (1901 to 1970) led Indonesia's independence movement and became its first president. He proclaimed independence in 1945, ruled until 1967, and was removed from power after the killings of 1965 and 1966.
--- fact 1
Sukarno was born on 6 June 1901 in Surabaya, in the Dutch East Indies, and died on 21 June 1970 in Jakarta, under house arrest.
--- fact 2
He founded the Indonesian National Party in 1927; the Dutch arrested him in 1929 and released him in 1931, then exiled him to Flores in 1934 and to Sumatra in 1938.
--- fact 3
He cooperated with the Japanese occupation from 1942 to 1945, which forced millions into labor and whose food requisitions contributed to a famine that killed over a million people in Java in 1944 and 1945.
--- fact 4
On 1 June 1945 he presented Pancasila, five founding principles for the state, and on 17 August 1945 he proclaimed independence, becoming president the following day.
--- fact 5
He hosted the Bandung Conference of Asian and African states in 1955, and in 1959 introduced "Guided Democracy," ending parliamentary rule.
--- fact 6
After the 30 September Movement of 1965, an army-led campaign killed between 500,000 and over a million people, most of them communists and alleged sympathizers, and General Suharto took power in 1966 and 1967.
--- context
Sukarno worked in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], in the years of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] and the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a nation spread across thousands of islands was named and given five founding principles before its state fully existed — story-glue in its plainest form. He tried to hold nationalist, religious and communist camps under one roof, and the arrangement broke apart in 1965 in mass killing. A specialist could argue that army and foreign pressures made the collapse of Guided Democracy likely whatever he chose, which would weaken any reading that places the outcome in his leadership alone.
--- question 1
What role, if any, did he play in the events of 30 September 1965?
--- question 2
How much did his wartime cooperation with Japan cost, and how much did it actually gain?

=== person/suleiman-the-magnificent
title: Suleiman the Magnificent
src: 648fd8e7f8bb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Suleiman the Magnificent (1494 to 1566) ruled as Ottoman sultan from 1520 to 1566, presiding over the empire at its greatest territorial extent, military power, and legal and cultural achievement.
--- fact 1
His armies captured Belgrade in 1521 and Rhodes in 1522, then pushed deep into Central Europe, defeating and killing the King of Hungary at the Battle of Mohacs in 1526, though a first siege of Vienna in 1529 failed to take the city.
--- fact 2
Known by the epithet Kanuni, the Lawgiver, an epithet absent from sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Ottoman sources and one that may date only from the early 18th century, he issued a major legal code that harmonized Ottoman civil law with Islamic religious law across the empire's diverse territories.
--- fact 3
His navies, under admirals such as Barbarossa Hayreddin Pasha, contested control of the Mediterranean with Spain and its allies, while Ottoman forces also pushed into Persia and Mesopotamia, capturing Baghdad in 1534.
--- fact 4
His reign saw Ottoman architecture flourish under the chief architect [[person/mimar-sinan|Mimar Sinan]], alongside court poetry, painting and decorative arts, all centered on Istanbul.
--- context
Suleiman led the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] from [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], extending its reach across three continents. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Suleiman's failed siege of Vienna in 1529, rather than his many successes, marked the practical limit of Ottoman expansion into Central Europe for the rest of his reign; the empire's reputation for irresistible advance rested on decades of victories that stopped, in the end, at one unsuccessful siege.
--- question 1
How far did overextended supply lines, rather than Habsburg defense alone, decide the failure of the 1529 siege of Vienna?

=== person/sun-tzu
title: Sun Tzu
src: 71d51a082186
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sun Tzu (traditionally about 544 to 496 BCE) is the name attached to The Art of War, the most influential text on military strategy from ancient China, said to have served the state of Wu; whether he was one historical person at all is disputed.
--- fact 1
Tradition places Sun Tzu, personal name Sun Wu, as a general who advised King Helü of Wu in the late 6th century BCE; The Art of War is organized into about 13 short chapters.
--- fact 2
The historian Sima Qian, writing centuries later, told a story of Sun Tzu proving his discipline by drilling the king's concubines and executing two who disobeyed; scholars treat this as legend.
--- fact 3
His name appears in no text earlier than Sima Qian's history, and one theory holds that "Sun Wu" was an invented title, with The Art of War actually the work of his supposed descendant, Sun Bin.
--- fact 4
In 1972 bamboo strips from a Han tomb at Yinqueshan turned up versions of both The Art of War and a separate text by Sun Bin, confirming that The Art of War circulated early and that Sun Bin was a distinct author.
--- fact 5
The text has shaped military and business thought for over two thousand years, and it is translated worldwide.
--- context
Sun Tzu's traditional dates place him in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] near the end of the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]], roughly contemporary with [[person/confucius|Confucius]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a text on organized, calculated sanctioned harm has outlived any certainty about who wrote it, carried forward in mark-code through copies made centuries after the events it claims. The 1972 tomb find settled that the text existed early, without settling who composed it. A specialist could argue that "Sun Tzu" was a legendary attribution rather than one single author, which would weaken any biographical reading of the text's origin.
--- question 1
Was Sun Tzu a real individual, a legendary figure, or simply a name attached to a composite text?
--- question 2
How much of The Art of War as read today actually reflects the earliest version found at Yinqueshan?
