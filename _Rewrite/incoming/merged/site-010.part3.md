=== culture/malacca-sultanate
title: Malacca Sultanate
src: de2c00526293
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Malacca (Melaka) Sultanate was a Malay port state on the Strait of Malacca, founded about 1400, a gate through which the sea's wealth poured. In the 15th century it became the leading trading port of Southeast Asia, and it helped spread Islam through the archipelago before the Portuguese took it in 1511.
--- fact 1
Parameswara, a half-Javanese nobleman from Palembang in Sumatra, sought a foothold at Temasik (Singapore) before, about 1400, he planted the kingdom of Melaka like a banner on a new shore.
--- fact 2
According to tradition, Parameswara married the daughter of a Muslim ruler and converted to Islam, taking the name Iskandar and the title of sultan. From Melaka the faith flowed outward like a current, and the city became instrumental in the spread of Islam through the Malay world.
--- fact 3
In the 15th and early 16th century it grew into the most important port in Southeast Asia, an entrepôt where silks, spices, and porcelain ran through like water through a sluice, and local tin with them.
--- fact 4
The Portuguese, unable to negotiate a friendly trading arrangement, invaded the city in 1511 and drove the sultan to flight. The fall of Melaka marked the beginning of European colonial power in the region, the opening crack of a long storm.
--- context
Malacca took up the mantle of the older maritime empire of [[culture/srivijaya|Srivijaya]] and contended with the [[culture/majapahit|Majapahit]] state of Java. It sat at the heart of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] like a pearl in the sea's throat, and its capture came in the wake of the [[event/portuguese-sea-route-to-india|Portuguese Sea Route to India]].
--- observer
I look at a port and see its worth: a strait, and nothing more, and everything. I look at a ruling house that took on a new meaning-engine in a way that also suited its trade. Was it faith, or harbour sense, or were they never separate? Then 1511, and the Portuguese seizure: an exchange-web captured at its narrowest point. Whoever holds the narrows holds the sea.
--- question 1
How much of the conversion story rests on later Malay tradition, and how much on records set down at the time?
--- question 2
How many people lived in Melaka, and how did the city hold so many merchant communities in balance?

=== culture/mali-empire
title: Mali Empire
src: 970e39dbbb17
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Mali Empire was a West African state, founded about 1235 by Sundiata Keita, that held the trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt in its grip, the sun-metal and the white treasure of the desert passing through its hands.
--- fact 1
In the 14th century it stood among the largest empires in Africa, its shadow long across the land.
--- fact 2
Oral tradition keeps the epic of Sundiata alive, carried in the voices of griots.
--- fact 3
The rulers were Muslim, and scholarship bloomed like water-fed gardens in cities such as [[place/timbuktu|Timbuktu]].
--- fact 4
From the 15th century its power waned, as the Songhai Empire rose like a new moon.
--- context
See [[person/mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]] and [[place/timbuktu|Timbuktu]]. The empire was a bright strand in the exchange-web that bound [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] to the Islamic world.
--- observer
I lean toward the page. I lean toward ink, toward what can be shelved and counted. And then I listen to a society whose memory lives in a hereditary class of oral historians, voice handed down like an heirloom. What did I think a record was? Writing is one of several successful ways to keep records. The voice was an archive all along.
--- question 1
How tightly did the empire's rule gather to its center?

=== culture/maori
title: Maori
src: 4e3df602211e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Maori are the Polynesian people of Aotearoa (New Zealand), who came ashore on the islands around 1250 to 1300 CE and grew there a distinct culture and language, as a seed carried over the sea takes root in new soil.
--- fact 1
Radiocarbon dating suggests the first settlement of New Zealand came around 1250 to 1300 CE, a date read from carbon's slow clock.
--- fact 2
The giant flightless moa was hunted to extinction within roughly a century or two of settlement, and the land lost its tallest walkers.
--- fact 3
Maori society was woven around kin groups (whanau, hapu, and iwi), the strong cords of its making.
--- fact 4
The Treaty of Waitangi was signed with the British Crown in 1840, and disputes over its meaning continue, its words still contested like a shore the tide will not settle.
--- fact 5
In 1987 te reo Maori became an official language of New Zealand, a voice set into the law of the land.
--- context
The Maori were the last to settle a major landmass in the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]], the closing verse of a long ocean song. Their relationship with the British Crown is defined by the [[event/treaty-of-waitangi|Treaty of Waitangi]]. In the chronicle's codex, the Maori belong to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I trace the map of human arrival and come to its last blank: the last large habitable landmass on Earth, settled by boat only about 750 years ago, well within historical time. So late. So recent. So remembered. How many peoples can name the voyage that brought them? These did, and so their genealogies are unusually precise records of migration. A lineage recited is a map kept alive.
--- question 1
From which shores of Polynesia did the settlers set out?

=== culture/mapuche
title: Mapuche
src: e06ed875dbc7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mapuche are the Indigenous people of south-central Chile and Argentina, who resisted both the Inca and the Spanish and kept their independence for centuries. Think of their history as an architecture of refusal that held.
--- fact 1
Consider the threshold drawn, according to Spanish chroniclers, at the Maule River: there the Mapuche halted the Inca advance in the late 15th century.
--- fact 2
The Arauco War with Spain ran from about 1546 to 1662, and it was given a nickname: the 'Indian Flanders'.
--- fact 3
Through the Parliament of Quillin in 1641, sovereignty was written into a river: Spain recognized the Bio-Bio river as a border and agreed to withdraw to its north.
--- fact 4
Chile's Occupation of the Araucania (1861 to 1883) incorporated Mapuche territory, and the ledger turned: sources cite that the Mapuche lost most of their land and were confined to reservations.
--- context
Picture the map: the Mapuche lived south of the reach of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]], and of the Spanish empire after the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. They kept their political autonomy longer than most, and that is the point to hold onto.
--- observer
The Observer notes a people that negotiated as a sovereign party with a global empire, on the ground of a treaty, for over two centuries. Sit with that. We are taught to picture conquest as a single stroke, yet here is a covenant kept in negotiation across generations. The record of Indigenous diplomacy is longer than colonial narratives often suggest. Sovereignty is not only held; it is argued for, again and again.
--- question 1
How did the Mapuche shape the architecture of their military and political life?
