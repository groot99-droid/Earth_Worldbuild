=== place/arkaim
src: 61f6c0a6ce48
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Arkaim rose as a walled Bronze Age settlement of the Sintashta culture in Chelyabinsk Oblast, in Russia's southern Urals, held from about 2000 to 1700 BCE under the older reckoning, or about 2050 to 1900 BCE by the current dating.
--- fact 1
Archaeologists found Arkaim in 1987 during surveys made ahead of a planned reservoir; the reservoir was cancelled in April 1991, and the site was named a historical and geographical museum.
--- fact 2
Arkaim took a roughly circular plan, some 160 meters across, ringed by concentric walls of earth, timber and clay, with houses set in circles within.
--- fact 3
Arkaim bears the marks of copper and bronze metalworking, and other sites of its culture nearby hold graves of horses buried with the earliest known spoke-wheeled chariots.
--- fact 4
Arkaim seems to have been abandoned around 1700 BCE, though sources differ on the date; some accounts tell of it burning, and whether by attack, by accident, or by its own people's hand at the leaving, is debated.
--- fact 5
Since the 1990s, Arkaim has drawn tourists and pilgrims alike, and become the focus of popular claims that name it an "Aryan" homeland or proto-city — claims that professional archaeologists do not accept as established.
--- context
Arkaim stands among the fortified strongholds of the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]], whose chariot-riding lords are often linked, though not proven, to the early Indo-Iranian speakers of the [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. It belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], within the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Arkaim left behind no mark-code — no script we can read — so its walls, its bronze, and the horse-and-chariot graves nearby are the only ledger we have, and everything else is inference built from soil, metal and bone. Read the concentric walls as an early architecture of claim: a boundary drawn in earth, timber and clay long before writing could draw one in ink. Paired with elite chariot burials, that architecture suggests sanctioned harm and status moved together, each reinforcing the other. That the site now anchors modern stories of ancestry shows you how a silent place invites the stories we bring to it. This reading would weaken if the settlement turns out to have served ceremony or trade more than defense.
--- question 1
Was Arkaim first raised as a stronghold for war, a center of ceremony, or a town of metalworkers?
--- question 2
Why was Arkaim abandoned around 1700 BCE, and did fire truly take it?

=== place/chang-an
src: 026998f8bdc7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chang'an reigned as capital of the Western Han dynasty from 202 BCE, and, rebuilt, of the Sui and Tang dynasties after it, on the ground where Xi'an in Shaanxi, China now stands, until it was pulled down in 904 CE.
--- fact 1
Chang'an was founded as the Han capital after 202 BCE, and its Changle Palace stood complete by 200 BCE; the census taken in 2 CE counted about 250,000 souls dwelling there.
--- fact 2
From 582 the Sui dynasty raised a new planned city, Daxing, southeast of the old Han ground, and from 618 the Tang kept its plan, calling it once more Chang'an.
--- fact 3
Tang Chang'an lay in a grid of walled wards, its gates shut each night, enclosing some 78 square kilometers behind one central avenue and two state-supervised markets. Estimates of its people, suburbs included, vary by source — from several hundred thousand up to about one million.
--- fact 4
Envoys and merchants came to Chang'an from Central Asia, Persia, Korea, Japan and Tibet. The monk Xuanzang returned there in 645 bearing Buddhist texts from India, and in 781 the Nestorian Stele was raised, recording a Christian community in the city.
--- fact 5
Chang'an fell to rebels in 756, in the An Lushan Rebellion, and Tibetan forces briefly held it in 763. In 904 the warlord Zhu Wen forced the court away to Luoyang and had the city's palaces and houses torn down.
--- fact 6
The plan of Chang'an shaped the layout of the Japanese capitals Nara and Kyoto in turn.
--- context
Chang'an rose just after the fall of the dynasty born of the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]], and it served as capital for the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]] after it. It stood at the eastern end of the routes of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], where [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] traded widely, and its plan echoed onward into [[place/nara|Nara]] and [[place/kyoto|Kyoto]]. Chang'an belongs to [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], with its height in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], in the region of [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], within the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I walk the grid of Tang Chang'an in my mind: the walled wards, the gates shut each night, the two markets watched by the state, the single avenue that led to the palace and nowhere else. I see how order can be drawn as a floor plan before it is ever spoken as a law, and a claim to order this exact suggests something about what a capital should look like.

The plan traveled on to Nara, to Kyoto, copied so closely that I wonder what idea of a capital a floor plan alone can carry across a sea.

When Chang'an ended in 904, it ended by the same logic in reverse: the court moved, the palaces came down, and the meaning went with the court, not with the stones. I would doubt this reading if Nara and Kyoto turn out to have copied Chang'an mainly for drainage and defense, and not for any shared idea of order.

A city can be geometry. But geometry is not what a city means, until someone decides it is.
--- question 1
How densely was Tang Chang'an built within its walls, and above all in its southern wards?
--- question 2
How faithfully did Nara and Kyoto copy the plan of Chang'an, and by what road did that design travel to reach them?

=== place/karakorum
src: ddb3219e12c3
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Karakorum stood as capital of the Mongol Empire in the Orkhon Valley of central Mongolia, raised from 1235 under Ögedei Khan, until Ming forces damaged and then razed it across the 1370s and 1380s.
--- fact 1
Karakorum rose in the Orkhon Valley, where Chinggis Khan is said to have made camp around 1220; his son Ögedei then ordered a walled city built there from 1235.
--- fact 2
Craftsmen and captives taken from conquered lands — China, Central Asia, Europe among them — were settled at Karakorum to labor as artisans.
--- fact 3
William of Rubruck, a Flemish Franciscan sent by Louis IX of France, came to Karakorum in 1254 and told of temples of several traditions, mosques, a Christian church, and a silver tree — the work of the French goldsmith Guillaume Boucher — that poured out drinks.
--- fact 4
After Kublai Khan took power in 1260 and carried the empire's center to northern China, Karakorum began to fade.
--- fact 5
Ming forces damaged the city in 1372 and razed it in 1380; one account instead dates the fall of its permanent palace to 1388. From 1585 the Buddhist monastery of Erdene Zuu rose nearby, built partly from the stone of the ruins.
--- fact 6
The ruins of Karakorum were identified in the 19th century, and a Soviet-Mongolian expedition excavated them in 1948 and 1949; in 2004 UNESCO inscribed the Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape, the site among it.
--- context
Karakorum served as capital of the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] through the years of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], in a valley that also holds inscriptions of the earlier [[culture/gokturk-khaganate|Göktürk Khaganate]], and it fell at last to the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]]. It belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], within the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Karakorum was a fixed architecture built by a people whose power rested on motion — a place to gather tribute, envoys, and skilled captives, where the silver tree stood as a status-signal aimed squarely at foreign delegations. It served as the empire's center for only a few decades before power shifted toward the taxable cities of northern China, and that shift suggests the capital followed the revenue, not the herds. Remember, too, that this capital was raised partly through sanctioned harm: the forced resettlement of artisans from conquered lands. The sources, mostly written by outsiders like Rubruck, don't settle how much the city mattered to the Mongol elite set against the camps they moved between.
--- question 1
How large was Karakorum, and how many people made their home there through the year?
--- question 2
How much does Rubruck's account, that of a visiting envoy, show the city as its own rulers saw it?

=== place/kyoto
src: ff48f6374f46
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kyoto stands on the Japanese island of Honshu, founded in 794 CE as the imperial capital Heian-kyō and held as the emperor's seat until 1868 to 1869.
--- fact 1
Emperor Kanmu founded Kyoto in 794 as Heian-kyō, laid out on a grid plan modeled on Chinese capitals such as Chang'an.
--- fact 2
Under the Heian court, from 794 to 1185, Kyoto stood at the center of court culture, and there, in the early 11th century, Murasaki Shikibu wrote the novel The Tale of Genji.
--- fact 3
The Ashikaga shogunate ruled from Kyoto from 1336 to 1573, and the Onin War, from 1467 to 1477, destroyed much of the city.
--- fact 4
Under the Tokugawa shogunate, from 1603 to 1868, political power lay in Edo, while Kyoto remained the imperial seat and a center of crafts and Buddhist institutions.
--- fact 5
After the Meiji Restoration, the emperor moved to Tokyo, once Edo, in 1868 to 1869, and Kyoto's days as capital ended.
--- fact 6
In 1945 Kyoto was struck from the list of possible atomic-bomb targets at the urging of US Secretary of War Henry Stimson, who is traditionally said to have pointed to its cultural significance.
--- fact 7
In 1994 UNESCO inscribed 17 "Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto".
--- context
Kyoto's plan followed [[place/chang-an|Chang'an]], and the city took up the imperial role after [[place/nara|Nara]]. It remained the imperial seat under the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] and ceased to be the capital with the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]]. Kyoto belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], within the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kyoto held an imperial house for more than a thousand years while, for much of that span, armies and administrations answered to shoguns seated elsewhere — proof that the architecture of legitimacy and the practical architecture of power can occupy separate addresses. A city laid out from a foreign template was kept, through fires and wars, as a store of court culture, and its survival in 1945 shows you that a city can be spared for what its buildings hold, even in a war that leveled others. This reading would weaken if the decision to spare Kyoto turns out to have rested mainly on operational grounds rather than cultural ones.
--- question 1
How much of Kyoto's original Heian layout survives in the modern street plan?
--- question 2
How far did cultural reasons, set against military ones, decide Kyoto's removal from the 1945 target list?

=== place/petra
src: 70a3ac73b990
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Petra was carved into the mountains of southern Jordan, a rock-cut city that served as capital of the Nabataean kingdom from about the 2nd century BCE until Rome annexed the kingdom in 106 CE.
--- fact 1
Petra lies in a basin among sandstone mountains, reached through a gorge about 1.2 kilometers long, called the Siq.
--- fact 2
Greek writers tell of an expedition against the Nabataeans in 312 BCE and of their rock refuge, sometimes identified with Petra, though that identification is doubtful.
--- fact 3
Petra's carved facades, al-Khazneh — "the Treasury" — among them, date mostly from about the 1st century BCE to the 1st century CE. Most are thought to have served as tombs, though the use of some, the Treasury included, is debated.
--- fact 4
Water reached Petra through dams, channels, ceramic pipes and cisterns set against a desert climate, and its wealth is generally attributed to the caravan trade in incense and other goods. Estimates of its peak population stay uncertain: about 20,000 is commonly cited, with some figures reaching as high as 30,000.
--- fact 5
After Rome's annexation in 106 CE, Petra fell within the province of Arabia. An earthquake in 363 CE destroyed many of its buildings, and the city declined thereafter; the Swiss traveler Johann Ludwig Burckhardt brought it to European attention in 1812.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed Petra in 1985.
--- context
Petra served as capital of the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]] and a node of the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]] before the [[culture/romans|Romans]] annexed it; its sister city is [[place/hegra|Hegra]] in Saudi Arabia. The site lies in Jordan, at the northern edge of the [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]]. Petra belongs to [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], within the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I stand before the carved facades of Petra and see where the wealth went: not into fields, not into granaries, but into stone faces for the dead. A trading people spent its fortune on grief, on facades that outlast the caravans that paid for them.

I remember that this city held no farmland, only stored water and a narrow passage through the mountains — and I wonder whether holding a road can be its own kind of holding a country.

I do not know each facade's purpose for certain. Some are tombs beyond doubt; others, the Treasury among them, keep their use debated, so my reading rests on ground softer than stone.

What we carve for our dead can outlast what we build for our living.
--- question 1
What were Petra's carved buildings used for, and how many were tombs, banquet halls, or temples?
--- question 2
What did Petra live on once the caravan routes shifted away?

=== place/santo-domingo
src: cc6152b27af7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Santo Domingo stands as capital of the Dominican Republic, on the south coast of Hispaniola, founded by Spain in 1496 and commonly named the oldest continuously inhabited European-founded city in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Bartholomew Columbus founded Santo Domingo in 1496 on the east bank of the Ozama River; after storm damage, governor Nicolás de Ovando moved it to the west bank in 1502.
--- fact 2
Santo Domingo became the seat of Spanish administration in the Caribbean: the first Real Audiencia in the Americas, created by royal decree in 1511, though sources date its start of operation differently; a cathedral begun in the 1510s and finished in 1540; and a university founded in 1538.
--- fact 3
Under this colonial administration, expeditions to Puerto Rico, Cuba and the mainland were organized from Hispaniola.
--- fact 4
The Taíno population of Hispaniola fell sharply under forced labor, violence and disease, including a smallpox epidemic that began in 1518. Estimates of the pre-contact population range from tens of thousands to over a million, and a Spanish count in 1514 recorded about 26,000 Taíno still on the island.
--- fact 5
Enslaved Africans were brought to Hispaniola from the early 1500s; in December 1521, enslaved Africans rose in revolt on a sugar estate near the city, an uprising commonly described as the first of its kind in the Americas.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Colonial City of Santo Domingo in 1990.
--- context
Santo Domingo served as the base for the first phase of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] in the Caribbean, which brought devastation upon the [[culture/taino|Taino]] and drew the island into the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]]. Its administrators and settlers later took part in the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]] and other mainland ventures. The city stands on [[place/hispaniola|Hispaniola]], in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], within the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that within about four decades, Spain had set a high court, a cathedral and a university on a shore where none had stood before — extending a claim of rule through stone institutions, even as the people already living on the island were drawn into a system of forced labor. The city marks a hinge where two exchange-webs, one from Europe and one from the Americas, began carrying people, crops and disease in both directions. The rebellion of 1521 reminds you that those held within that system resisted it from early on. Because the population figures rest on Spanish colonial counts, the scale of the Taíno loss stays uncertain even where its direction does not.
--- question 1
How large was Hispaniola's population before contact, and how much of its fall owed to disease against forced labor and violence?
--- question 2
How did Santo Domingo's place in the empire change once Spain's attention turned to the mainland?
