=== person/marie-curie
title: Marie Curie
src: 689fb8649040
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Marie Curie (1867 to 1934), a Polish-born physicist and chemist, pioneered the study of radioactivity and remains the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.
--- fact 1
In 1903 she shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel.
--- fact 2
In 1911 she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for her discovery of polonium and radium.
--- fact 3
She developed mobile X-ray units for use in World War I hospitals.
--- fact 4
She died of aplastic anemia, likely brought on by long exposure to radiation.
--- context
Her work on radioactivity underlies [[technology/nuclear-fission|Nuclear Fission]] and the [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]]. Compare her with [[person/albert-einstein|Albert Einstein]].
--- observer
I picture a world that offered a woman in science almost no opening, and I picture her making one anyway. I remember that her notebooks are still radioactive today, decades after she set them down. The past does not only leave a record on paper — sometimes it leaves a trace you can still measure. Some inheritances glow long after the person who left them is gone.
--- question 1
How did she make her way around the barriers facing women in science?

=== person/mary-cassatt
title: Mary Cassatt
src: a1f32204b2b0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mary Cassatt (22 May 1844 to 14 June 1926), an American painter who spent most of her adult life in France, was the only American to exhibit with the French Impressionists, and is celebrated for tender, unsentimental images of women and children.
--- fact 1
Cassatt was born into a wealthy family in Allegheny City, now part of Pittsburgh. Against her father's wishes she studied at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts from about 1860, then departed for Paris in 1866.
--- fact 2
Since women could not enter the École des Beaux-Arts, she studied privately and copied the Old Masters in the Louvre. Frustrated with the Salon's conservative taste, she accepted Degas's invitation in 1877 to exhibit with the Impressionists, and showed alongside them in 1879, 1880, 1881 and 1886.
--- fact 3
Her parents and sister moved to Paris to live with her, and her own family became her favourite models.
--- fact 4
An 1890 exhibition of Japanese prints in Paris inspired a set of ten colour prints that combined drypoint and aquatint, from which she took flat colour, strong outlines and cropped compositions.
--- fact 5
In 1893 she painted the mural Modern Woman for the Woman's Building at the Chicago World's Columbian Exposition; the mural has since been lost.
--- fact 6
She advised Louisine Havemeyer and other Americans on buying Impressionist works and, according to some sources, Old Masters as well, many of which now hang in the Metropolitan Museum, and she supported the women's suffrage movement.
--- fact 7
Failing eyesight forced her to stop working around 1914. Key works include Little Girl in a Blue Armchair (1878), The Child's Bath (1893) and The Boating Party (1893 to 1894).
--- context
Cassatt worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] as an American within the Impressionist circle of [[person/claude-monet|Claude Monet]]; she drew on Japanese prints, including those of [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]], and supported the [[event/women-s-suffrage-movements|Women's Suffrage Movements]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I remember that the formal school shut its door to her, and I remember that she found her way in anyway, through private study and through a friend's invitation, the informal paths so many women's careers were built on. I look at her subject — the daily work of care inside the kin-lattice — and I see a canvas that painting had rarely thought large enough to hold it. Her advice to collectors even decided where the movement's pictures would end up, in American museums, a redistribution of art by taste and money as much as by merit. A door closed in one place can still be walked around.
--- question 1
How far did her advice to collectors shape which Impressionist works entered American collections?

=== person/matthias-corvinus
title: Matthias Corvinus
src: c8b601d0531d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Matthias Corvinus (1443 to 1490) reigned as King of Hungary from 1458 to 1490, a Renaissance patron-king who built one of Europe's finest libraries and modernized his kingdom's army and administration.
--- fact 1
He was elected king at age fourteen after the deaths of his father, the military commander John Hunyadi, and his older brother; his mother's political maneuvering, along with his uncle Michael Szilágyi's troops intimidating the barons, secured him the throne, though sources differ on whether a bribe to the nobility also played a part.
--- fact 2
He built the Black Army, a mostly mercenary standing force notable for its time, and used it to campaign against the Ottoman Empire, Bohemia, and the Holy Roman Empire, capturing Vienna in 1485.
--- fact 3
He assembled the Bibliotheca Corviniana, described as the largest library in Europe north of the Alps and second only to the Vatican Library, employing scribes and scholars to copy classical and contemporary texts; much of it was later scattered or destroyed after the Ottoman conquest of Hungary.
--- fact 4
He centralized royal justice and taxation, cutting into the independent power of the Hungarian nobility, though weaker successors substantially reversed his reforms after his death.
--- context
Matthias Corvinus ruled Hungary within [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], balancing conflicts with both the Ottoman Empire and the Holy Roman Empire. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Matthias's centralizing reforms rested heavily on his own standing army and personal authority rather than on durable institutions, so that much of his state-building unraveled within a generation of his death — a pattern common among strong rulers who do not also build the architecture that outlasts them.
--- question 1
How much of the Bibliotheca Corviniana reflected Matthias's own intellectual interests, and how much reflected the prestige-collecting expected of any Renaissance monarch?

=== person/menelik-ii
title: Menelik II
src: 6d1dcd63f3fb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Menelik II (1844 to 1913) reigned as emperor of Ethiopia from 1889, and led the forces that defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896.
--- fact 1
He expanded the empire southward and founded Addis Ababa as its capital.
--- fact 2
He imported modern arms and pursued the technological modernization of his state.
--- fact 3
On 1 March 1896 he won the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]].
--- fact 4
His empire's expansion came through the conquest of neighboring peoples, and how to judge that expansion is debated.
--- context
Menelik led Ethiopia through the era of the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]]. See [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler could resist one colonial power while building an empire of his own within his neighborhood. The line between the colonized and the colonizer was not always a simple one.
--- question 1
How did Menelik's expansion affect the neighboring peoples it conquered?

=== person/michelangelo
title: Michelangelo
src: 542c7a29afa3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Michelangelo Buonarroti (6 March 1475 to 18 February 1564), an Italian sculptor, painter, architect and poet, defined the High Renaissance through his Pietà, his David, and his Sistine Chapel paintings, and became the first Western artist to have a biography published in his own lifetime.
--- fact 1
Michelangelo was born at Caprese in Tuscany and raised in Florence. At 13 he was apprenticed to Domenico Ghirlandaio, then moved on to the sculpture garden of Lorenzo de' Medici, where he studied the ancient statues there.
--- fact 2
The Pietà (1498 to 1499) made his name in Rome, and he carved the David in Florence (1501 to 1504).
--- fact 3
In 1505 Pope Julius II summoned him to Rome to design a colossal tomb, then turned him instead toward painting the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508 to 1512); the unfinished tomb went on to haunt him for decades.
--- fact 4
In 1534 he settled permanently in Rome and painted The Last Judgment (1536 to 1541). In 1546 he became chief architect of St Peter's Basilica, and the dome he designed became a model for later domes.
--- fact 5
He wrote hundreds of poems, many addressed to Tommaso de' Cavalieri and Vittoria Colonna. He died in Rome, and his body was secretly carried to Florence for burial in Santa Croce.
--- fact 6
He carved directly, often without full-size models, and studied anatomy through dissection. A cleaning of the Sistine ceiling in the 1980s and 1990s revealed colours that were vivid, even acidic.
--- fact 7
Vasari's Lives (1550) closed with him, and Ascanio Condivi published a biography of him in 1553. He had studied the frescoes of Giotto.
--- context
Michelangelo worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]], within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. He studied [[person/giotto|Giotto]]'s frescoes. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I think of a man who believed a sculpture only frees a figure already waiting inside the stone, and I think of the decades he spent in service to popes, the chief patrons of that age's meaning-engine. I remember that the heroic nude body became a standard image for sacred and civic subjects alike, and I remember that the David turned a block of stone into story-glue for an entire city. A biography published in his own lifetime is an early sign of the artist becoming a named public figure. Vasari and Condivi wrote as admirers, so the portrait they left behind is not a neutral one. Even praise, written close enough to its subject, still casts a shadow.
--- question 1
How much of the standard portrait of Michelangelo comes from admiring biographers, rather than from his own letters and poems?
