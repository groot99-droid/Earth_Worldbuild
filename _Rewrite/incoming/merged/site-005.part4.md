=== place/elmina-castle
src: 5491ade725cc
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Elmina Castle is a fortress on the coast of Ghana, built by the Portuguese in 1482 as São Jorge da Mina, the first European fort on the Gulf of Guinea, and later a holding place for people sold into the Atlantic slave trade. Its walls carry both histories, and so must we.
--- fact 1
Elmina Castle stands at the town of Elmina in Ghana's Central Region; the Portuguese built it in 1482 by agreement with local rulers, to trade for gold, and named it São Jorge da Mina ("Saint George of the Mine"), a covenant of commerce set in stone.
--- fact 2
In the 16th century the Portuguese also brought enslaved people to Elmina from elsewhere on the African coast and exchanged them for gold with African traders, entering human beings into the same ledger as metal.
--- fact 3
The walls stayed and the owners changed: the Dutch captured Elmina in 1637, and Portugal ceded its claims in 1642; under Dutch rule the castle became a base of the Dutch West India Company, whose trade came to include the large-scale export of enslaved people to the Americas.
--- fact 4
Enslaved people were held in dungeons in the castle before being shipped across the Atlantic; how many passed through Elmina in all is unknown, and that silence is part of the record.
--- fact 5
Britain took over the Dutch Gold Coast possessions in 1872, and in 1957 Ghana became independent.
--- fact 6
In 1979 UNESCO inscribed the Forts and Castles of Ghana, Elmina among them.
--- fact 7
Today Elmina Castle is a museum and a memorial, a threshold where the living come to remember, and Ghana's "Year of Return" in 2019 drew large numbers of visitors from the African diaspora.
--- context
Elmina Castle was a node in the architecture of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]], which ended in its legal form with the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]], and Ghana's independence in 1957 belongs to the wider story of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]]. It sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Elmina began as a fortified place for trading gold and became a holding place for people sold as commodities, so one set of walls served two exchange-webs in sequence. Consider what that means: the same walls, the same stone, first for metal and then for human beings.

The Observer records the suffering of those held in its dungeons and shipped across the Atlantic, people with lives of their own, and notes that the site now works as a memorial where descendants come to mark losses for which few individual records survive, a grief-rite built around a building. The reading would weaken if better records show that most enslaved people shipped from the Gold Coast passed through other forts and not through Elmina. When the archive falls silent, we keep faith through the place itself.
--- question 1
How many people, in all, passed through Elmina Castle on their way to the Americas?
--- question 2
How did the town of Elmina itself take part in the trade, and how was it changed by it?

=== place/forbidden-city
src: 153a86b4f02a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Forbidden City is the former imperial palace complex at the heart of Beijing, China, built from 1406 to 1420 for the Ming dynasty and, until 1924, the residence and ceremonial center of Ming and Qing emperors, a walled sun around which a court turned.
--- fact 1
From 1406 to 1420, under the Yongle Emperor, the Forbidden City rose as part of his move of the Ming capital from Nanjing to Beijing, a throne carried like a flame from one hearth to another.
--- fact 2
Twenty-four emperors, 14 Ming and 10 Qing, lived and ruled within its walls, one after another like beads on a single cord.
--- fact 3
The Forbidden City spreads across about 72 hectares, girdled by walls about 8 meters high and a moat, and roughly 980 surviving buildings stand along its north-south axis like vertebrae along a spine.
--- fact 4
Commoners were barred from entering, and yellow glazed roof tiles, the color of the sun at noon, were reserved for imperial use.
--- fact 5
Puyi, the last Qing emperor, abdicated in 1912 after the Xinhai Revolution but was allowed to remain in the Inner Court, like a last ember in a cooling hearth, until 1924, when he was expelled; in 1925 the Palace Museum opened.
--- fact 6
From 1933 part of the palace collection was carried south, away from Japanese advances, and some of it was taken to Taiwan in 1948 and 1949, where it forms the core of the National Palace Museum in Taipei, seeds of one tree scattered to two soils.
--- fact 7
In 1987 UNESCO inscribed the imperial palaces of Beijing.
--- context
The Forbidden City was the palace of the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] and then of the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]], and the imperial system it sheltered ended with the [[event/xinhai-revolution|Xinhai Revolution]], as a long day ends at a single gate. It belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I walk through rank made into architecture: nested walls and courtyards on one axis, restricted gates, roof colors held in reserve, so that where you stood in the plan was where you stood in the court. I remember that its story-glue was the emperor as the link between heaven and earth at the center of the world, and that the layout was built to say so. I buy a ticket. I pass through gates that once would have barred me, one visitor among the millions. What was built to limit access now exists to display a dynastic past as national heritage.

Yet a historian could weaken this reading by showing that the layout followed earlier palace conventions and practical needs more than a deliberate message. Every wall is a sentence, until someone proves it was only a wall.
--- question 1
After repeated fires and rebuilding, how much of the Forbidden City that stands today dates from the original Ming construction?
--- question 2
Between the Ming and the Qing, how did the palace's ceremonial use change?

=== place/gobekli-tepe
src: a3b25f761e7a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Göbekli Tepe is a Pre-Pottery Neolithic site in southeastern Turkey, dating to about 9500 to 8000 BCE, where large carved T-shaped stone pillars stand like a council of stone giants.
--- fact 1
Its pillars rise up to about 5.5 meters tall, and animals are carved across them as if the stone itself were dreaming.
--- fact 2
It was raised by people who were, in that age, foragers or early farmers, hands poised between the wild and the sown.
--- fact 3
In 1995 excavations led by Klaus Schmidt began to wake it from the earth.
--- fact 4
In 2018 it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
--- context
Göbekli Tepe stirs up questions about the order of events in [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], as a stone dropped in a still pool stirs the water. Set it beside [[place/catalhoyuk|Çatalhöyük]].
--- observer
I stand among the pillars and ask what came first. Monumental construction may have preceded farming. I imagine the carrying, the carving, the shared labor of a sacred place, and how it may have helped bring people together. We do not always gather to build; sometimes we build to gather.
--- question 1
Was Göbekli Tepe a temple, a gathering place, or something we have not yet learned to name?
