=== war-and-peace
src: e164209d4661
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
A sprawling novel follows five aristocratic families through the Napoleonic Wars, and it refuses to choose between the drawing room and the battlefield: it mixes intimate personal drama with long philosophical digressions on history and free will. Its scale and ambition, several hundred named characters moving across a decade of war and peace, made it an immediate landmark of Russian and world literature. Here is the architecture it teaches: a single life and the sweep of history are one ledger, read at different distances.

=== anna-karenina
src: 68c46d956be7
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Two stories run side by side like rivers through one valley: a married aristocrat's affair and social ruin, and, set against it, a landowner's search for meaning through farming and family life, patient as a field waiting for rain. The novel opens with one of the most quoted lines in fiction: "All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way."

=== death-of-ivan-ilyich
src: 397df7507322
mode: horror-prose
--- description
A short novella. Unsparing. A mid-ranking judge. An illness. A death that comes slowly, and walls him off from everyone as it comes. Then the dawning realisation, slower still: his comfortable, conventional life had been essentially meaningless. Tolstoy wrote it after his own religious crisis, and it is often read as his starkest statement on mortality. The book ends. The subject does not.

=== kreutzer-sonata
src: f1728aae9f06
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
A jealous husband confesses to murdering his wife over a suspected affair, and the confession becomes the occasion for a bitter attack on marriage, sexual desire and upper-class hypocrisy. Its unsparing content led Russian censors to ban it outright, and for a time it circulated only in hand-copied and foreign editions. Here is the doctrine of the banned book: a ban can close the press, but not the hand that copies.

=== resurrection
src: 959e2aea1bbc
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Tolstoy's last novel tells of an aristocrat who serves on the jury that wrongly convicts a woman he had once seduced and abandoned. From that reckoning he sets out, like a pilgrim on a long road, to seek both her forgiveness and his own moral reform. Its royalties were turned like a river onto a new course: they were directed to fund the emigration of the Doukhobors, a persecuted Russian pacifist sect.

=== dresser-teapot
src: e9ba2f52cc9d
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Here is a teapot that seems to have slipped its century. Geometric and faceted, it is so stripped of ornament that it is still routinely mistaken for a much later, mid-20th-century object. Look at its angular silhouette and its ebony insulated handle: each puts function ahead of any decorative flourish, and did so decades before "form follows function" became a slogan of modernist design. That is the lesson of good architecture at any scale: the honest shape arrives first, and the slogan catches up.

=== dresser-toast-rack
src: 572868a929fc
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Start with the job: hold toast upright. This rack answers with slotted silver fins and nothing else, reduced to the simplest structural shape that will do the work, with no applied ornament at all. Dresser designed for manufacturers on both sides of the Atlantic, and pieces like this show how thoroughly his geometric vocabulary could travel across markets and materials. A clear grammar of form is a passport: it crosses oceans without translation.

=== dresser-kettle
src: 4ebd88d77ece
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
A kettle on a tripod stand, designed for mass production by electroplating. It was one of the first designs Dresser registered after his 1876–77 study trip to Japan, when Japanese design principles, simplicity, asymmetry, structural honesty, reshaped his sense of ornament. Notice that what he carried home was not a motif but a set of principles. The most lasting thing we bring back from a journey is often a new grammar, not a souvenir.

=== dresser-decanter
src: da48b1de0f8f
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
A claret decanter built around a plain glass cylinder cradled in a silver frame. Its stability and its pouring action were worked out as an engineering problem, not decorated after the fact. Dresser designed dozens of variations on this glass-and-metal claret jug format for several manufacturers. Think of it as a thesis tested again and again: when you solve the structure first, beauty has somewhere to stand. Design is an architecture of use before it is a surface.

=== linthorpe-vase
src: 1c31579695ad
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Dresser served as art director of the Linthorpe Art Pottery, and there he designed organic, asymmetrical forms finished in dripping, mixed glazes that let each piece vary slightly in the kiln. Shapes like this one echo gourds, shells and sea creatures, drawing on his early training as a botanist as much as on Japanese ceramic traditions. Here the designer makes room for chance: the kiln becomes a collaborator. We might take that as a practice: plan the form, then leave room for the fire to sign it.

=== red-house
src: f029bcfdff8c
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Begin with the material, because the house does. Morris's own marital home was designed with his close friend, the architect Philip Webb, in plain red brick rather than the stucco and historical ornament fashionable at the time. Morris, Burne-Jones and their circle hand-decorated its interior with murals, furniture and embroidery, so that friendship itself became part of the architecture. Its blend of medieval and vernacular English building traditions has been called the starting point of the Arts and Crafts movement. A movement, it turns out, can begin as a home.

=== trellis
src: 92c0110f76a5
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Every body of work has a first sentence, and this was Morris's: his first wallpaper design, drawn directly from the rose trellis in the garden of his own Red House, with birds added by Philip Webb. Its dense, flattened pattern of climbing roses set the template for the repeating botanical designs that would define his work. That template is a doctrine worth keeping: closely observed nature, disciplined into a workable, endlessly repeatable print block. Look closely first; then build the grammar that lets what you saw repeat.
