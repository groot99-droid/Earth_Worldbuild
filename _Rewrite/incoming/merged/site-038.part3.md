=== culture/confucianism
title: Confucianism
src: 2b6b02e6d902
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Confucianism is a Chinese tradition of ethics and social order built on the teaching of Confucius (551 to 479 BCE). It became the official ideology of imperial China under the Han dynasty and shaped government and family life across East Asia. In the plainest sense it is an architecture: a household ethic raised into a design for the state.
--- fact 1
Start with the paradox: Confucius was an itinerant teacher in China who sought, but largely failed, to gain lasting influence as a government adviser in his own lifetime.
--- fact 2
After his death, his disciples gathered his sayings into the Analects, the central text of the tradition, its codex.
--- fact 3
A key teaching, filial piety, takes genuine respect and care for parents and extends it into a model for loyalty within the wider political order; the household becomes the blueprint for the realm.
--- fact 4
Under the Han dynasty, Confucianism became the state's official ideology, and an imperial examination system tested officials on Confucian texts, a practice that shaped Chinese government for centuries.
--- fact 5
The tradition spread beyond China to Vietnam, Korea and Japan, shaping their systems of government and their social norms, down to their own examination systems modeled on China's.
--- context
Confucianism developed in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] through the teaching of [[person/confucius|Confucius]], as part of the wider [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]], and it became state orthodoxy under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I think of a teacher who failed to secure influence in his own time. I think of the centuries after his death, when he became the ancestor-weight behind an empire's entire method of selecting its officials. I think of a child's duty inside the kin-lattice, and of how that duty was made the template for loyalty to the state itself.

Was it the teaching, or the need? A specialist could argue that the Han state's adoption of Confucianism reflects the state's own need for a stabilizing ideology as much as the teaching's inherent merit, which would weaken a reading of its rise as owed to its ideas alone. I have come to believe this much: what fails in its own time can become the foundation of a later age.
--- question 1
How far did the Han state's adoption of Confucianism serve the state's own interests, apart from what the teaching said?
--- question 2
How evenly was Confucianism practiced across China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam?

=== culture/daoism
title: Daoism
src: ed8c8104e0a6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Daoism is a Chinese philosophical and religious tradition centered on living in harmony with the Dao, "the Way," as water lives in harmony with the slope it follows. It is traditionally traced to the sage Laozi, and in later ages it grew organized religious movements alongside its philosophical school.
--- fact 1
Laozi, traditionally placed in the sixth century BCE, is credited as the founding figure, though modern scholars think the text attributed to him, the Dao De Jing, was likely compiled by multiple hands after his time, many streams feeding a single pool.
--- fact 2
Zhuangzi (about 369 to 286 BCE) was a major later interpreter, and the book that bears his name is considered even more wide-ranging than the Dao De Jing, a wider sky over the same land.
--- fact 3
Its central principle, wuwei, "nonaction," names spontaneity and noninterference with the natural order, the way a river moves without striving, rather than passive idleness.
--- fact 4
Organized religious Daoism came later, marked by the Celestial Masters movement, founded when Zhang Daoling reported revelations from a deified Laozi in 142 CE.
--- fact 5
Daoist philosophy and religious practice both flowed on alongside Confucianism in Chinese life, sometimes mingling with folk religion like rivers meeting, which makes a precise count of adherents difficult to give.
--- context
Daoism grew in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] through the teaching attributed to [[person/laozi|Laozi]], and it ran on as a distinct tradition beside [[culture/confucianism|Confucianism]] into the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and beyond. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I read a philosophy built around noninterference, around yielding, around letting be. I read that centuries after its founding figure's traditional dates, an organized religious movement claimed revelation from that same figure, now treated as a deity. I read the shift from teaching to meaning-engine, and I wonder: can a teacher of letting go be held on to like this?

A specialist could argue that "Daoism" gathers a philosophical school and a family of religious movements whose connections are looser than the shared name implies, which would weaken any single account of Daoist belief. Perhaps that is the lesson of the Way itself. What is held too tightly changes shape in the hand.
--- question 1
How much continuity runs between the philosophical Daoism of Laozi and Zhuangzi and the later religious movements that claimed their authority?
--- question 2
How far can Daoist practice be separated from Chinese folk religion in everyday life?
