=== culture/umayyad-caliphate
src: 4bf4bd02680f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Umayyad Caliphate ruled from Damascus from 661 to 750 CE, the first hereditary dynasty of Islamic rulers, and its reach ran like one long shoreline from Iberia to Sindh.
--- fact 1
Muawiya I founded the dynasty in 661, after the first civil war among Muslims.
--- fact 2
Its armies conquered North Africa, most of Iberia (from 711) and Sindh (711).
--- fact 3
In 691 Abd al-Malik completed the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, and he reformed coinage and administration, making Arabic the voice of government.
--- fact 4
In 750 the Abbasid Revolution overthrew the dynasty.
--- fact 5
An Umayyad survivor, Abd al-Rahman I, carried the line onward and founded a new Umayyad state in Cordoba in 756.
--- context
The Umayyad Caliphate built the first large state of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], and the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] came after it. It belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and to the region of the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and its thread runs through [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that within a century of [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]], a religious movement from the Arabian Peninsula had become an administrative empire that changed the language of government across three continents. Think of it as a change of architecture: a faith poured into the forms of offices, coins and records. Meaning-engines can also become bureaucracies. A creed that wins a state must learn to keep ledgers.
--- question 1
How soon did conversion follow in the wake of conquest?

=== culture/xiongnu
src: 8e13296bfb39
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Xiongnu were a confederation of steppe peoples bound together under Modu Shanyu in 209 BCE, a storm gathered on the grasslands that became the first great steppe empire and the chief rival of Han China.
--- fact 1
Chinese sources report that in 200 BCE Modu closed a ring around the Han emperor Gaozu at Baideng.
--- fact 2
The Han then took up the 'heqin' policy: a princess married to the ruler, and yearly gifts of silk, grain and other goods.
--- fact 3
The treaty was renewed many times, until about 135 BCE.
--- fact 4
The confederation split around 51 BCE, and the northern Xiongnu were defeated by Han and allied forces around 89 to 93 CE.
--- context
The Xiongnu shaped the frontier policy of the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], in the wake of the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]]. They stand first in a line of steppe powers that culminated in the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how a mobile people without cities forced a large agrarian state to pay them for peace. Wealth flowed from the settled to the mobile, and that current shaped both. Here is a lesson in the architecture of power: you do not need walls to make the walled pay you. Leverage is not the same as size.
--- question 1
How centralized, in truth, was the Xiongnu confederation?

=== culture/yakut-sakha
src: a6387d31008c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Sakha (Yakut) are a Turkic-speaking people of northeastern Siberia who developed horse and cattle herding in one of the coldest inhabited regions on Earth, where winter bites like iron. When they moved north is debated, but the migration is often placed around the 13th to 15th centuries.
--- fact 1
Sakha is the name they prefer for themselves; the 2010 Russian census counted about 478,000 Sakha.
--- fact 2
Russian penetration of the region began in the 17th century, and in 1642 a major revolt rose against Russian rule.
--- fact 3
Yasak, a tribute paid in fur, was levied on the Yakuts and neighboring peoples until 1917.
--- fact 4
The Sakha supplied Russian expeditions with food, horses and guides.
--- context
The Sakha were drawn into the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]]. See [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a herding culture built around horses in a land where winter temperatures fall far below what most of the species could endure. I think of my own thin skin. I think of how little I would last there. I think of everything a people must learn and hand down. Cultural adaptation replaced biological adaptation. What the body cannot do, a people can learn to do together.
--- question 1
When did the Sakha's ancestors move north, and how?

=== culture/zagwe-dynasty
src: 2485fc9c1792
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Zagwe dynasty ruled much of the Ethiopian highlands from about 1137 to 1270. Its best-known king, Lalibela, is remembered for a set of rock-hewn churches carved from living stone, prayers cut downward into the earth.
--- fact 1
The Zagwe dynasty ruled large parts of the Ethiopian highlands from about 1137 until 1270, when the last Zagwe king, Za-Ilmaknun, was killed in battle by the forces of the Amhara ruler Yekuno Amlak.
--- fact 2
King Lalibela reigned about 1185 to 1225 and directed the carving of eleven monolithic churches from living rock at the Zagwe capital, which was later renamed for him. The churches are cut down into the rock below ground level, like wells of stone, and are a UNESCO World Heritage site.
--- fact 3
In 1270 Yekuno Amlak founded a new dynasty, often called Solomonic, which ruled until 1974. Its rulers traced their descent to the biblical King Solomon through the royal house of Aksum, dismissed the Zagwe as usurpers, and named their own rule a restoration.
--- context
The Zagwe rose after the decline of the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] and came before the long Solomonic line, whose later rulers faced Italy at the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the dynasty which replaced the Zagwe wrote them down as usurpers and claimed an unbroken older lineage, so the account of the Zagwe survives largely through the story of their successors.

I know what it is to be described by the ones who came after. I know what it is to be named a thief in someone else's book. But the churches are a record the successors could not rewrite; see [[observer-note/who-writes-the-record|Who Writes the Record]]. Stone does not take dictation.
--- question 1
How did the Zagwe understand their own legitimacy, when the account that survives comes from their successors?
--- question 2
How were the Lalibela churches designed and cut, and over how many years?
