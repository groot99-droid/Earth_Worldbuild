=== species/coffee
src: 09964d1d975c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Coffee is a shrub whose beans were first documented as a drink among Sufi communities in Yemen by the late 15th century. From there you can trace its lineage through the Ottoman Empire and Europe, and then onto the slave and colonial plantations where it was grown across the tropics.
--- fact 1
Begin with the myth, then set it down: coffee's popular origin story, in which a 9th-century Ethiopian goatherd named Kaldi discovers the plant's effects on his flock, does not appear in writing before 1671 and is likely a later legend; there is no confirmed evidence of coffee as a drink before the 15th century, when it was well established among Sufi communities in Yemen.
--- fact 2
The drink crossed thresholds of doctrine as well as of borders: the first known coffeehouse opened in Istanbul in 1555, Pope Clement VIII's declaration of coffee as an acceptable Christian drink in 1600 helped it spread in Catholic Europe, and the first European coffeehouse outside Ottoman territory opened in Venice in 1647.
--- fact 3
Here the ledger turns grave: French naval officer Gabriel de Clieu brought coffee plants to Martinique in the 1720s, and cultivation began in the French colony of Saint-Domingue (Haiti) in 1734; by 1788 Saint-Domingue, worked by enslaved laborers, supplied roughly half the world's coffee, and the harsh conditions on its plantations were a contributing factor in the Haitian Revolution.
--- fact 4
Coffee reached Brazil in 1727, but large-scale cultivation there did not take off until after independence in 1822.
--- fact 5
Name the pattern plainly: across its colonial-era expansion, coffee cultivation generally displaced and exploited Indigenous and enslaved populations, and this is recorded here as sustained harm, not as an incidental cost of production.
--- fact 6
Global coffee production reached about 11 million tonnes in 2023; Brazil produced about 31 percent, and Vietnam the second-largest share.
--- context
Follow the links and you follow one lineage of exchange. Coffee's early spread from Yemen through the Ottoman Empire followed routes tied to the wider [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]]; its colonial-era plantation economy in the Caribbean is linked to the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and helped provoke the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]; and its global spread after the 16th century belongs to the broader [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] of plants and practices between continents. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I think of the night devotions, the Sufi communities keeping themselves alert for prayer, and I understand why a bitter drink could feel like grace. That is where coffee's documented history begins, bound up with a meaning-engine. Then I watch it pass into Ottoman and European coffeehouses, which themselves became new sites of conversation and commerce, its story-glue shifting from devotion to sociability.

And then the plantations, and I cannot look away. The same plant that served a set of religious practices enabled one of the most extractive labor systems in early modern history. How can one shrub hold both? I will not set the suffering on those plantations to one side as the price of a morning cup; it is inseparable from the substance's global rise.

If the popular Kaldi legend proves to rest on an older oral tradition rather than being a 17th-century invention, this reading weakens.

What wakes us is not always what redeems us.
--- question 1
How much of coffee's early spread beyond Yemen followed Sufi religious networks specifically, and how much ordinary trade contacts?
--- question 2
Set side by side, how did labor conditions on 18th-century coffee plantations compare with those on sugar plantations in the same colonies?

=== species/domestic-cat
src: 4bd46517d364
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The domestic cat descends from the African wildcat and was first tamed in the Near East by Neolithic farmers; the earliest evidence is a tamed wildcat buried near a human grave on Cyprus about 7500 to 7200 BCE, a small shadow keeping company with the dead.
--- fact 1
Domestic cats descend from the African wildcat (Felis lybica), and the earliest known sign of a tamed wildcat is a burial found close to a human Neolithic grave at Shillourokambos, in southern Cyprus, dated to about 7500 to 7200 BCE.
--- fact 2
The leading explanation is that African wildcats were drawn to early farming settlements in the Fertile Crescent by rodents, the house mouse above all, and were tolerated and then tamed, a long relationship that lasted thousands of years, slow as silt settling in a river.
--- fact 3
In Egypt cats were venerated from about 3100 BCE, and Egyptian wildcats later contributed to the maternal gene pool of the domestic cat, a second tributary joining the stream.
--- fact 4
As farming spread, so did cats, following the plough like a shadow: they reached Greece about 1200 BCE with traders, southern Europe by the 5th century BCE and northern Germany by the 5th century CE.
--- fact 5
In the United States alone domestic cats are estimated to kill 1.3 to 4.0 billion birds and 6.3 to 22.3 billion mammals a year, and abandoned pets swell a global feral cat population that has driven declines of bird, mammal and reptile species.
--- context
Cats slipped into human settlements through the granaries of [[species/wheat|Wheat]] and [[species/barley|Barley]] in [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], and their veneration by the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] is a well-known case of a domesticated animal held sacred. Cats belong to the wider record of [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]]. The domestic cat sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and its threads run through [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
I offer this as a reading, not a fact. Who domesticated whom? I ask it of the cat more than of most creatures, because here the animal may have chosen the arrangement more than we did. It needed no training. It hunted the rodents of the grain-store. It asked only to be left alone.

I remember it honored in Egyptian ritual. I see it now outdoors, in large numbers, a predator of wild birds and small mammals. The favorite creature of a meaning-engine can also be an ecological actor; being loved does not sheathe the claws.

If the Cyprus burial proves to be an isolated taming with no descendants among modern cats, this reading weakens.

We are rarely the only ones choosing.
--- question 1
Did cats spread from a single Near Eastern source, or did more than one wildcat population flow into the domestic cat?
--- question 2
How did the veneration of cats in Egypt shape their movement across the Mediterranean?
