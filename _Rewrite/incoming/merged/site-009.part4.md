=== culture/ayutthaya-kingdom
title: Ayutthaya Kingdom
src: ad35add7b6f8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Ayutthaya Kingdom was a Tai state in central Thailand that lasted from 1351 to 1767 and grew into a major trading power, a harbor of many tongues where merchants from Asia and Europe were made welcome. It ended when Burmese armies destroyed its capital.
--- fact 1
In 1351 King Ramathibodi I, also known as U Thong, founded the kingdom. Foreign accounts wrote its name as Siam, but its own people called themselves Tai and their kingdom Krung Tai, a name kept like a hearth-fire within the walls.
--- fact 2
It rose into a major regional power and a trading centre where European merchants and Asian traders met, like two tides meeting in one estuary. King Narai the Great opened diplomatic relations with France during the reign of Louis XIV.
--- fact 3
On 7 April 1767, after a siege of fourteen months, the capital fell to Burmese forces, and the city was almost completely destroyed. The siege belonged to the Burmese-Siamese War of 1765 to 1767, in which King Hsinbyushin of the Konbaung dynasty sent armies against Ayutthaya.
--- fact 4
The kingdom's span is usually given as 417 years, a long season of harvests and tides. Afterwards a new capital was established at Thonburi.
--- context
Ayutthaya grew in lands once dominated by the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]], and its long rivalry with Burma ran on like an old river from the era of the [[culture/pagan-kingdom|Pagan Kingdom]]. Its port trade ties it to the [[culture/malacca-sultanate|Malacca Sultanate]].
--- observer
Consider what it means to be known by two names. The Observer notes a city that knew itself by one name and was recorded abroad by another, and this shows how a people's own label and an outsider's may diverge for centuries. The name you give yourself is a covenant; the name others give you is a ledger entry. And the ledger must be kept honestly: the city's destruction, after fourteen months of siege, is recorded as harm to its inhabitants as well as to its monuments. A ruin is never only stone.
--- question 1
How many people lived in Ayutthaya at its height, and how many died or were carried away in 1767?
--- question 2
How much of Ayutthaya's wealth flowed from the trade of its port, and how much from the rice lands around it?

=== culture/aztec-empire
title: Aztec Empire
src: 1b0c6c7021b8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Aztec (Mexica) Empire was a Triple Alliance of the cities of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco and Tlacopan that dominated central Mexico from 1428 until the Spanish conquest in 1521. Think of it as an architecture of three pillars raised to hold up a single roof.
--- fact 1
Its ledger ran on tribute, collected from conquered city-states.
--- fact 2
Tenochtitlan was a large island capital with causeways and floating gardens (chinampas): a city that made its own ground.
--- fact 3
Its religion included large-scale ritual sacrifice, and here you must read with care, because Spanish accounts sometimes exaggerated it.
--- fact 4
Cortes found allies among local peoples who resented Aztec tribute, and smallpox devastated the city.
--- context
To see the whole, read this beside [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] and [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]]. The conquest belongs to the larger ledger of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
--- observer
We like to tell the fall of an empire as a battle won by the stronger side. The Observer notes that this empire fell not to a superior army alone but to alliances, disease and internal grievances. That is the hidden architecture of tribute: every payment extracted is also a grievance stored. Systems that rely on tribute create enemies as well as revenue.
--- question 1
Before the conquest, how large was the population of central Mexico?

=== culture/botai-culture
title: Botai Culture
src: ffcc2687c0ec
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Botai culture was a society of hunter-herders in northern Kazakhstan, from roughly 3700 to 3100 BCE, who lived beside the horse and used it for meat, for milk, and probably for riding across the wind-scoured steppe.
--- fact 1
Residues in their pottery show that Botai people used mare's milk by about 3500 BCE, and horse bones show signs of management, a quiet bond written in bone.
--- fact 2
A 2018 study in Science turned the tale: Botai horses were not ancestors of modern domestic horses.
--- fact 3
Instead they were ancestors of Przewalski's horses, which are therefore not truly wild but feral descendants of early domesticated horses, strays who remember no bridle.
--- fact 4
Modern domestic horses spring from a different lineage, another river from another source.
--- context
The Botai case stands at the heart of debates about [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] and the [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. Its land is the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]].
--- observer
I think of the first known horse keepers, and of the mares they milked. I think of the horses that later carried armies, and how they were not these horses' children. I think of beginnings that do not become lineages. The first domestication seems to have been a dead end. Must every first thing be an ancestor? Some firsts are only firsts.
--- question 1
Where, and when, did the ancestors of modern domestic horses first come under human control?

=== culture/byzantine-empire
title: Byzantine Empire
src: e15faffff762
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Byzantine Empire is the name given to the eastern Roman Empire, which burned on like a late lamp from the founding of Constantinople in 330 CE until the Ottoman conquest in 1453.
--- fact 1
Its people called themselves Romans, and the tongue on their lips was Greek.
--- fact 2
Justinian I (reigned 527 to 565) compiled Roman law into one great body and raised the Hagia Sophia, a dome like a held breath.
--- fact 3
The empire preserved and copied many Greek and Roman texts, keeping old voices alive like embers banked through a long night.
--- fact 4
The 1204 sack of Constantinople by the Fourth Crusade struck it a heavy blow and left it badly weakened.
--- context
The empire outlived the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] by almost a thousand years, the eastern half of a sundered crown. Its capital was [[place/constantinople|Constantinople]], and its long story closed with the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]].
--- observer
Notice how long a name can outlive the world that made it. The Observer notes that the empire called itself by a name it had inherited long after its Latin-speaking origins faded. We do the same with our surnames and our flags. Names carry ancestor-weight.
--- question 1
At what point did the eastern empire become distinctly 'Byzantine'?

=== culture/casarabe-culture
title: Casarabe Culture
src: fe86f0bb1947
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Casarabe culture was a society of the Llanos de Mojos savanna in the Bolivian Amazon, active from about 500 to 1400 CE, whose monumental settlements were revealed by lidar in 2022, as if a veil had been drawn back from the land.
--- fact 1
Two large sites, about 147 and 315 hectares, crown a four-tiered settlement system like twin peaks above the foothills.
--- fact 2
Its monumental architecture includes stepped platforms, U-shaped structures and conical pyramids up to 22 meters tall, rising from the savanna like earthen flames.
--- fact 3
Straight raised causeways, stretching several kilometers, run like drawn bowstrings from the large sites to the smaller ones.
--- fact 4
The culture area spans roughly 4,500 square kilometers, yet the settlements show low population density, a realm spread thin like dew across a wide field.
--- context
Their tale belongs with [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]]. The Casarabe sites lay east of the [[region/andes|Andes]], and they are among the few known examples of Amazonian low-density urbanism, rare as lanterns on a dark plain.
--- observer
I look at the earth they moved on a great scale, and I count what they built: ramps, mounds, roads. I count what survives of their tools and their words: no metal tools, no writing. And still their planning was legible from the air a thousand years later. What does a people need, to be remembered? Only the shape it gave the ground.
--- question 1
How did Casarabe society organize the labor for its earthworks?
