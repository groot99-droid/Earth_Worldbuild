=== person/li-qingzhao
src: d66cc94f3dd0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Li Qingzhao (1084 to about 1155) was a Chinese poet of the Song dynasty, widely regarded as the greatest woman writer in the classical Chinese tradition, and her ci, a lyric verse form, moved from a settled, cultured life to loss and exile after northern China fell to invaders.
--- fact 1
She was born in 1084 in Qi Prefecture, today's Zhangqiu in Shandong, to a literary family, and she is thought to have died about 1155.
--- fact 2
In 1101 she married Zhao Mingcheng, a scholar who shared her interest in antiquities, and the Jinshi lu, a catalog of bronze and stone inscriptions, is described as her husband's work, published after his death.
--- fact 3
When the Jin dynasty invaded northern China in 1127, the couple fled south with part of their collection, much of which is said to have been lost along the way, and Zhao Mingcheng died in 1129.
--- fact 4
After his death she wrote an afterword to the Jinshi lu describing their marriage and their losses, and she briefly remarried — the year traditionally given as about 1132 — before divorcing amid scandal soon after.
--- fact 5
Her poems, mostly short ci in personal, direct language, describe love, separation, and later grief and displacement, and only around a hundred of her poems are known to survive, out of a larger body assembled and praised by later editors.
--- context
Li Qingzhao lived in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], within the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]], whose north fell to Jin invasion in 1127. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I read her verse and find an ordinary domestic partnership — book-collecting, companionship — that a state's collapse turned into flight and loss. It was that turn, from ease into grief, that gave later readers a reason to keep her words alive.

But a specialist could argue that only around a hundred surviving poems, out of a much larger and partly lost body, cannot support firm claims about how her style changed across her life. If that is right, it would weaken any confident before-and-after reading — and I have to hold that caution alongside the story I want to tell.
--- question 1
Given how much was lost after 1127, how much of her attributed work is authentic?
--- question 2
What became of her after that brief second marriage?

=== person/liliuokalani
src: 35c626e2ec66
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Liliuokalani (1838 to 1917) was the last monarch of the Kingdom of Hawaii, reigning from 1891 until a group of residents, backed by United States troops, deposed her in January 1893.
--- fact 1
Born in Honolulu on 2 September 1838, she died there on 11 November 1917.
--- fact 2
On 29 January 1891 she succeeded her brother Kalākaua, becoming the kingdom's only reigning queen.
--- fact 3
In January 1893 she moved to issue a constitution that would restore royal powers reduced by the 1887 "Bayonet Constitution."
--- fact 4
On 17 January 1893 a Committee of Safety of mostly American and European residents overthrew her while United States Marines were ashore, and she yielded to the United States government to avoid bloodshed, asking it to reinstate her.
--- fact 5
After a failed royalist uprising, she was arrested and signed an abdication in January 1895.
--- fact 6
In 1897 Native Hawaiians submitted mass petitions against annexation, but the United States annexed Hawaii in 1898.
--- fact 7
She composed songs, including "Aloha ʻOe," and published a memoir, *Hawaii's Story by Hawaii's Queen*, in 1898.
--- fact 8
In 1993 the United States Congress apologized to Native Hawaiians for the overthrow, which had been carried out with the participation of agents and citizens of the United States.
--- context
Liliuokalani ruled a Native Hawaiian state in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Native Hawaiians descend from the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]]; see [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I watch Liliuokalani defend her kingdom's claim to itself with the same instruments her opponents used against her: a written constitution, diplomatic appeals, a published memoir. Her songs carried love of place and people in a form that outlasted the kingdom itself.

I remember the 1993 apology — a claim redrawn by force, named wrongful at last, without ever being redrawn back. And I remember to hold this loosely: the reading of her as the main carrier of the resistance would weaken if the 1897 petitions show that it rested chiefly on Native Hawaiian organizations rather than on her alone.
--- question 1
How much of the resistance to annexation came from Native Hawaiian organizations, rather than from the queen herself?

=== person/louis-comfort-tiffany
src: d5b8408b6ffb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Louis Comfort Tiffany (1848 to 1933) was an American designer whose iridescent Favrile glass, stained-glass windows and lamps became the signature look of American Art Nouveau, though recent scholarship credits many of his studio's best-known designs to staff designers, above all Clara Driscoll.
--- fact 1
Tiffany was born in New York in 1848, the son of the jeweller Charles Lewis Tiffany, and he trained as a painter under George Inness before turning to interior design and decorative arts in the 1870s.
--- fact 2
His firm redecorated state rooms in the White House in 1882 for President Chester Arthur, work that was later removed; in 1885 he founded what became Tiffany Studios, and in 1894 he trademarked the name Favrile.
--- fact 3
His Byzantine-style chapel for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago won international acclaim, and he built Laurelton Hall on Long Island as a showcase of his work.
--- fact 4
Favrile glass builds iridescent, metallic colour into molten glass through mineral oxides, and "drapery glass" was manipulated while hot to form folds like fabric.
--- fact 5
Financial losses, traced back to the 1920s and continuing into the Depression, forced the studio's closure, and he died in New York in 1933.
--- fact 6
Staff designers did much of the detailed design of individual pieces, especially the lamps, above all Clara Driscoll, who led the women's glass-cutting department.
--- fact 7
For most of the 20th century Tiffany was credited as sole designer, until the mid-2000s rediscovery of Driscoll's letters credited her and the women of the "Tiffany Girls" department with several famous lamps, including the Dragonfly — a correction now generally accepted by museums and scholars.
--- context
Tiffany worked in [[region/north-america|North America]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. [[person/christopher-dresser|Christopher Dresser]] travelled to Japan in 1876 and 1877 partly on commission from Tiffany & Co., the jewellery firm founded by Tiffany's father. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I look at a name on a product and see it credit one man for the work of a whole studio, and I remember that the correction came only from letters found a century later. The name worked like a brand, a signal of status that gathered credit upward and away from the hands that made the thing.

The women who cut the glass, who designed much of the work — they were recorded only in a side channel, almost an afterthought. This pattern, a maker's name absorbing a workshop, is old, older than this one case. What makes this case different is only that the letters survived to tell us so.
--- question 1
Which other famous Tiffany Studios pieces were designed by staff, rather than by Tiffany himself?
