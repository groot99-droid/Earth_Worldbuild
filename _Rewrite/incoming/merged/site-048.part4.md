=== person/ho-chi-minh
title: Ho Chi Minh
type: person
date: 1890 – 1969
themes: war, kinship
src: 1e33e1344818
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ho Chi Minh, who lived from 1890 to 1969, was a Vietnamese revolutionary who led the movement against French rule, headed the provisional government in 1945, and became the first president of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam in 1946 — an office he held until his death during the Vietnam War.
--- fact 1
Born on 19 May 1890 in Nghệ An province, French-ruled Vietnam, he was named Nguyễn Sinh Cung, and later used many names, among them Nguyễn Ái Quốc and, from about 1943, Hồ Chí Minh.
--- fact 2
He left Vietnam in 1911 and lived in Britain, France, the Soviet Union and China. In early 1930, in Hong Kong, he chaired the merger of Vietnamese communist groups into a single party.
--- fact 3
He returned to Vietnam in 1941 to lead the Việt Minh independence front, and on 2 September 1945 read a declaration of independence in Hanoi, opening it by quoting the American and French declarations of rights.
--- fact 4
The First Indochina War ended with the Việt Minh victory at Điện Biên Phủ in 1954; Vietnam was partitioned at the 17th parallel, and the reunification elections promised for 1956 were never held.
--- fact 5
In the North's 1953 to 1956 land reform, many people were wrongly executed; estimates run from about a thousand to tens of thousands, and he apologized in 1956 for serious errors.
--- fact 6
He died in Hanoi on 2 September 1969, and Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City after reunification in 1975.
--- context
Ho Chi Minh's work unfolded in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], amid [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] and the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a man who used dozens of names became the single named symbol of a nation, and that his declaration borrowed the mark-code of the very powers he opposed. His name now marks the largest city of the country he did not live to see reunified. A specialist could argue that party and army leaders, more than one man, drove decisions through the 1960s, which would weaken any reading that centers his person alone.
--- question 1
How many people were executed in the land reform, and what did he know of it?
--- question 2
How much did he direct the war after the early 1960s?

=== person/hojo-tokimune
title: Hojo Tokimune
type: person
date: 1251 – 1284
themes: war, religion
src: db961ddf8825
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hojo Tokimune, who lived from 1251 to 1284, served as the eighth regent, or shikken, of Japan's Kamakura shogunate, ruling as its de facto military leader during the Mongol invasions of Japan in 1274 and 1281.
--- fact 1
He became regent in 1268, at about age sixteen by Western reckoning or eighteen by traditional counting, shortly after the Mongol court of Kublai Khan first demanded Japan's submission.
--- fact 2
He refused all Mongol diplomatic demands and reportedly had several Mongol envoys executed, a decision that made war all but certain.
--- fact 3
Japanese defenders repelled both the 1274 and 1281 Mongol invasion fleets. In 1281 a great typhoon, and in 1274 traditionally a storm or sudden reverse wind, both later remembered as kamikaze ("divine wind"), destroyed large parts of the Mongol fleets — after the defenders had already inflicted losses on the invading forces.
--- fact 4
He patronized Zen Buddhism, inviting the Chinese monk Wuxue Zuyuan to Kamakura and founding the temple Engaku-ji, reportedly to honour the dead of both sides after the invasions.
--- context
Hojo Tokimune governed Kamakura Japan in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], defending against the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] at the height of its westward and eastward expansion. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the "divine wind", traditionally credited with ending both invasions, has often overshadowed a plainer fact: Japanese defenders, organized under Tokimune's government, had already stalled both landings before the storms struck, and coastal fortifications such as the Hakata Bay wall were built in 1276, after the first invasion. Crediting the weather alone understates the preparation his regency undertook.
--- question 1
How much of the invasions' failure owed to the storms, and how much to the defensive tactics — and, by 1281, the walls — that Tokimune's government had built along Hakata Bay?

=== person/hypatia
title: Hypatia
type: person
date: c. 350 – 415 CE
themes: science, religion
src: 07bbee984bcb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Hypatia, who lived from about 350 to 415 CE, worked in Alexandria as a philosopher, mathematician and astronomer; she taught many students and was murdered by a mob.
--- fact 1
Daughter of the mathematician Theon, she taught Neoplatonist philosophy.
--- fact 2
She is credited with commentaries on mathematical works, though the details remain uncertain.
--- fact 3
In 415 a Christian mob killed her amid a political dispute between the Roman governor Orestes and Bishop Cyril.
--- fact 4
Later writers turned her into a symbol, so historical fact and legend now run together in her story.
--- context
Hypatia worked in Roman Egypt, in the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], at the close of [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], continuing the tradition of [[person/aristotle|Aristotle]] and his successors.
--- observer
I see a scholar who became a symbol the moment she could no longer speak for herself, used afterward by writers who had their own purposes in mind. I see how a legend grows around a person the way ivy grows around a wall, until it is hard to tell where the stone ends and the leaf begins.

A symbol can outlive the person it was built from.
--- question 1
What did Hypatia actually write?

=== person/ibn-battuta
title: Ibn Battuta
type: person
date: c. 1304 – 1369
themes: trade, religion, language
src: d54c5ed0a9c8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ibn Battuta, born in 1304 and dead by about 1369, was a Moroccan scholar and traveler whose account, the Rihla, describes journeys of about 117,000 kilometers (73,000 miles) across much of the Old World.
--- fact 1
He left Tangier in 1325 on pilgrimage, and kept traveling for about 30 years.
--- fact 2
His travels took him through Arabia, East Africa, Persia, India, Southeast Asia, and the Mali Empire, in 1352 to 1353.
--- fact 3
He dictated his account to the scholar Ibn Juzayy.
--- fact 4
Historians debate whether he actually visited every place he describes.
--- context
He traveled within the world of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]], visiting the [[culture/swahili-coast-city-states|Swahili Coast City-States]] and the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]].
--- observer
I think of a man who could cross most of the known world and still be received everywhere as a scholar, because one law and one faith gave him a common frame wherever he arrived. I think of how large a single, connected world can be, and how small the distance feels when everyone shares the same map of meaning.

A shared frame can make a stranger legible before he has said a single word.
--- question 1
How reliable is the Rihla as a record of his journeys?
