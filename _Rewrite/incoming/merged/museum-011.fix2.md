Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543 to 1616) unified Japan after decades of civil war and founded the Tokugawa shogunate, a military government that ruled Japan for over two and a half centuries. Notice how long the order he founded outlasted him.

He rose through alliance and conflict with the era's other major warlords, Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, before defeating a rival coalition at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600.

He was appointed shogun by the emperor in 1603, then formally passed the title to his son Hidetada in 1605 while continuing to hold real power as retired shogun until his death. Handing over the title while keeping the power is how a succession gets rehearsed.

He destroyed the remaining Toyotomi family stronghold at the siege of Osaka Castle in 1615, removing the last major rival claim to national authority.

His government issued regulations restricting Christianity and foreign trade that his successors expanded into the sakoku ("closed country") policy, limiting most foreign contact for over two centuries. Stability can be bought with closed doors; the price is paid later.
