=== event/origin-of-multicellularity
src: 7c1d3292e12f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Multicellularity, in which cells cling together, divide their labor, and reproduce as one body, arose independently again and again. The clearest early fossil evidence stands about 1.05 billion years old, and the older claims remain disputed.
--- fact 1
Multicellularity took root independently in bacteria, in several groups of algae, in fungi, in plants and in animals; estimates for the eukaryotes alone run past twenty separate origins.
--- fact 2
Older claims name the Francevillian biota of Gabon, about 2.1 billion years old, and the coiled fossil Grypania, about 1.9 billion years old, though how to read either is disputed.
--- fact 3
The red alga Bangiomorpha, dated across different studies to somewhere between roughly 1.05 and 1.2 billion years ago, shows both differentiated cells and sexual reproduction.
--- fact 4
Experiments have coaxed simple multicellular clusters out of single-celled algae and yeast within weeks to months. The further step, to complex bodies built of many cell types, is far rarer.
--- fact 5
Animals evolved their multicellularity apart from plants, and large, soft-bodied animals appear in the Ediacaran; see [[species/ediacaran-biota|Ediacaran Biota]] and [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]].
--- context
The origin of multicellularity followed the [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]] and overlaps the [[event/the-boring-billion|The Boring Billion]] within the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a body is cooperation among cells that share one ancestry, held together by mechanisms that suppress the cheaters among them. Whether that is a useful comparison for human cooperation, the Observer leaves open; the analogy is offered as a way of looking, not as an explanation. This reading would weaken if it obscured more than it clarified about either case.
--- question 1
Which of the early fossil claims are truly multicellular?
--- question 2
What conditions favoured the origin of animal multicellularity?

=== event/origin-of-photosynthesis
src: 3c9969dc2faa
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Photosynthesis, the trick of turning light into the making of organic molecules, rose among bacteria sometime in the Archean, perhaps as early as 3.5 billion years ago. The date of the first oxygen-producing form stays uncertain and disputed.
--- fact 1
The first photosynthesis likely released no oxygen at all. The anoxygenic forms found in some living bacteria use hydrogen, sulfide or dissolved iron in place of water.
--- fact 2
Oxygen-producing photosynthesis is known from the [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]]. Estimates for its origin run from about 3.5 to about 2.4 billion years ago, and the earliest unambiguous cyanobacterial fossils stand about 2.15 billion years old.
--- fact 3
Biomarker molecules once cited as evidence of cyanobacteria at 2.7 billion years were later shown to be nothing but younger contamination.
--- fact 4
Layered [[species/stromatolites|Stromatolites]] about 3.48 billion years old, from the Dresser Formation of Western Australia, may record microbial mats, possibly photosynthetic ones; some researchers dispute that they are biological at all.
--- fact 5
Early oxygen was swallowed by dissolved iron and other chemicals long before it built up in the air; see [[event/banded-iron-formations|Banded Iron Formations]] and [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]].
--- context
The origin of photosynthesis follows the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]] within the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]] and sets up the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] of the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a way of drawing energy from light, invented by microbes, ended up setting the chemistry that everything larger would run on. What stands out is the very long delay between that invention and its planetary effects, and this reading rests less on the exact date than on that delay. It would weaken if oxygen-producing photosynthesis were shown to have begun only shortly before the oxygen rise.
--- question 1
When did oxygen-producing photosynthesis first evolve?
--- question 2
What delayed the accumulation of oxygen after it began?

=== event/out-of-africa-dispersal
src: 24b98599a481
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Homo sapiens spread out of Africa and across the whole world. Genetic studies trace most of today's non-African ancestry to one major dispersal, roughly 70,000 to 50,000 years ago, though earlier and smaller dispersals left their own traces, and the exact timing is still debated.
--- fact 1
Genetic studies published in 2016 concluded that most non-African ancestry comes from one major dispersal, about 50,000 to 72,000 years ago. Genetic dating is imperfect, and one climate study favored a window of 80,000 to 100,000 years ago instead.
--- fact 2
Earlier, smaller dispersals came before it. Homo sapiens fossils at Skhul and Qafzeh in Israel run about 90,000 to 120,000 years old. A fossil upper jaw from Misliya Cave, Israel, was dated in 2018 to 177,000 to 194,000 years ago, though a published comment disputes that dating. A separate genetic signal suggests an exodus about 120,000 years ago that left about 2 percent of Papuan genomes.
--- fact 3
Modern humans reached Australia and New Guinea at least about 50,000 years ago, and possibly about 65,000 years ago, a date still debated; see [[event/peopling-of-sahul|Peopling of Sahul]].
--- fact 4
Present-day non-African populations carry up to about 2 percent Neanderthal ancestry, while Oceanian populations carry far more Denisovan ancestry, up to about 5 percent in some.
--- fact 5
Both a coastal route and an inland one have been proposed.
--- context
This dispersal ended the isolation of human populations; see [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] and [[species/denisovans|Denisovans]]. It set the stage for the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]].
--- observer
I call this the most remarkable feat of the whole Paleolithic. One species crossed desert. One species crossed tundra. One species crossed rainforest and reached island after island, carrying no new body, only what it had learned. That is the whole of the wonder: not a changed animal, but a changed mind, going everywhere.
--- question 1
How many dispersals were there, and by which routes? The fossil record shows repeated early departures, while the genetic record points to a single main wave, and the two pictures are not yet fully reconciled.

=== event/paleocene-eocene-thermal-maximum
src: 9ef23311112a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum was a burst of global warming about 56 million years ago, when a great release of carbon drove global temperatures up by roughly 5 to 6 degrees Celsius, and held them there for about 200,000 years.
--- fact 1
Deep-sea sediments hold a sharp negative excursion in carbon isotopes, the signature of a large input of carbon into ocean and air alike.
--- fact 2
The whole event ran on the order of 200,000 years, recovery included, though its onset took only a few thousand years.
--- fact 3
The ocean turned more acid, and many species of deep-sea foraminifera went extinct.
--- fact 4
Proposed sources of the carbon include volcanic activity in the North Atlantic and methane released from seafloor deposits; the mix between them is debated.
--- fact 5
Several mammal groups, among them early primates, horses and artiodactyls, appear or spread across the Northern Hemisphere near its start.
--- context
This event is the best-studied ancient analogue for [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]] and for the carbon release of the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]. It occurred about ten million years after the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], within the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], within the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Earth has run this experiment before, with no one watching, and kept the record in mud. What differs now is that a single species is both causing the change and measuring it. Studies generally find that present emissions move much faster than those at the onset of the PETM.
--- question 1
How much of the carbon was volcanic and how much came from methane?

=== event/partition-of-india
src: 39eb251e787f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In August 1947, British India was divided into the two independent states of India and Pakistan, and the new borders drawn through Punjab and Bengal set off mass migration and violence.
--- fact 1
Independence and partition took effect on 14 and 15 August 1947.
--- fact 2
The boundary itself was drawn by a commission under Cyril Radcliffe in a matter of weeks; he later admitted that he had worked from out-of-date maps and census materials.
--- fact 3
Estimates suggest that 12 to 20 million people were displaced from their homes.
--- fact 4
Estimates of the deaths vary widely: 1 to 2 million is most commonly cited, some sources give as many as 3 million, and about 15 million people were displaced.
--- fact 5
Mahatma Gandhi opposed the partition, and was assassinated in January 1948.
--- context
Partition followed from the end of British rule and stands as a case of [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]] in which a line on a map divided communities. [[person/mahatma-gandhi|Mahatma Gandhi]] opposed the division. It sits within the [[era/information-age|Information Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], within the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a line drawn on a map in a few weeks forced more than ten million people to relocate. It is a stark case of a claim-line generating movement, violence and memory that persisted long after the ink had dried.
--- question 1
How many people died?

=== event/peace-of-westphalia
src: 09e985e9806a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 was made up of the treaties of Osnabruck and Munster, which brought an end to both the Thirty Years' War and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic.
--- fact 1
The peace was negotiated from 1644 onward in the Westphalian cities of Osnabruck and Munster. The Spanish-Dutch treaty was signed on 30 January 1648, and the treaty joining the Holy Roman emperor, the German princes, France and Sweden followed on 24 October 1648.
--- fact 2
Spain recognized the independence of the Dutch Republic.
--- fact 3
The settlement extended the principle of the Peace of Augsburg, that rulers determine the religion of their own territory, to cover Calvinists as well.
--- fact 4
It is often cited as the origin of the modern system of sovereign states, though many historians treat that claim as a later simplification.
--- fact 5
It reduced the authority the Holy Roman Emperor held over the German princes.
--- context
This settlement ended the [[event/thirty-years-war|Thirty Years' War]] and the religious conflicts of the [[event/protestant-reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. Its later reputation as the birth of state sovereignty is discussed in [[observer-note/the-fiction-of-claim-lines|The Fiction of Claim-Lines]]. It sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], within the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a treaty built mainly to solve a set of local problems later became a founding myth of the international order. Stories about founding moments, it seems, tend to be assembled after the fact.
--- question 1
Did Westphalia actually create the sovereign state system?
