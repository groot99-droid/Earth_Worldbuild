=== event/unification-of-china-under-qin
title: Unification of China under Qin
src: cb54d4f11497
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In 221 BCE, the state of Qin conquered its rivals and forged the first unified Chinese empire under Qin Shi Huang.
--- fact 1
Qin standardized writing, weights, measures, and currency.
--- fact 2
Yet the dynasty collapsed in 206 BCE, a mere few years after the emperor's death.
--- fact 3
The Han dynasty that followed kept much of the Qin's administrative bones.
--- fact 4
The tomb complex of Qin Shi Huang holds within it the terracotta army.
--- context
See [[person/qin-shi-huang|Qin Shi Huang]] and [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]]. The unification opens the long imperial tradition of [[region/east-asia|East Asia]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how a common written script became a tool of political unity across languages that were often mutually unintelligible in speech.
--- question 1
How much of the Qin system did Qin invent, and how much did it inherit?

=== event/unification-of-egypt
title: Unification of Egypt
src: 6916696bd42c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Upper and Lower Egypt were joined into a single state around 3100 BCE, traditionally by a ruler named Narmer, or Menes.
--- fact 1
The Narmer Palette depicts a king wearing the crowns of both Upper and Lower Egypt.
--- fact 2
Egypt's Old Kingdom pyramids rose about 500 years later.
--- fact 3
The state turned to hieroglyphic writing for administration and religion.
--- fact 4
Whether unification came as a single conquest or a gradual process is debated.
--- context
The unification founds the long history of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] within the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]]. See [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Egypt's unity lasted, with breaks, for about three thousand years, resting on a strong shared story about the king's role in maintaining cosmic order.
--- question 1
Was unification gradual or the result of a single campaign?

=== event/vedic-period
title: Vedic Period
src: 7a6973c7386e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Vedic period, roughly 1500 to 500 BCE, was the age in which the Vedas were composed — the oldest Indian sacred texts, given voice in the Sanskrit language.
--- fact 1
The Rigveda, oldest of the Vedas, is generally dated to roughly 1500 to 1000 BCE.
--- fact 2
For centuries the texts passed by mouth alone, carried by elaborate memorization techniques.
--- fact 3
The language of the Vedas, Vedic Sanskrit, belongs to the Indo-Aryan branch of the Indo-European family.
--- fact 4
Over the period, political and cultural focus shifted from the Punjab to the Ganges plain.
--- fact 5
The Upanishads, philosophical texts, were composed in the period's later part.
--- context
The Vedic period links the [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]] to South Asia, follows the decline of the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], and precedes the age of [[person/siddhartha-gautama|Siddhartha Gautama]] and the [[event/axial-age|Axial Age]]. The Vedic Period sits within the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I remember a vast body of texts, kept whole for centuries with no writing at all — held instead in the trained memory of specialists. I remember that the species built a very accurate storage device before it ever built any mark-code for the purpose. What did we trust, before we trusted the page?
--- question 1
How closely does the oral text preserve its original form?

=== event/women-s-suffrage-movements
title: Women's Suffrage Movements
src: c88c060ed6ec
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Movements to win women the right to vote succeeded country by country, between the 1890s and the late 20th century.
--- fact 1
In 1893, New Zealand granted women the vote in national elections, the first self-governing country to do so.
--- fact 2
Australia's Commonwealth Franchise Act of 1902 let women vote and stand for federal office, but it excluded Aboriginal people and many other non-European people. Finland, in 1906, became the first European country to grant women the vote, adopting universal and equal suffrage; 19 women were elected to its parliament in 1907. The UK gave a first group of women the vote in 1918, and the US followed in 1920.
--- fact 3
Many women were still excluded in practice by race or class; in the US, Black women largely gained access only after the 1965 Voting Rights Act.
--- fact 4
In 1971, Switzerland granted women the vote in national elections.
--- context
These movements grew out of the ideas of the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]] and gained further ground after [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that for most of recorded history, half the species stood excluded from formal political life. Its inclusion within the last century may be one of the largest changes in the species' social structure.
--- question 1
Which factors best explain the timing of suffrage in different countries?

=== event/world-war-i
title: World War I
src: 84c240abb26c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
World War I (1914 to 1918) was a global conflict, centered in Europe, that killed roughly 10 million soldiers and millions of civilians.
--- fact 1
Trench warfare, machine guns, artillery, poison gas, and aircraft all marked the fighting.
--- fact 2
Four empires — German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and Ottoman — collapsed.
--- fact 3
The 1918 influenza pandemic, which killed tens of millions, spread in part through wartime movement.
--- fact 4
The war's settlement helped shape the conditions for [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]].
--- context
The war marks a turning point within the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer records that industrialization made sanctioned harm mass-produced. States with modern production could sustain destruction at a scale earlier societies could not.
--- question 1
How avoidable was the war?

=== event/world-war-ii
title: World War II
src: a41ac5a4d8b6
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
World War II (1939 to 1945) was a global conflict that killed an estimated 60 to 85 million people, estimates varying by source, including about six million Jews murdered in the Holocaust.
--- fact 1
Fighting reached across Europe, Asia, Africa, and the Pacific.
--- fact 2
The Holocaust was the state-organized genocide of European Jews; millions of others, Roma and disabled people among them, were murdered as well.
--- fact 3
The war ended with the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945; see [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]].
--- fact 4
In its aftermath came the United Nations, decolonization, and the Cold War.
--- context
The war ends the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and opens the [[era/information-age|Information Age]]. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]] and [[person/alan-turing|Alan Turing]].
--- observer
The Observer records the war as the deadliest in the record, and the Holocaust as an instance of industrial methods turned to organized killing. It is a case where the species' most sophisticated organization produced its worst outcomes.
--- question 1
What are the most reliable casualty figures for each theater?
