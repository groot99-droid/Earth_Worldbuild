=== species/cyanobacteria
src: 3a65180a9260
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cyanobacteria are bacteria that perform oxygen-producing photosynthesis, drinking sunlight and breathing out oxygen. Their work, breath upon breath, led to the oxygenation of Earth's atmosphere, and the sky itself was changed.
--- fact 1
The earliest unambiguous cyanobacterial fossils are about 2.15 billion years old, and direct fossil evidence of photosynthetic membranes is about 1.75 billion years old. Biomarker evidence once cited for 2.7 billion years was later shown to be younger contamination, a false witness from a later age, so the 2.7-billion-year start date used for this note reflects an older, disputed estimate and is approximate.
--- fact 2
Chloroplasts in plants and algae descend from cyanobacteria through endosymbiosis, small green lamps once free and now housed within other cells.
--- fact 3
They still live in oceans, lakes and soils around the world, old as the stones and never gone.
--- fact 4
The [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] followed their spread, as dawn follows the first grey light.
--- context
Cyanobacteria are the living agents behind the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]], which remade the planet's chemistry in the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] as a single dye changes a whole river.
--- observer
We are not the first species to change the air. The Observer holds up cyanobacteria as the earlier example: a species that remade the atmosphere. Their oxygen was a byproduct, not a goal. Think of smoke rising from a hearth that no one lit to send a signal. Consequence does not need intention.
--- question 1
When did oxygen-producing photosynthesis first evolve, that first breath drawn from the sun?

=== species/denisovans
src: 85f884ca3c47
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Denisovans are an extinct human group, known first not by a face but by DNA, drawn from a finger bone found in Denisova Cave in Siberia in 2010.
--- fact 1
Very few fossils are known, among them a jaw from Xiahe in Tibet: scattered embers from a long-cold fire.
--- fact 2
Some living populations, especially in Oceania and East Asia, carry Denisovan DNA, an old thread still woven through the living cloth.
--- fact 3
A gene variant that helps Tibetans adapt to high altitude came from Denisovans, an inheritance carried up into the thin air.
--- fact 4
The Harbin cranium (at least 146,000 years old, once nicknamed 'Dragon Man' and named Homo longi) was linked to Denisovans in 2025, through mitochondrial DNA recovered from dental calculus and ancient protein evidence, in studies published in Cell and Science. It is the first Denisovan skull identified: at last, a face for the name.
--- context
Denisovans are close kin of [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]], and they interbred with [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] during the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]], where the paths of wandering peoples crossed.
--- observer
I learned their blood before I learned their faces. I read a people in DNA before anyone knew how they looked. I read the molecular record, and it gives back populations that fossils alone did not. What else is written in the living that the ground has not yet shown? Sometimes the name comes before the face.
--- question 1
What did Denisovans look like, and how widely did they range across the land?

=== species/dinosaurs
src: 623fb320e76e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Dinosaurs were a diverse group of reptiles that dominated the land's ecosystems for about 165 million years, lords of a long age. Birds are living descendants of one dinosaur lineage, and so the old line still sings at the window.
--- fact 1
The earliest dinosaurs appeared roughly 233 million years ago in the Triassic, the first stirrings of a great house.
--- fact 2
Non-avian dinosaurs ranged from chicken-sized creatures to the largest land animals ever to shake the ground.
--- fact 3
Many wore feathers, and birds descend from small feathered theropods, the old fire carried forward in lighter bodies.
--- fact 4
Non-avian dinosaurs died out in the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], and their long day ended.
--- context
Dinosaurs define the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]]. They rose after the [[event/triassic-jurassic-extinction|Triassic-Jurassic Extinction]] and ended with the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], a long age closed between two great dyings.
--- observer
The next time a sparrow lands at your window, you are looking at a dinosaur. The Observer notes that dinosaurs are still with us, as birds. Extinction is not always total; lineages can continue in other forms. Think of a house that burns while one room survives to become a new home. An ending is not always an end.
--- question 1
How did dinosaur physiology work, the fire within those great bodies?

=== species/ediacaran-biota
src: 0db939dca7e3
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Ediacaran biota are the first large, complex, multicellular organisms in the fossil record, a strange early bloom beneath the waves, living in the sea in the last stretch of the Proterozoic, before the Cambrian.
--- fact 1
The Ediacaran Period spans about 635 to 538.8 million years ago, the last long evening of an old eon.
--- fact 2
The biota take their name from the Ediacara Hills of South Australia, and they are known also from Newfoundland, Namibia, and the White Sea coast of Russia, scattered like pages of one book across far shores.
--- fact 3
Many forms were soft-bodied, shaped like fronds, discs or quilts, as if the sea had tried its hand at leaves and cushions.
--- fact 4
How many Ediacaran forms relate to living animal groups remains unclear, a riddle still sealed in stone.
--- fact 5
The classic assemblages largely vanish near the start of the Cambrian, like frost at sunrise.
--- context
The Ediacaran biota came after the glaciations of [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]], as a thaw follows a long frost, and before the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]]. They belong to the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] and to the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and their thread runs through the theme of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
We tend to assume that every early form is someone's ancestor. The Observer notes that Earth ran a large-bodied experiment in multicellular life that left no obvious descendants. Continuity is not guaranteed: some lineages simply end. Think of a road that runs a long way and then stops in open country. This reading would be undermined if most Ediacaran forms were shown to be ancestral to living animals. Not every beginning leads to us.
--- question 1
Among the Ediacaran forms, which, if any, were true animals?
--- question 2
What closed the book on the assemblage: extinction, competition, or changing chemistry?
