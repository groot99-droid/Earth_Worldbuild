=== species/anomalocaris
src: cfd23b87fd34
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anomalocaris was a large swimmer of the Cambrian seas, armed with spiny grasping appendages, a round mouth ringed by plates, and great compound eyes. It lived about 515 to 505 million years ago, is known from Canada and Australia, and stands as a stem-group relative of the arthropods.
--- fact 1
Anomalocaris is securely known from two stone archives: the Burgess Shale of British Columbia (about 505 million years old) and the Emu Bay Shale of South Australia (about 515); Chinese fossils once given to it have mostly been moved to other genera.
--- fact 2
Its pieces were first misread as three creatures: in 1892 the appendages as a crustacean tail, the mouth as a jellyfish, the body as a sea cucumber, until Harry Whittington and Derek Briggs showed in 1985 that they belonged to one animal.
--- fact 3
Complete Burgess specimens run about 20 to 25 cm long; estimates of 35 to 50 cm include appendages and tail, and a claim of about 1 m rests only on fragments.
--- fact 4
Compound eyes from the Emu Bay Shale, up to 3 cm long, keep at least 16,000 lenses, rivalling the sharpest-sighted arthropods alive today.
--- fact 5
Its mouth looks too weak to crack trilobite shells, and studies propose suction feeding or soft prey; whether it ruled its seas as the top predator is debated.
--- context
Anomalocaris swam in the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]], a signature animal of the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], sharing the shallow seas with [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]], [[species/hallucigenia|Hallucigenia]] and [[species/pikaia|Pikaia]]. Its sites are the Burgess Shale and the Emu Bay Shale; fossils from the Chengjiang Fossil Site in China, once assigned to it, have since been moved to other genera. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Anomalocaris was first pieced together from three fossils believed to be three different animals, and that the apex predator of popular pictures depends on how you read its mouth. Think of it as one of many body plans that arose and vanished in the Cambrian, when nothing marked in advance which designs would leave descendants. That is the lesson: success looks inevitable only afterward. The reading would weaken if radiodonts were shown to be ancestral to a living animal group.

--- question 2

=== species/barley
src: 24258996195d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Barley is a cereal grass domesticated in the Fertile Crescent from around 9000 BCE, one of the first crops humans ever farmed, and in ancient Mesopotamia it was food, drink and even a standard of value, a grain that fed bodies and kept accounts.
--- fact 1
Barley was tamed from wild barley in the Fertile Crescent, with early domesticated forms recorded in the Jarmo region of modern Iraq from about 9000 to 7000 BCE; grinding stones bearing starch traces of wild barley at Ohalo II, near the Sea of Galilee, show it was eaten long before domestication, by about 23,000 BCE.
--- fact 2
Domestication gave grain heads that hold together instead of shattering, so the plant became far easier to harvest but came to depend on deliberate replanting.
--- fact 3
Barley was likely among the first grains brewed into beer, one of the earliest alcoholic drinks made by Neolithic farmers.
--- fact 4
In Mesopotamia barley served as a de facto currency, the shekel standing as a unit of its weight, and cuneiform records from around 2350 BCE, under the ruler Urukagina, tell of monthly barley rations issued to adults.
--- fact 5
Barley travelled out of the Fertile Crescent across Eurasia, reaching eastern Finland by about 4200 BCE and the Indus civilization region between roughly 5,700 and 3,300 years ago; by about 2000 BCE its use had spread throughout Eurasia, and it was grown on the Korean peninsula from about 1500 to 850 BCE.
--- fact 6
World production reached about 146 million tonnes in 2023, the fourth largest cereal harvest after maize, rice and wheat; about 70 percent goes to animal feed and about 30 percent to malting for beer and whisky.
--- context
Barley was domesticated beside [[species/wheat|Wheat]] as one of the founding crops of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent, and it carried the economies of early Mesopotamian cities such as [[place/ur|Ur]] and, later, [[place/babylon|Babylon]]. It belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I notice a grain that was both food and a unit of account, two functions many later societies kept apart: food, and money. I notice how Mesopotamian rationing records turn barley into a record of a kin-lattice's obligations, who was owed what, and by whom. I notice that brewing barley into beer is one of the oldest recorded transformations of a raw crop into something socially significant, tying farming to gathering and celebration.

What is a debt, if not a meal promised? The reading would weaken if barley's early monetary role proves to have been more local and limited than the shekel-based accounts suggest.
--- question 1

--- question 2
