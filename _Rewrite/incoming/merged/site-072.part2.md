=== place/yellow-river
src: ab5f414c2767
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Yellow River, the Huang He, runs about 5,460 kilometers through northern China; its loess-laden floodplain nursed early farming and the Shang and Zhou states, and its floods and shifting course have shaped Chinese history ever since.
--- fact 1
The Yellow River rises in the Bayan Har Mountains of Qinghai and runs through northern China to the Bohai Sea.
--- fact 2
Farming communities growing millet are recorded in the Yellow River basin from about the 7th to 6th millennia BCE, and the Shang and Zhou states rose in its middle and lower reaches.
--- fact 3
The river carries a heavy load of loess sediment down from the Loess Plateau, which has lifted its bed above the surrounding plain in the lower reaches and made dikes a necessity; records tell of many breaches and several major changes of course.
--- fact 4
From 1128 — some sources give 1194 — until 1855, the river mostly ran south of the Shandong Peninsula into the Yellow Sea, until a breach around 1851 to 1855 turned it back to a northern course.
--- fact 5
A flood in 1887 is estimated to have taken between about 900,000 and 2 million lives.
--- fact 6
In 1938, during the Second Sino-Japanese War, Nationalist forces breached the dikes at Huayuankou to slow the Japanese advance; several hundred thousand people died, with published estimates running up to about 900,000, and millions more were displaced.
--- context
The Yellow River is bound to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in northern China, to the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]] and the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] as states that organized flood control along it, and to the [[event/second-sino-japanese-war|Second Sino-Japanese War]]. It belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a river which floods without warning helped tie authority to the architecture of water control, and the traditional tale of Yu the Great, said to have tamed the floods and founded the first dynasty, binds rule to engineering as a kind of civic myth. The 1938 breach shows you the same river turned to sanctioned harm, used by a state against an invading army, with civilians paying the greater cost. Treat the Yellow River as the sole cradle of Chinese civilization with some caution, too — it is itself a story, since sites on the Yangtze and elsewhere show early farming and state formation of their own. And the very idea that flood control drove state formation is contested; specialists could weaken this reading by showing other factors mattered more.
--- question 1
How large was the death toll of the 1938 flood, and how many people did it displace?
--- question 2
How much did flood control shape early state formation, set against warfare and trade?

=== culture/anglo-saxons
src: 2e452127e539
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Anglo-Saxons were Germanic-speaking settlers, and their descendants after them, who founded the English kingdoms of early medieval Britain, by convention from the fifth century until the Norman Conquest of 1066; their tongue became Old English.
--- fact 1
Settlers from the North Sea coasts, whom Bede named Angles, Saxons and Jutes, reached eastern and southern Britain from the fifth century, perhaps earlier, as Roman rule there ended.
--- fact 2
Most Anglo-Saxons farmed from timber-built villages; from the seventh century, trading towns such as Ipswich and Hamwic appeared, and slavery was a common practice among them.
--- fact 3
Small kingdoms, later grouped under the label of the seven-kingdom Heptarchy, competed for overlordship; Kent's law code, probably from about 600, set compensation according to rank.
--- fact 4
Augustine's mission reached Kent in 597; the Sutton Hoo ship burial, from the early seventh century, held Byzantine silver, and the practice of furnished burial had largely ended by about 680.
--- fact 5
Vikings raided Lindisfarne in 793; Alfred of Wessex defeated Guthrum in 878; Cnut conquered England in 1016; and Harold Godwinson died at Hastings in 1066.
--- context
The Anglo-Saxons settled Britain, within [[region/europe|Europe]], during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], as the [[culture/romans|Romans]] departed and the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] unfolded. They shared the island with Celtic-speaking Britons (see [[culture/celts|Celts]]), turned to [[culture/christianity|Christianity]], and fought the [[culture/norse|Norse]] before the Norman conquest. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Anglo-Saxons bound strangers together first through the architecture of kinship, its duties priced in wergild, and then through a Christian order that kings themselves adopted. Watch how their funeral practice shifted, from graves heavy with goods to plain ones, and what survives of them now is buried gold, monastic manuscripts, and Offa's Dyke, perhaps a boundary claim in earth. A specialist could argue that "Anglo-Saxon" was a modern scholar's label more than a felt, shared identity, and that argument would weaken a reading of one people held together by shared obligation.
--- question 1
How large was the Anglo-Saxon migration, and how did the settlers and the Britons deal with one another?
--- question 2
Should "Anglo-Saxon" stay the scholarly label, given the debate over its meaning and its modern political use?

=== culture/etruscans
src: 961eb44409d7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Etruscans were a people of Etruria in central Italy — roughly modern Tuscany and northern Lazio — whose city-states flourished from about the eighth to the third century BCE, before Rome conquered and absorbed them by degrees.
--- fact 1
Etruscan city-states included Tarquinia, Cerveteri, Vulci and Veii, and Livy tells of a league of twelve peoples meeting at Voltumna's shrine.
--- fact 2
Their wealth rose from farming, metals and sea trade: Populonia smelted iron ore from Elba, and ports such as Pyrgi linked Etruria to the Greeks and Phoenicians.
--- fact 3
They wrote a non-Indo-European tongue in an alphabet drawn from the Greek, and the gold tablets found at Pyrgi, bearing Etruscan and Phoenician texts alike, date to about 500 BCE.
--- fact 4
Etruscans and Carthaginians fought the Phocaean Greeks at Alalia about 540 BCE, and Greek fleets defeated the Etruscans at Cumae in 474 BCE.
--- fact 5
Rome traditionally took Veii in 396 BCE and sacked Volsinii in 264 BCE; the Etruscans gained Roman citizenship around 90 BCE, and their language lived on into the first century CE.
--- context
The Etruscans flourished in [[region/europe|Europe]] from the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]], trading with the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]], [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]] and [[place/carthage|Carthage]], before the [[culture/romans|Romans]] absorbed them. Etruscan writing was likely one route by which the [[technology/alphabet|Alphabet]] reached Latin. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I read what the Romans left of the Etruscans and find not one state but a shared tongue and a shared way of meaning — cities bound by belief before they were bound by any crown. Their ritual books, Roman writers say, treated the drawing of a boundary as a sacred act.

At Cerveteri I walk a cemetery built like a town: streets of houses for the dead. Their own books are lost to me, so tombs and short inscriptions are what remain.

Was there ever one Etruscan people, or only neighbors that outsiders counted as one? A specialist could call the league's unity a Greek and Roman simplification — and if so, my reading of one community would not hold.

What a people believe together may outlast what they rule together.
--- question 1
Did the Etruscans see themselves as one people, or as citizens of separate cities?
--- question 2
How far did Etruscan rulers shape early Rome, given that stories of Etruscan kings are partly legendary?

=== culture/funan
src: 368a707219f6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Funan is the Chinese name given to a polity, perhaps a league of towns, in the Mekong delta and Cambodia from about the 1st to the 6th century CE, known to us through Chinese records and the excavations at Oc Eo; no known local text uses the name itself.
--- fact 1
A canal of 70 to 80 km linked the port of Oc Eo to Angkor Borei, likely serving both transport and water management.
--- fact 2
Oc Eo yielded Roman gold medallions of Antoninus Pius, and possibly Marcus Aurelius, alongside Indian and Persian objects.
--- fact 3
Chinese sources credit the early 3rd-century king Fan Shiman with a fleet and attacks upon more than ten kingdoms, and later histories quote the envoys Kang Tai and Zhu Ying describing walled towns and archives.
--- fact 4
Funan practiced both Hinduism and Buddhism, and left Sanskrit inscriptions behind.
--- fact 5
Chinese records list envoys from Funan's last known king, Rudravarman, down to 539; Chenla is said to have annexed Funan somewhere between the 6th century and about 627, though scholars dispute how.
--- context
Funan lay in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], beside [[culture/champa|Champa]] and along the sea routes of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] that carried [[culture/hinduism|Hinduism]] and [[culture/buddhism|Buddhism]] to it. Its canals in the [[place/mekong-river|Mekong River]] delta relate to other [[technology/irrigation-canals|Irrigation Canals]], and the later [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] arose nearby. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I picture Roman, Indian, and Persian goods gathered at one port, and I wonder if the rulers' borrowed script and religion doubled as a signal, meant to bind strangers into one trust.

Canals remain. Statues remain. But the name Funan, and any claim to its borders, come to me mostly through outsiders' words, not its own.

Was it one realm, or only ports that agreed, for a while, to be counted together? A specialist could tell me it was the second, and my reading of one story would not survive that telling.
--- question 1
Was Funan a single kingdom, or several centers that Chinese writers chose to treat as one?
--- question 2
Did Chenla conquer Funan outright, or did power merely shift among Cambodian centers?
--- question 3
What did Funan's own people call themselves, and what tongue did they speak?

=== culture/garifuna
src: 5ec1c9d35399
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Garifuna are an Afro-Indigenous people, formed on St Vincent from African and Kalinago ancestry, deported by Britain to Central America in 1797, and living now along the Caribbean coast from Belize to Nicaragua.
--- fact 1
Tradition traces Garifuna origins to Africans from Spanish slave ships wrecked near St Vincent, in 1635 or about 1675; no eyewitness account of this is known, and many historians favor instead an origin among runaways from nearby islands.
--- fact 2
Garifuna women grew cassava and made ereba bread, while the men fished; remittances now supplement many rural incomes.
--- fact 3
Chatoyer, a Black Carib leader, resisted British expansion in the First Carib War, from 1769 to 1773, and was killed on 14 March 1795 in the Second Carib War.
--- fact 4
Britain deported over 4,000 Garifuna to Baliceaux from 1796, and about half died there; about 2,000 landed on Roatan on 12 April 1797, and most of them moved on afterward to Trujillo.
--- fact 5
UNESCO proclaimed Garifuna language, dance and music in 2001, and in 2015 the Inter-American Court found that Honduras had violated the collective property rights of two Garifuna communities.
--- context
The Garifuna took shape in the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], through the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and alongside the [[culture/kalinago|Kalinago]]. Their war and deportation in the 1790s fell within the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Garifuna joined African and Kalinago ancestry into one architecture of kinship, sustained by a shared language and the dugu ancestor rite, through an exile that killed thousands. The 1773 treaty's promised boundary did not hold, and Honduran land titling later failed some Garifuna communities, as the Inter-American Court found. A specialist could argue that the shipwreck story is a later founding narrative, and that argument would weaken any reading that dates the kinship structure to one seventeenth-century event.
--- question 1
How much of the shipwreck story rests on documented events, rather than on later tradition?
--- question 2
How did Arawakan grammar and Carib-derived vocabulary come together in Garifuna speech?
