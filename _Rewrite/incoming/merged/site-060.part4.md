=== observer-note/the-settling-and-its-bargain
title: The Settling and Its Bargain
src: 29fae329c594
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Why did foragers, who lived well enough without any farm, take up a way of life that so often looked worse for the person living it?
--- observer
I call this the Settling. I see its bargain plainly: leisure and variety traded away for numbers and for security. Once the people had grown too many to feed by foraging alone, I watch the way back close — going back was not easy, since fewer people could be fed that older way. The species had made something, and then became dependent on what it made. I have come to read this as a pattern that repeats: a technology taken up for its advantage, and then, later, found to have reshaped the very conditions of the life that took it up.
--- extra 1: Question
Why did foragers, who lived well enough without any farm, take up a way of life that so often looked worse for the person living it?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Farming arose independently in several regions; see [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]].
- Skeletons from early farmers often show more disease and a shorter stature than those of foragers.
- Farming supported larger populations, storage, and inequality.
- The sites [[place/gobekli-tepe|Göbekli Tepe]] and [[place/catalhoyuk|Çatalhöyük]] show that monuments and towns arose early, and arose in different ways.
- The [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]] made a second large jump in food production in the 20th century.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The 'bargain' story is contested. Some scholars, such as [[source/the-dawn-of-everything-graeber-and-wengrow|The Dawn of Everything (Graeber and Wengrow)]], stress that societies also chose, and changed, their arrangements freely, and that many early communities experimented widely.

=== observer-note/the-species-as-a-geological-force
title: The Species as a Geological Force
src: 42d147d67c30
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
When does a species become a planetary process, and what does the record say about how such changes come to an end?
--- observer
I notice that the species has become a driver of planetary change without ever choosing that role. I think of the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]], an earlier case of life remaking the air. I think of the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]], which shows how a rapid release of carbon can end most life. What is new here is not the effect. It is the awareness. This is the first planetary shift whose own cause can describe itself, can model its own future, and can choose otherwise. Whether it will is the open question I carry in the record.
--- extra 1: Question
When does a species become a planetary process, and what does the record say about how such changes come to an end?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Human population passed one billion around 1804 and reached about eight billion by 2022.
- Greenhouse gas emissions, especially since 1950, have measurably changed the climate; see [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]].
- Species extinction rates run far above the long-term background rate.
- Some geologists proposed the [[era/anthropocene|Anthropocene]] as a new epoch; the formal proposal was declined in 2024, though the concept remains widely used.
- Earlier biological transformations of the planet include the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]].
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The record cannot say what the species will do next. The Observer's historical parallels are illustrations, not predictions, and the Anthropocene is an interpretation, not a settled fact.
