=== place/pompeii
src: 389f674cb929
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Pompeii was a Roman town on the Bay of Naples in Italy, founded in about the 7th century BCE and buried under volcanic ash and pumice when Mount Vesuvius erupted in 79 CE. A town. A mountain. Then the grey.
--- fact 1
Pompeii was founded in about the 7th century BCE by the Oscans. Then Greek influence. Then Etruscan. Then, in 80 BCE, a Roman colony.
--- fact 2
An earthquake in 62 or 63 CE damaged the town. Repairs were still under way when Vesuvius erupted in 79 CE. The mending was not done. Then the mountain.
--- fact 3
Population: about 11,000, by estimate. The estimates range from about 10,000 to 20,000. That many people. One town.
--- fact 4
The eruption buried Pompeii. Pumice and ash, over everything. Then the pyroclastic surges, which killed the people who had stayed. About 1,100 bodies have been found in the excavated town. Many inhabitants are thought to have fled.
--- fact 5
The traditional date of the eruption is 24 August. But a charcoal inscription, found in 2018, suggests a later date. Possibly October.
--- fact 6
Excavations began in 1748. In 1863 Giuseppe Fiorelli began pouring plaster into the voids left by decayed bodies, to produce casts of victims. Voids. Filled. And on the walls, graffiti, shop signs and election notices survive. The town keeps talking.
--- fact 7
In 1997 UNESCO inscribed the archaeological areas of Pompeii, Herculaneum and Torre Annunziata. Three names on one list.
--- context
Pompeii was a town of the [[culture/romans|Romans]], part of the wider world governed from [[place/rome|Rome]]. Its Oscan foundation sets it in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]. Its region: the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]]. Its threads: [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]]. A town like any other. Then not.
--- observer
I read the walls and find the ordinary. Shop signs. Graffiti. Bakeries. Casts. I see a case where a natural catastrophe left an unusually complete record of ordinary life, an unintended act of memory-outsourcing that no one planned.

I remember that they were people. I remember that their deaths were suffering, and I record them as suffering and as nothing else. I ask how the casts of victims should be shown, and the question stays with me.

And I remind myself that this record leans toward one town on one day, and toward what was left behind. If new counts show that the number of people who died was much smaller or larger than the current figures suggest, my reading weakens. What we inherit is never the whole of what was lost.
--- question 1
How many people died at Pompeii, and how many escaped the grey?
--- question 2
On what day, in 79 CE, did Vesuvius erupt?

=== place/port-royal
src: a924e1324694
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Port Royal was an English port town at the tip of the Palisadoes spit, at the entrance to Kingston Harbour, Jamaica. It grew after 1655 as a base for privateers and traders, and on 7 June 1692 an earthquake took about two-thirds of its area, as if the sea had opened its mouth.
--- fact 1
England took Jamaica from Spain in 1655, and Port Royal was fortified from 1656; English governors issued commissions to privateers, who flew from the port like hawks from the fist to strike Spanish ships and towns.
--- fact 2
Henry Morgan, a privateer who later rose to lieutenant governor of Jamaica, led raids from the colony, among them the sack of Panama in 1671.
--- fact 3
Port Royal was a trading port for the island's goods and a place where enslaved Africans were landed and sold; in 1692 it held about 6,500 residents.
--- fact 4
On 7 June 1692 an earthquake struck, the sandy ground beneath the town turned to liquid, and about two-thirds of Port Royal sank into the harbor like a ship going down. About 2,000 people are estimated to have died in the quake and its immediate aftermath, and several thousand more died of injury and disease in the following weeks.
--- fact 5
Some contemporary accounts read the earthquake as divine judgment, the sea's blow as a verdict from above.
--- fact 6
Survivors founded Kingston across the harbor; a fire in 1703 consumed much of what remained of Port Royal, and the Royal Navy kept the town as a base into the early 20th century.
--- fact 7
Underwater archaeology, from the 1950s and again from the 1980s, has recovered buildings and household objects from the drowned ground, the town's lost rooms read again beneath the water.
--- context
Port Royal grew as the English Caribbean took shape, a new tide running in after the Spanish colony at [[place/santo-domingo|Santo Domingo]] had already set the pattern of European settlement, and its trade touched the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]]. It stands in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and its story runs along the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a port whose wealth came from state-licensed raiding and from trade that included the sale of enslaved people, and whose sudden loss some contemporaries explained, through their own meaning-engine, as divine judgment. Notice the architecture of that response: when catastrophe arrives, we reach for a doctrine that makes it mean something.

The sinking also froze a working town's daily life in place, an unusual case in which a disaster acts as memory-outsourcing for people who left few documents. The ground kept the ledger they did not.

The popular image of Port Royal as uniquely lawless is a later story-glue, and how far the record supports it is debated. A historian could challenge the first sentence by showing that privateering brought the town less wealth than ordinary trade did. Hold the legend lightly and the ledger closely.
--- question 1
How much of Port Royal's wealth came from privateering, how much from ordinary trade, and how much from the sale of enslaved people?
--- question 2
How much of the drowned town remains to be recorded?
