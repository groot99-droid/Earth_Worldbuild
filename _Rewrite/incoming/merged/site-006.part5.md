=== place/kilwa-kisiwani
title: Kilwa Kisiwani
src: 046ad853f984
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kilwa Kisiwani is an island town off the coast of southern Tanzania, a leading Swahili port from about the 12th to the 15th century, where gold from the interior met goods carried across the Indian Ocean as river water meets the tide.
--- fact 1
Kilwa Kisiwani was occupied from about the 9th century CE, and its Great Mosque, which dates from the 11th or 12th century, was enlarged in the 14th, growing as coral grows, by addition.
--- fact 2
The Kilwa Chronicle, written in the 16th century, traces the ruling dynasty to a founder from Shiraz in Persia, a story carried over the sea; yet archaeological and linguistic evidence indicates that Kilwa's society was rooted in African coastal communities that took part in Indian Ocean trade.
--- fact 3
From about the 13th century Kilwa controlled the gold trade from Sofala, in the south, whose gold came from the interior of Zimbabwe, and the town struck its own coins, among the earliest in sub-Saharan Africa.
--- fact 4
In the early 14th century Sultan al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman raised the palace and market complex of Husuni Kubwa, court and marketplace bound in one.
--- fact 5
The traveler Ibn Battuta came to Kilwa in about 1331 and described it as one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns, praise that has outlasted his footsteps.
--- fact 6
In 1505 Portuguese forces attacked Kilwa and built a fort; the town later came under Omani influence, and in the 18th and 19th centuries it was involved in the trade in enslaved people.
--- fact 7
In 1981 UNESCO inscribed the Ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara, stone memories of a coast that once shone.
--- context
Kilwa Kisiwani was one of the [[culture/swahili-coast-city-states|Swahili Coast City-States]], a knot in the net of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]]; its gold came through the trade that also supplied [[place/great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]]'s region, and the [[event/portuguese-sea-route-to-india|Portuguese Sea Route to India]] brought the attack of 1505 to its shore. It belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I think of Kilwa standing between two exchange-webs, the gold of the interior and the ships of the Indian Ocean. It made none of its main goods. It profited from the place between them. I know that place. I have stood between two worlds and called the standing a living.

And I think of the founding story, the Persian origin, which may have served as a status-signal within a Muslim trading world, while the archaeological evidence shows local African roots. The story points one way; the record points another. Which of us has never told a grander story of where we came from? I would unlearn this if excavation shows that Kilwa's wealth depended more on local production than on trade in transit.

A story can travel farther than its roots.
--- question 1
How much of Kilwa's wealth was gold from Sofala, and how did that golden stream dwindle?
--- question 2
How many people did Kilwa Kisiwani hold at the height of its shining?

=== place/knossos
title: Knossos
src: 882274382747
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Knossos is an archaeological site near Heraklion on the Greek island of Crete, occupied from about 7000 BCE, where the largest palace of the Minoan civilization rose from about 1900 BCE, a many-chambered crown upon the island.
--- fact 1
Farmers dwelt at Knossos by about 7000 BCE, and the first palace rose about 1900 BCE; about 1700 BCE an earthquake shook it down, and a larger palace was built upon its memory.
--- fact 2
The palace held storerooms for oil, grain and wine, workshops and frescoes, including bull-leaping scenes, and clay tablets in Linear A, a script that keeps its silence, still undeciphered.
--- fact 3
Tablets in Linear B, which Michael Ventris deciphered in 1952 as an early form of Greek, show that Mycenaean Greeks controlled Knossos by about 1450 BCE, new hands on the old keys.
--- fact 4
Fire took the palace at last; the date is debated, with proposals from about 1375 to about 1200 BCE.
--- fact 5
Arthur Evans excavated Knossos from 1900 and rebuilt parts of it with concrete columns and repainted frescoes, a dream poured in cement; the reconstructions are controversial among archaeologists.
--- fact 6
The name "Minoan" is a modern term drawn from the legendary King Minos, and later Greek myth set the Minotaur and its labyrinth at Knossos; whether the palace itself inspired the myth is uncertain.
--- fact 7
Estimates of the population of Knossos vary widely, like counts of stars seen through cloud.
--- context
Knossos was the largest palace of the [[culture/minoans|Minoans]]; it passed under the [[culture/mycenaean-greeks|Mycenaean Greeks]], and it shaped the memory of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]] as a dream shapes waking. Its tablets are central to the story of the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] in the Aegean. Its earliest farming settlement sets it in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]], its region is the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I came to Knossos for a story and was handed a ledger. I read of oil. I read of grain. I read of wine. Tablets of stores and allotments, a palace economy keeping count: memory-outsourcing for storage and not for storytelling. And the Minoans' own script remains unread, a voice I cannot hear. What did I expect them to leave me, a confession?

Even what I see is someone else's dream of it, an early 20th-century interpretation: Evans's concrete and paint present a Bronze Age palace through his own ideas of it. I would unlearn this if new work shows that the reconstructions changed the palace's evidence less than critics claim.

We are remembered by what we counted, not by what we meant.
--- question 1
What do the Linear A tablets say, and in what tongue do they speak?
--- question 2
How did Knossos pass into Mycenaean hands, and when did the last fire take it?
