=== place/timbuktu
src: 4fe726030011
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Timbuktu is a city in Mali on the southern edge of the Sahara that became a hub of the trans-Saharan trade and of Islamic scholarship, a lamp of learning set at the desert's shore.
--- fact 1
It was founded around 1100 CE as a seasonal camp, a pause in the turning of the seasons.
--- fact 2
Salt from the Sahara and gold from the south met there, white wealth and yellow trading hands.
--- fact 3
The Sankore mosque and madrasa were centers of learning, wells from which scholars drew.
--- fact 4
It holds hundreds of thousands of historical manuscripts, a memory stored like water against drought, and in 1988 it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
--- context
Its story is bound to the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]] and to [[person/mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]], like gold to the sand.
--- observer
I think of a city at the edge of a desert. I think of it becoming famous as a repository of knowledge. I think of manuscript libraries kept in private homes, another form of distributed memory, held in many hands.

What do we keep when we keep it at home? Memory, too, can be kept by a household.
--- question 1
What do the surviving manuscripts contain, and what do they remember?

=== place/troy
src: 6a0b20fa9362
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Troy is an archaeological site at Hisarlık in northwestern Turkey, occupied from about 3000 BCE into the Byzantine era in nine main layers, and identified by many scholars as the city of Homer's Iliad: a city stacked on itself like the rings of an old tree.
--- fact 1
Troy stands at Hisarlık, near the Dardanelles in Çanakkale Province, Turkey; excavators distinguish nine main settlement layers, Troy I to Troy IX, beginning around 3000 BCE, each city laid upon the one before.
--- fact 2
Heinrich Schliemann began excavations at Troy in 1870, and after him came Wilhelm Dörpfeld, Carl Blegen and Manfred Korfmann, each turning the earth anew.
--- fact 3
Schliemann's "Treasure of Priam" came from Troy II, about 2500 BCE, far older than any Trojan War; he carried it out of the Ottoman Empire, and it vanished from Berlin in 1945 and resurfaced in Moscow in the 1990s, a hoard that wandered like a ghost.
--- fact 4
Troy VI (about 1700 to 1300 BCE) was a large fortified city, walled like a clenched fist, and Troy VIIa was destroyed by fire around 1180 BCE; it is often proposed as the city of the Trojan War, if the war has a historical basis.
--- fact 5
Hittite texts speak of a land called Wilusa in western Anatolia, whose name is often linked to Ilios, a Greek name for Troy, as an echo is linked to a voice; the identification is debated.
--- fact 6
Whether a Trojan War took place is unknown; the Iliad was composed in Greek around the 8th century BCE, centuries after the events it describes, memory ripening slowly into song.
--- fact 7
Greeks and Romans later raised the city of Ilion at the site, the old name worn by a new city, and UNESCO inscribed Troy in 1998.
--- context
Troy had the [[culture/hittites|Hittites]] and the [[culture/mycenaean-greeks|Mycenaean Greeks]] as neighbors and lived on in the memory of the later [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]]; Alexander visited it in 334 BCE during [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. The destruction of Troy VIIa falls close in time to the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Troy is a place where a story led to a spade. Schliemann set out to find the city of the Iliad and did find a city, though not necessarily the city of the poem. That is the lesson worth practicing: a narrative can direct an excavation and then be mistaken for its evidence.

The poem's story-glue gave the site a long life as a destination, from Alexander to modern tourists. Think of how often you arrive somewhere already knowing what you expect to find.

The reading would weaken if the identification of Wilusa with Troy is refuted, or if Troy VIIa proves unrelated to any historical conflict. A good story is a map, not the ground it describes.
--- question 1
Did a war take place at Troy, and if it did, in which layer of the stacked city?
--- question 2
How many people lived within Troy VI?

=== place/upano-valley-settlements
src: 4e1af9847ab8
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Upano Valley settlements are a network of ancient urban centers in the Ecuadorian Amazon, documented by lidar and reported in 2024, occupied from about 500 BCE for many centuries, cities that slept beneath the green.
--- fact 1
The network spans roughly 300 square kilometers (about 115 square miles) and includes some 15 urban centers, a constellation hidden under leaves.
--- fact 2
More than 6,000 rectangular earthen platforms, probably the foundations of houses and civic buildings, are linked by roads and drainage canals, a web spun across the valley.
--- fact 3
About 2,000 years ago the valley probably held at least 10,000 farmers, and possibly many more, a quiet multitude.
--- fact 4
Between about 300 and 600 CE the population began to leave, and why they went remains unknown, a door closed without a word.
--- context
The valley is among the earliest known Amazonian urban settlements. Follow its thread to [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]] and the [[culture/casarabe-culture|Casarabe Culture]].
--- observer
I think of cities roughly contemporary with Rome's rise. I think of them unknown to science until laser mapping stripped away the forest. I think of everything else I have not yet looked for.

How much is missing only because no one searched? Absence from the record can reflect what was not yet looked for. Not found is not the same as not there.
--- question 1
Who were the people of the Upano Valley, and why did they leave?
