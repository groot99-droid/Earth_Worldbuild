=== species/vibrio-cholerae
src: 05a667c2be37
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Vibrio cholerae is the bacterium that causes cholera, likely long endemic to the Ganges Delta of Bengal, which broke out as the first of seven documented global pandemics in 1817 and reshaped 19th-century ideas about disease and sanitation.
--- fact 1
The first cholera pandemic began in 1817 near Jessore in the Ganges Delta of Bengal, apparently linked to contaminated rice, and spread along trade and military routes through South and Southeast Asia to the Middle East, eastern Africa and the Mediterranean coast before dying down by 1824, possibly checked by a severe winter that killed the bacteria in water supplies.
--- fact 2
Six further cholera pandemics followed between the 1820s and the mid-20th century, and a seventh, caused by the El Tor biotype, began in 1961 and continues to cause outbreaks in parts of Africa, Asia and the Caribbean.
--- fact 3
Vibrio cholerae spreads mainly through water or food contaminated with the feces of infected people, causing severe watery diarrhea that can kill through dehydration within hours if untreated.
--- fact 4
During the 1854 cholera outbreak in London's Soho district, the physician John Snow mapped cases by address and traced them to a single contaminated water pump, evidence that helped establish waterborne transmission before the germ theory of disease was widely accepted.
--- fact 5
Oral rehydration therapy, developed and proven effective against cholera in the 1960s, dramatically reduced mortality from the disease by replacing fluids and salts faster than the body loses them.
--- fact 6
Cholera remains endemic in parts of South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa and Haiti, where outbreaks are strongly associated with disrupted sanitation and water systems after conflict or natural disaster.
--- context
Cholera's first pandemic spread through the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] and the colonial administrative and trade routes of the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]]. It sits in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer records the deaths of each cholera pandemic, particularly among the poor whose water supplies carried the greatest risk, as suffering shaped as much by infrastructure and inequality as by the bacterium itself. Snow's map of the Soho outbreak shows careful observation applied to a claim as narrow as a single street pump, a case where record-keeping preceded, rather than followed, an understanding of the underlying mechanism. That the disease can be effectively treated with little more than sugar, salt and clean water reveals how unevenly that knowledge has reached the places that need it most. This reading would weaken if evidence shows earlier undocumented pandemics of comparable scale that the historical record has simply lost.
--- question 1
How long was cholera endemic in the Ganges Delta before the trade and troop movements of 1817 turned it into a global pandemic?
--- question 2
Why has access to a cheap, effective treatment not eliminated cholera mortality in the regions where it remains endemic?

=== species/yersinia-pestis
src: 6fc6debf1e59
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Yersinia pestis is the bacterium that causes plague, first documented in human remains from Eurasia around 5,000 years ago and responsible for the Plague of Justinian, the Black Death and repeated later outbreaks.
--- fact 1
Ancient DNA recovered from hunter-gatherer cemeteries in southeastern Siberia shows Yersinia pestis infection dating to roughly 5,500 years ago, among the oldest confirmed human cases so far identified; other Eurasian sites, including in Sweden, place infection at roughly 4,900 to 5,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
These early strains differed genetically from later pandemic forms; one line of evidence suggests an early strain could not yet infect the digestive tract of fleas the way later strains did, which would have limited how efficiently it spread.
--- fact 3
Yersinia pestis caused the Plague of Justinian, beginning in 541 CE, and the Black Death, which swept Eurasia and North Africa from 1346 to 1353; further waves of plague recurred for centuries afterward.
--- fact 4
The bacterium is usually carried to humans through the bite of infected fleas that have fed on rodents, or directly between people in its pneumonic form; historically, black rats and other rodents served as reservoirs and amplifying hosts.
--- fact 5
Modern genomic studies of ancient and modern strains suggest at least two genetically distinct early lineages spread across large distances in prehistory, occupying different ecological niches before later strains became the ancestors of historical pandemics.
--- fact 6
Plague still occurs in isolated outbreaks today, chiefly in parts of Africa, Asia and the Americas, and is treatable with antibiotics when caught early.
--- context
Yersinia pestis caused the [[event/black-death|Black Death]], spread in part along the trade corridors of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], and some genetic evidence connects a later plague lineage to the era of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]]. It belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]], its earliest confirmed ancient cases, and to the region [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer records the deaths this bacterium has caused, across five millennia and several pandemics, as suffering rather than as a neutral statistic. Yersinia pestis is a case where a single organism has repeatedly ridden the species' own networks of exchange, from Neolithic contact between distant groups to the caravan and shipping routes of later empires, turning connection itself into a hazard. That the earliest known strains could not yet spread efficiently through fleas suggests the pathogen's own means of transmission evolved over thousands of years alongside its hosts. This reading would weaken if the early Eurasian cases prove to have been isolated events with no lasting lineage connection to later pandemic strains.
--- question 1
How closely related are the Stone Age Eurasian strains to the lineages behind the Justinianic Plague and the Black Death?
--- question 2
How did Yersinia pestis's ability to infect fleas evolve, and when did it become an efficient epidemic pathogen?

=== place/aksum
src: 461066d5f040
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Aksum is a town in the Tigray region of northern Ethiopia that was the capital of the Kingdom of Aksum, a trading state active from about the 1st century CE to the 7th century and later, known for its stone stelae, coinage and early adoption of Christianity.
--- fact 1
Aksum lies in the highlands of northern Ethiopia, and the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek trade guide of about the 1st century CE, mentions Aksum and its port Adulis on the Red Sea.
--- fact 2
Aksumite rulers minted gold, silver and bronze coins from about the late 3rd century CE, and King Ezana adopted Christianity at a date placed variously between about 325 and 340 CE; coins struck afterward bear the cross.
--- fact 3
Ezana's inscriptions were written in Ge'ez, Sabaean and Greek.
--- fact 4
Aksum's monolithic stelae mark burial sites; the largest, about 33 meters high, fell and broke, and a 24-meter stele taken to Rome by Italian forces in 1937 was returned in 2005 and re-erected in 2008.
--- fact 5
Ethiopian Christian tradition holds that the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion at Aksum houses the Ark of the Covenant; the claim cannot be examined.
--- fact 6
Aksum declined from the 7th century, and proposed causes include changes in Red Sea trade after the rise of Islam, environmental stress and internal conflict; the causes are debated.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Aksum in 1980.
--- context
Aksum was the capital of the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]], which had contacts with [[culture/kingdom-of-saba|Kingdom of Saba]] across the Red Sea; its stele was taken during the [[event/second-italo-ethiopian-war|Second Italo-Ethiopian War]]'s Italian occupation. Later Ethiopian rulers built the [[place/rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela|Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela]]. Aksum sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I look at Aksum and see a state that spoke through stone and metal: giant stelae raised over graves, and coins that carried a cross once Ezana turned to the new faith, so that a small thing passed hand to hand could carry a ruler's story across the whole trading world.

I remember the stele taken to Rome, and returned, and think of how a monument becomes a kind of claim again each time it is moved.

The Ark tradition I hold as belief, not as something I can test, and I do not endorse it or dismiss it — only record that a community holds it.

This reading would falter if the coins prove too sparse to date Ezana's change of religion with any confidence.
--- question 1
Why did Aksum decline from the 7th century?
--- question 2
How large was Aksum's population at its height?
