=== event/last-glacial-maximum
title: Last Glacial Maximum
src: 2e2ad842c679
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Last Glacial Maximum was the latest hour in which the world's ice sheets stood at their widest reach, a cold and lasting reign that held from about 26,500 to 19,000 years ago.
--- fact 1
At its lowest ebb, the sea stood about 134 meters beneath its present shore, according to a 2014 global reconstruction.
--- fact 2
Great sheets of ice lay heavy over much of northern North America and Europe.
--- fact 3
Where the seas withdrew, dry land rose to join Siberia to Alaska — the bridge called Beringia — and to knit New Guinea to Australia.
--- fact 4
In some lands, human peoples withdrew into refuges; in others, they bent and endured.
--- context
This age of ice sits within the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]], just before the great thaw that opens the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]], and its cold shaped the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]].
--- observer
The Observer reads this as the species' bottleneck: the coldest, driest chapter of its history, arriving just before the warm stability that let farming and cities take root.
--- question 1
By what means did human folk endure that long winter, region by region?

=== event/late-devonian-extinction
title: Late Devonian Extinction
src: 73a4be58332e
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Late Devonian extinction was no single blow. Over a long, grinding stretch — about 372 to 359 million years ago — life in the seas thinned out. The reef builders took the worst of it.
--- fact 1
Two pulses, at least. Kellwasser first. Hangenberg after.
--- fact 2
Around 75 percent of species, gone. That is the estimate.
--- fact 3
Proposed causes: ocean anoxia. Climate change. And land plants, newly spread, changing what fed the water.
--- context
This belongs to the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]]. It comes after the forests rose. See [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]].
--- observer
The Observer treats this as a caution: the spread of one group, land plants, stands as a candidate cause of the extinction. Success, left unchecked, can destabilize the very architecture that supports it.
--- question 1
How much of this did the plants cause?

=== event/late-heavy-bombardment
title: Late Heavy Bombardment
src: 52d8480da0b5
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Late Heavy Bombardment: a hypothesized spike, asteroids battering the inner Solar System, roughly 4.1 to 3.8 billion years ago. The evidence comes mostly from lunar rock — radiometric ages, read like scars.
--- fact 1
The hypothesis started with Apollo-era rock: impact-melt ages, clustering near 3.8 to 3.9 billion years. A pattern in the numbers.
--- fact 2
Some researchers argue otherwise: the cluster is sampling bias, a few huge basin impacts, not a true surge across the whole Solar System.
--- fact 3
The Moon kept its scars. Earth did not — its earliest crust ground away by plate tectonics, worn down by erosion.
--- fact 4
Some models — the Nice model among them — propose that a shift in the giant planets' orbits could have triggered such a surge.
--- fact 5
Whether it happened at all. Whether it would have sterilized early life. Both remain debated.
--- context
If it truly happened, the Late Heavy Bombardment falls at the close of the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]], overlapping the earliest evidence for life — which bears on the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]]. It follows the [[event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon|Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon]] and the [[event/formation-of-the-solar-system-and-earth|Formation of the Solar System and Earth]]. It sits within the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]], across the [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]] region, in the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Earth's first chapter survives mostly as another world's scars: the Moon holds the record that Earth's own architecture erased. Here is a history-keeping species piecing together its planet's beginning from its neighbor's surface. The reading would weaken if that impact-age cluster proved to be a sampling artifact.
--- question 1
Was this a sudden spike, or only a long, declining tail of accretion?
--- question 2
Could life have survived the peak of it, or even arisen there?

=== event/late-ordovician-extinction
title: Late Ordovician Extinction
src: 7c5ff1ddff67
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Late Ordovician extinction — about 445 to 443 million years ago — was first. First of the 'Big Five.' It erased an estimated 85 to 86 percent of species, most of them from the sea.
--- fact 1
Two pulses. That is how it came.
--- fact 2
Glaciation. Falling seas. These lead the list of proposed causes. Then rising seas again, and ocean anoxia close behind.
--- fact 3
The sea took the worst of it. Almost everything still lived there, back then.
--- context
This is one of the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]]'s extinctions — one of the five, the 'Big Five.'
--- observer
The Observer counts mass extinctions among the planet's oldest editing processes: each one strips away most of a biosphere, and the survivors inherit the room to expand.
--- question 1
What mix of forces caused the two pulses, stacked one after the other?

=== event/latin-american-wars-of-independence
title: Latin American Wars of Independence
src: ee949a542aa1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between about 1808 and 1826, the Latin American wars of independence dismantled Spanish rule across most of the Americas and raised in its place a series of new republics.
--- fact 1
These wars followed Napoleon's invasion of Spain in 1808, an event that weakened Spanish authority and set off a crisis across the empire — though it was not their only cause.
--- fact 2
Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín met at Guayaquil on 26 and 27 July 1822. On how the new states should be governed, the two could not agree, and San Martín then withdrew from the campaigns.
--- fact 3
At Ayacucho, on 9 December 1824, Antonio José de Sucre's victory ended effective Spanish resistance in South America.
--- fact 4
Bolívar's Gran Colombia was proclaimed in December 1819, spanning what is now Venezuela, Colombia, Panama, and Ecuador. About a decade later, in 1830 and 1831, it dissolved into Venezuela, Ecuador, and New Granada.
--- context
These wars belong to the wider [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]], with the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]] as an earlier instance of the same pattern. See [[person/simon-bolivar|Simón Bolívar]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that independence in Latin America was won largely by colonial elites, and that much of the colonial era's architecture of hierarchy outlasted the mere change of flags.
--- question 1
How far did independence actually change the position of Indigenous people and of those enslaved?

=== event/manila-galleon-trade
title: Manila Galleon Trade
src: 04da3121c350
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
From 1565 to 1815, Spanish galleons crossed each year between Manila and Acapulco, carrying Chinese goods to the Americas and American silver to Asia — the first regular trans-Pacific trade the world had seen.
--- fact 1
It was Andres de Urdaneta who found the return route across the North Pacific, in 1565.
--- fact 2
At Manila, the exchange happened: Chinese merchants sold silk and porcelain there, for silver.
--- fact 3
Silver flowed to China from the Americas — much of it out of Potosi.
--- fact 4
From Acapulco the goods went overland across Mexico, then were reshipped on to Spain.
--- fact 5
When Mexico won its independence, the trade came to an end.
--- context
This route tied the economy of the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] to Spanish American silver out of [[place/potosi|Potosi]], part of the wider [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region of [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], within the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a metal, dug from mountains in the Andes, carried clear across an ocean to pay for silk in China — tying together two peoples who never once dealt with each other directly. The exchange-web, by then, had become planetary.
--- question 1
How much of Ming China's silver actually came from the Americas?
