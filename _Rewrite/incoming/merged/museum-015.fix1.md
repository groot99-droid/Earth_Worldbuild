--- description
I stand in the room of Georges Méliès (1861 to 1938), the Paris stage magician who became a filmmaker and invented cinema's first special effects, who over roughly 500 films in under twenty years, including A Trip to the Moon (1902), turned the new camera from a device for recording reality into a machine for staging the impossible.

I read that he was born in Paris to a prosperous shoe-manufacturing family and wanted to study painting, but his father would not support him as an artist, and he supervised machinery at the family factory. I read that in about 1888 he bought the Théâtre Robert-Houdin, a famous conjuring theatre. I read that after the Lumière brothers' first public film screening in December 1895 he tried to buy a camera from them and was refused, that he acquired one from a British inventor, and that he built a studio at Montreuil with a glass-walled stage.

About 1896 he discovered, by accident when his camera jammed mid-shot, that stopping and restarting filming could make objects vanish or transform: the "stop trick." His techniques included stop-motion substitution, multiple exposure, dissolves, painted sets built to force perspective, and hand-tinted colour done frame by frame by a team of women colourists.

American piracy of his films, the rise of narrative studio filmmaking and the disruption of the First World War ruined his business. He was reduced to running a toy stall in a Paris train station.

The man who filmed a trip to the moon, selling toys in a station. Where does wonder go when the money leaves? Into small hands, perhaps.
