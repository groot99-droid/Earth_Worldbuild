=== person/gustav-klimt
title: Gustav Klimt
type: person
date: 1862 – 1918
themes: art
src: f33e1f2be85a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Gustav Klimt (14 July 1862 to 6 February 1918) led the Vienna Secession as an Austrian painter, and his covenant with ornament — gold, pattern, women, love — made *The Kiss* above all an icon of turn-of-the-century Vienna.
--- fact 1
Klimt was born at Baumgarten near Vienna, the second of seven children of a gold engraver. A scholarship took him to the Vienna School of Arts and Crafts in 1876, and with his brother Ernst and with Franz Matsch he painted murals and ceilings for the Burgtheater and the Kunsthistorisches Museum.
--- fact 2
In 1897 he became the first president of the Vienna Secession, a group that broke from the official academy under the motto "To every age its art, to art its freedom".
--- fact 3
His ceiling paintings for the University of Vienna, showing Philosophy, Medicine and Jurisprudence, drew scandal over their nudity and pessimism. He withdrew from the commission, and fire destroyed the paintings in 1945.
--- fact 4
He never married. A lifelong companionship bound him to the designer Emilie Flöge, and he fathered several children with his models. He supported younger artists such as Egon Schiele and Oskar Kokoschka, and died in Vienna after a stroke.
--- fact 5
In his "golden period", roughly 1899 to 1910, he applied real gold and silver leaf, drawing on Byzantine mosaics he saw in Ravenna in 1903, and on Japanese and Egyptian art. Key works from these years include The Kiss (1907 to 1908) and the Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I (1907).
--- fact 6
Many of his works belonged to Jewish collectors before the Nazis looted them. The return of the Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I to Maria Altmann made headlines worldwide, and Ronald Lauder bought it in 2006 for a reported $135 million.
--- fact 7
In 2023 Lady with a Fan sold for £85.3 million, a record for a painting sold at auction in Europe.
--- context
Klimt worked in Vienna, within [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and died amid the [[event/1918-influenza-pandemic|1918 Influenza Pandemic]]. Works he had painted were later looted in the [[event/holocaust|Holocaust]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I see a motto that turned art into a claim on institutions, and I see a painter who walked away from a commission the moment it drew scandal — a university refusing images of its own faculties. I see the gold leaf, and I see the auction record beside it: the object's shine and its price are one signal, carried to an extreme.

What does a painting carry, if not the story of every hand it has passed through? I see provenance as a second biography — paintings taken from Jewish owners by the Nazis, later returned through restitution — a sanctioned harm that never leaves the frame. A picture's history does not end at the frame's edge.
--- question 1
How many Klimt works taken from Jewish owners have yet to be restituted?

=== person/hammurabi
title: Hammurabi
type: person
date: c. 1810 – 1750 BCE
themes: war, language
src: 77571e1dd3f3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hammurabi ruled Babylon as king from about 1792 to 1750 BCE. His reign united much of Mesopotamia and left a famous inscribed law code as its lasting record.
--- fact 1
Carved into a stone stele about 1754 BCE, the Code of Hammurabi lists 282 laws.
--- fact 2
Its penalties vary by social class, built on the principle of proportionate punishment.
--- fact 3
Displayed in public, the stele turned law into something visible, a threshold of authority anyone could stand before.
--- fact 4
The original stele now stands in the Louvre, in Paris.
--- context
Hammurabi ruled in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], inheriting a written tradition the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] had begun.
--- observer
The Observer reads this as a state converting custom into codex: a body of unwritten habit turned into text anyone could point to. Carving law into stone changed what authority could claim: a rule that, at least in principle, everyone can read can bind the ruler as much as the ruled. A law you cannot see is a law you can only be told about; a law on stone is a law you can check.
--- question 1
How closely did courts follow the code in practice?

=== person/harald-hardrada
title: Harald Hardrada
type: person
date: c. 1015 – 1066
themes: war, trade
src: b08bc4d83f84
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Harald Hardrada, born about 1015 and killed in 1066, ruled Norway as king from 1046 to 1066. His path ran from exile through mercenary service in Byzantium to the throne, and his death at Stamford Bridge is often taken as marking the close of the Viking Age.
--- fact 1
After his half-brother King Olaf II fell in battle in 1030, the teenage Harald fled Norway and went on to command in the Byzantine Empire's elite Varangian Guard, campaigning across the Mediterranean and the Middle East.
--- fact 2
He returned to Scandinavia around 1045 with considerable wealth, secured a share of the Norwegian throne in 1046, and became sole king after his co-ruler's death in 1047.
--- fact 3
Years of inconclusive war against Denmark over rival claims followed, ending in peace in 1064, or 1065 according to Morkinskinna, without the Danish throne he had sought.
--- fact 4
In September 1066 he invaded England to press a rival claim to its throne, but King Harold Godwinson defeated and killed him at the Battle of Stamford Bridge, days before Godwinson had to march south to meet William of Normandy's invasion at Hastings.
--- context
As King of Norway, Harald Hardrada led the [[culture/norse|Norse]] in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer treats Harald's failed invasion as more than a footnote to the Norman Conquest. It forced Harold Godwinson's army into an exhausting march the length of England, a strain that arguably weakened it before Hastings; the defeat at Stamford Bridge may have shaped the battle that followed more than the traditional label of mere prelude suggests. A footnote can still bend the sentence that comes after it.
--- question 1
How much did the losses and exhaustion the English army suffered at Stamford Bridge actually affect its performance three weeks later at Hastings?
