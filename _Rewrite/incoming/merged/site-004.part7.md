=== place/ayutthaya
src: 7a3d8af6a0df
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ayutthaya is a city on an island at the meeting of three rivers in central Thailand, traditionally founded in 1350, where the waters braid together like the strands of a rope. It was the capital of the Ayutthaya Kingdom until the Burmese army destroyed it in 1767, and a major trading port of Southeast Asia.
--- fact 1
Ayutthaya lies on an island held in the arms of the Chao Phraya, Lopburi and Pa Sak rivers where they meet, and is traditionally said to have been founded in 1350 by King U Thong (Ramathibodi I).
--- fact 2
Ayutthaya's forces took Angkor in 1431, and afterward the Khmer court moved away from Angkor, leaving the old stone city behind.
--- fact 3
Chinese, Japanese, Persian, Indian and Malay merchants traded at Ayutthaya, a harbor of many tongues; the Portuguese, in 1511, were the first Europeans to reach it, the Dutch East India Company opened a trading post in 1608, and King Narai exchanged embassies with the French court in the 1680s.
--- fact 4
European visitors in the 17th century described a city of canals, palaces and many temples, a city threaded with water, and some estimates put its population at several hundred thousand at its height.
--- fact 5
In April 1767, after a siege of about 14 months, a Burmese army took Ayutthaya and burned much of the city; many thousands of people were carried off as captives to Burma, and much of the kingdom's written record was lost with it.
--- fact 6
Taksin raised a Siamese state again after 1767, and the capital moved to Thonburi and then, in 1782, to Bangkok.
--- fact 7
In 1991 UNESCO inscribed the Historic City of Ayutthaya.
--- context
Ayutthaya was the capital of the [[culture/ayutthaya-kingdom|Ayutthaya Kingdom]], which took over the region of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] and [[place/angkor|Angkor]] as one tide follows another, and it traded with the [[event/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and its threads run through the themes of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
Think of a city where the same water was your wall, your street and your market. The Observer notes that Ayutthaya was such a trading capital, built on an island where rivers and canals served as moat, road and marketplace at once, and where merchants of many origins met under one ruler. Then the destruction of 1767 removed much of the kingdom's own written record. So a large part of what is known about it comes from foreign visitors' accounts, a loss of memory-outsourcing that shapes how the city is understood, as if a family's story survived only in the letters of its guests. The Observer records the taking of captives as human suffering as well as a loss of skill and labor. The reading would weaken if surviving Thai and Burmese records prove richer than assumed. What a city writes about itself is part of its architecture; lose it, and others draw the plans.
--- question 1
How many people lived in Ayutthaya at its height?
--- question 2
How much of Ayutthaya's own written record survived 1767, and where is it held now?

=== place/babylon
src: c15ab030847f
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Babylon was a city on the Euphrates River in present-day Iraq, first named in the late 3rd millennium BCE. It became the capital of Hammurabi's kingdom and later of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, and a center of astronomy, law and religion, where the sky was watched and the law was set in stone.
--- fact 1
Babylon lies near Hillah, about 85 kilometers south of Baghdad, and it first appears in a tablet from the late 3rd millennium BCE, a name surfacing out of deep time.
--- fact 2
Babylon rose under Hammurabi (reigned about 1792 to 1750 BCE), whose law code was inscribed on a stele later found at Susa and now held in the Louvre.
--- fact 3
About 1595 BCE the Hittite king Mursili I sacked Babylon, and the Kassites then ruled it for centuries; in 689 BCE the Assyrian king Sennacherib destroyed it, and his son Esarhaddon raised it again.
--- fact 4
Under Nebuchadnezzar II (reigned 605 to 562 BCE) Babylon had the Ishtar Gate, a great ziggurat called Etemenanki, and palaces, and his armies destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BCE.
--- fact 5
Cyrus the Great of Persia took Babylon in 539 BCE; the Cyrus Cylinder, a clay text found there in 1879, presents him as chosen by the god Marduk, and claims that it is a charter of human rights are debated.
--- fact 6
Alexander the Great died at Babylon in 323 BCE; the city faded after the Seleucids founded Seleucia on the Tigris.
--- fact 7
Babylonian scribes kept the Astronomical Diaries from about 652 to 61 BCE, a long vigil over the heavens and one of the longest continuous records of celestial observation.
--- fact 8
The Hanging Gardens described by Greek writers have been confirmed by no Babylonian text and no archaeological trace, a garden seen only through foreign eyes; some scholars have proposed that they were at Nineveh.
--- fact 9
Military bases of US-led forces at the site in 2003 to 2004 damaged ancient remains, and UNESCO inscribed Babylon in 2019.
--- context
Babylon's rulers fought the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] and destroyed [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]] in 586 BCE, and the city passed to the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]] and then to [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and its threads run through the themes of [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
You may think you know Babylon. The Observer notes that it exists in memory in three forms that do not match: the excavated city; the scriptural symbol of exile and pride; and the source of a long astronomical record kept by scribes, an early case of memory-outsourcing in service of pattern-taming. Three cities, one name. The Hanging Gardens show a story outliving its evidence, since a wonder described by later writers leaves no trace in the city's own records. Think of a famous room that the house's own floor plans never mention. The reading would weaken if excavation at Babylon or Nineveh finds the gardens. A name can carry more than its ground can prove.
--- question 1
Where were the Hanging Gardens, if they existed at all?
--- question 2
