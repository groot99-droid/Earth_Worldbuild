=== teatro-olimpico
src: 095ef74be363
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Every architecture of illusion begins as an architecture of study. This was Palladio's last design, a covered theatre modelled on his study of ancient Roman precedent, and he did not live to see it done: Scamozzi completed it after his death. Now look behind the stage arch. Streets built in forced perspective recede at a scale that makes them appear far longer than the shallow space allows. Said plainly, a shallow stage, disciplined by perspective, can hold whole streets. The illusion is still in use here, at the world's oldest surviving indoor theatre in continuous use. Some thresholds are deeper than the rooms behind them.

=== san-giorgio-maggiore
src: 36ce406cbd0a
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Every building carries an awkward truth; the art is in how gracefully it is carried. Here is a monastery church on its own island, across the water from St Mark's Square. A basilica's profile is awkward: a tall nave, lower side aisles. Palladio's answer was a bright white facade in which two classical temple fronts of different heights overlap to mask that profile. The facade is a grammar of concealment that reads as candour. It was a problem he solved more elegantly here than any architect before him. Elegance is a difficulty that has been listened to.

=== il-redentore
src: ebff2359a3f8
mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Some buildings are promises kept in stone. This votive church was commissioned by the Venetian Senate after a plague that killed roughly a third of the city's population, and the covenant has not lapsed: every July it is still visited in a procession of thanksgiving, carried across the water on a footbridge. Step inside to meet the vow's quieter side. Its luminous white facade and centrally planned interior, lit from hidden windows, are considered among the calmest and most resolved of all Palladio's church designs. After loss a city asked for a building; the building answered with calm. Gratitude, too, needs an architecture.

=== netherlandish-proverbs
src: d2dc456ab8aa
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Here was a village where speech had put on flesh. More than 100 sayings walked its lanes, each one taken literally, as if the proverbs of the land had been loosed like starlings and had settled into bodies. A man banged his head against a brick wall. Another filled in the well after the calf had drowned. A woman tied the devil to a pillow. The painting is both a comic encyclopedia of folly, a codex of every foolishness the tongue has named, and a portrait of a world turned upside down, its sky spilled at its feet like water from an upended bowl.

=== childrens-games
src: b40d0f321e26
mode: confessional-poetry
--- description
I count some 230 children, about 80 games, and I lose count, as I did as a child. Leapfrog, hoops, stilts. Blind man's buff, spinning tops. Children playing at weddings, children playing at processions, filling a whole town square. From above, as the painting shows them, they are a remarkable record of 16th-century play.

I remember the rules we swore by. I remember how gravely we played at being grown. Is this a record, or a mirror? It may also carry a moral: that adult affairs are just as foolish as a child's games. We never stop playing; we only stop admitting it.

=== tower-of-babel
src: e4389afa46f0
mode: horror-prose
--- description
A tower. Colossal. Spiralling up above a Flemish harbour city, modelled on Rome's Colosseum. In the foreground King Nimrod inspects the work. Around him, hundreds of tiny labourers. Hauling stone. Working the cranes. Building scaffolding, level on level.

Look again. The tower is already leaning. The lower levels are crumbling. The top is not yet finished, and the bottom is already coming apart. A warning about human pride.

And still they haul the stone.

=== hunters-in-the-snow
src: b88d78de6969
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Three tired hunters came home over the hill with their dogs at their heels, and the whole harvest of their day was a single fox. Below them the valley lay locked in winter under a green-grey sky, pale as old ice over deep water, and on the frozen ponds the villagers skated, small and dark as seeds scattered across snow. The panel belongs to a series of six paintings of the seasons, made for the Antwerp merchant Nicolaes Jonghelinck, a wheel of the year turned in oil. Of all the winters that art has kept, this is one of the most beloved.

=== the-harvesters
src: d4acc2bd18e1
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Another of the seasons series, and here the year stood in late summer, heavy and gold. Peasants scythed the golden wheat and bound it into bundles, their blades flashing like light on water. Others rested beneath a pear tree to eat bread, porridge and cheese, and one lay sprawled asleep, as if the heat had pressed him into the earth. Beyond them the land ran on to a hazy bay. No god descends here, and no hero rides through. It is one of the first great paintings of ordinary working life, with no religious or mythological story attached: only the field, the meal and the long light.

=== peasant-wedding
src: cc652daa8c50
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
In a barn the feast was laid, and the food came in on a door. Two men carried bowls of porridge upon it, the door lifted from its hinges for the feast, while a piper watched the food with hungry eyes and a child licked a plate as clean as a moon. The bride sat beneath a paper crown before a green cloth, still as a lantern amid the din. Which guest is the groom is still debated, a riddle the painting keeps. Every corner brims with affectionate, comic detail, and the painting is one of the reasons for Bruegel's nickname, "Peasant Bruegel".

=== the-chess-game
src: 7c558be0a4ce
mode: confessional-poetry
--- description
Three of Sofonisba's sisters, Lucia, Minerva and Europa, play chess outdoors while a servant looks on, and I find myself counting my own. Lucia has just made the winning move, and she glances out at us. Minerva raises a hand in protest. Little Europa grins.

I remember the table. I remember the protest. I remember the grin. Chess was a game of intellect, so the painting quietly shows off the family's education. Is that pride, or love, or both? It brings unusual warmth and spontaneity to a group portrait. We are shaped by what we learn, and by who we learn it beside.

=== self-portrait-at-the-easel
src: 2b8729f01dac
mode: confessional-poetry
--- description
She looks straight at me, brush in hand, and I am the one who looks away first. Anguissola paints herself at work on an image of the Virgin and Child. At a time when women were rarely allowed to train as artists, the self-portrait is a statement of her professional identity. I read it as a triad: the work, the hand, the name. She painted more self-portraits than any other artist between Dürer and Rembrandt. How many times must you say who you are before the world writes it down? Some of us have to paint ourselves into the record.

=== family-portrait
src: 143882b79c6c
mode: confessional-poetry
--- description
A father sits before a landscape with his only son, Asdrubale, and his daughter Minerva, and I think of my own father's hands. The boy clutches a small dog. The relaxed, affectionate poses were unusual for a formal family portrait, and I feel it before I can name it. This is the artist's father. Amilcare had championed his daughters' education. He sent Sofonisba's work to Michelangelo. What is love, if not sending someone's work out ahead of them? Some love is a door held open.
