=== culture/champa
title: Champa
src: c98553fb0ad6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Champa was a group of Cham states along the coast of central and southern Vietnam, dated from 192 CE, that built Hindu temples at My Son and sent its trade across the South China Sea like gulls over the water. Little by little it was conquered by Vietnam, until its last remnant was annexed in 1832.
--- fact 1
Champa is usually dated from 192 CE, when the Cham clans of central and southern Vietnam were gathered into a single state, many streams joined in one river. In later ages the kingdom broke into separate principalities, as a river frays into a delta.
--- fact 2
Hinduism, probably carried by Indian merchants, was taken up early and blended with local traditions and with Buddhist, Khmer and Chinese influences, as rivers mingle in one sea. By the end of the 10th century Muslim traders had brought Islam to its shores.
--- fact 3
My Son is a group of Shaiva Hindu temples in central Vietnam, raised by Champa's kings between the 4th and 13th centuries, like prayers given lasting form.
--- fact 4
Vijaya stood as the capital from 938 until 1471, when the Vietnamese ruler Le Thanh Tong (Lê Thánh Tông) took it and annexed most of the country. One account records 40,000 Chams killed and 30,000 taken prisoner, though these numbers are not independently confirmed.
--- fact 5
The remaining principality of Panduranga held on like a last ember until 1832, when Emperor Minh Mang began the final conquest and annexed the last of Champa.
--- context
Champa stood as neighbour and rival to the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] and its temples at [[place/angkor|Angkor]], and its ships took part in the sea trade of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]].
--- observer
What does it take to make a borrowed faith your own? The Observer notes a people who took a meaning-engine from across the sea and made it local, and who were then absorbed, over centuries, by a neighbour to the north. Hold both parts of that story at once. The loss of 1471 is recorded as harm: a capital taken, people killed or carried off, and a state reduced to a remnant that lasted another three and a half centuries. A people can make a faith its own and still lose its state; the ledger should record both.
--- question 1
How far can the reported casualty figures for the fall of Vijaya be trusted?
--- question 2
How did the Cham communities that survive today (Hindu, Muslim, Buddhist) keep their identity alive after annexation?

=== culture/chinchorro-culture
title: Chinchorro Culture
src: 438f74ed152a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Chinchorro were fisher-hunter-gatherers of the Atacama Desert coast, in what is now northern Chile and southern Peru, known for the earliest deliberate artificial mummification, which began by about 5050 BCE on a shore between desert and sea.
--- fact 1
From about 5450 to 890 BCE the Chinchorro made their lives on the coast, between the dry land and the endless water.
--- fact 2
They preserved men, women and children of the community, not only an elite, rebuilding the bodies with clay, wigs and mineral pigments, the dead remade by patient hands.
--- fact 3
The tradition endured about 3,000 years, a long vigil ending about 1800 BCE.
--- fact 4
In 2021 UNESCO inscribed the settlement and mummification sites as a World Heritage Site, the old shore entered into the world's memory.
--- context
The Chinchorro's practice is the earliest known artificial mummification, thousands of years before the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]]. Their tale belongs beside [[observer-note/why-the-species-buries-its-dead|Why the Species Buries Its Dead]] and the lands of the [[region/andes|Andes]].
--- observer
I think of a shore with no farms and no cities. I think of coastal foragers there, preserving their dead, their children too. I think of all the work that keeping them took. Who needs a state to mourn? The grief-rite is older than the state.
--- question 1
What moved the Chinchorro to begin preserving their dead?

=== culture/chola-dynasty
title: Chola Dynasty
src: 988c2c90eb99
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Chola dynasty ruled much of southern India from around 850 to 1279 CE, a naval power whose ships traded and fought across the Bay of Bengal like hawks ranging over a wide field.
--- fact 1
Around 850 CE Vijayalaya Chola took Thanjavur, and the dynasty's star began to rise.
--- fact 2
Rajaraja I (r. 985 to 1014) raised the Brihadisvara Temple in Thanjavur, completed around 1010, a mountain of worship now named a UNESCO World Heritage site.
--- fact 3
Around 1025 Rajendra I sent a naval expedition across the waters against Srivijaya.
--- fact 4
The Cholas traded with China and Southeast Asia, their sails reaching toward far shores.
--- fact 5
Chola bronzes, among them the dancing Shiva (Nataraja), are celebrated works of art, metal made to move like flame.
--- context
The Cholas stood among the strongest states of the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]], contending with [[culture/srivijaya|Srivijaya]] for control of the sea lanes. In the codex the dynasty stands in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region of [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and three threads run through it: [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
We tend to sort states into those that face the land and those that face the sea. The Observer notes that an inland-looking temple-building state used its fleet to project power across an ocean. Its long-distance raid was unusual for its time, and it shows that maritime strength was not limited to coastal traders. A state's architecture need not decide its horizon.
--- question 1
What purpose drove the Chola expedition to Srivijaya?

=== culture/delhi-sultanate
title: Delhi Sultanate
src: 168257290d8a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Delhi Sultanate was a succession of five Muslim dynasties that ruled much of northern India from 1206 until Babur's victory at Panipat in 1526, crowns passing from hand to hand like torches in a relay.
--- fact 1
It rose in the wake of the conquests of Muhammad of Ghor, and Qutb al-Din Aibak became the first sultan.
--- fact 2
The five dynasties were the Mamluk, Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid and Lodi, names strung on one long thread of rule.
--- fact 3
In the 13th century it resisted several Mongol invasions, standing like a cliff against the storm.
--- fact 4
In 1398 Timur sacked Delhi, a dark year in the city's long memory.
--- fact 5
After the First Battle of Panipat in 1526 the Mughal Empire took its place, and the sultans' long day was done.
--- context
The sultanate bound northern India to the wider Islamic world like a bridge thrown over a river; it faced the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]], suffered the raid of [[person/timur|Timur]], and was followed by the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]]. In the codex it stands in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region of [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and its threads are [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
Ask what makes a ruler legitimate, and most of us reach for bloodline first. The Observer notes that in this era a state could be led by former slave soldiers and still survive Mongol invasions and repeated dynastic changes. Here, legitimacy appears to rest more on military competence and patronage than on ancestry. A crown can be earned as well as inherited.
--- question 1
How did Delhi's rulers govern a population that was largely non-Muslim?
