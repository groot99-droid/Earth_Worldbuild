=== place/athens
src: e5d1a535c181
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Athens is a Greek city that, in the 5th century BCE, rose to become the leading city of the Greek world, a lamp for its age, with an early form of direct democracy and great achievements in philosophy, drama and art.
--- fact 1
Cleisthenes' reforms of about 508 BCE laid down democratic institutions, the first stones of a new house.
--- fact 2
The Parthenon was raised between 447 and 432 BCE, a crown for the city.
--- fact 3
Socrates, Plato and the tragedians worked in Athens, many voices gathered in one city.
--- fact 4
In the Peloponnesian War (431 to 404 BCE), Athens met defeat at the hands of Sparta.
--- context
Athens was the hearth of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]]' intellectual world and the home ground of [[person/aristotle|Aristotle]]'s Lyceum.
--- observer
Size is not the measure of influence. The Observer notes a small city that produced a disproportionate share of the ideas later taken as foundational. Think of a crowded room where every argument has to meet another before it can leave. Density of argument seems to matter. Ideas sharpen fastest where they collide.
--- question 1
How representative was Athens of Greek city-states, one lamp among many?

=== place/bagan
src: ba2e612a5a66
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Bagan is an archaeological zone on the Irrawaddy River in central Myanmar, a plain crowded with thousands of Buddhist monuments like a field of stone flowers. It was the capital of the Pagan Kingdom from a traditional founding date of 849 CE until the kingdom's collapse in the late 13th century.
--- fact 1
Bagan lies on the Irrawaddy River in central Myanmar; tradition dates its founding to 849 CE, and it flourished from the 11th to the 13th century, its long noon.
--- fact 2
King Anawrahta (reigned 1044 to 1077) widened the kingdom and raised Theravada Buddhism to favor.
--- fact 3
More than 10,000 temples, pagodas and monasteries are estimated to have been built on the plain, a forest of spires, and about 2,200 still stand.
--- fact 4
Many monuments were funded by private donors as well as kings, and inscriptions record gifts of land, labor and goods flowing to the temples like rivers to the sea; some historians argue that tax-exempt land grants to religious institutions reduced state revenue, a view that is debated.
--- fact 5
Mongol armies defeated Pagan forces at Ngasaunggyan in 1277 and invaded again in 1287, after which the Pagan Kingdom collapsed; yet Bagan remained a religious center, its lamps still lit.
--- fact 6
The ground itself has turned against the monuments: earthquakes have damaged them, including one in 2016 that damaged hundreds, and UNESCO inscribed Bagan in 2019.
--- context
Bagan was the capital of the [[culture/pagan-kingdom|Pagan Kingdom]], which fell after the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]] reached Southeast Asia, and it rose in the same age as the temple-building of [[place/angkor|Angkor]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and its threads run through the themes of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
Walk out onto that plain and count the spires, and you are reading a ledger. The Observer notes that Bagan is the result of merit-making at scale: donors turned wealth into religious standing by building temples, and the sheer number of monuments, far beyond what kings alone would need, is a status-signal spread across a whole class of patrons. The proposal that land given to temples weakened the state suggests that a meaning-engine can also drain the institution that supports it, though historians debate the proposal. The reading would weaken if the record shows that temple land was too small a share of the kingdom's land to matter for its revenue. Devotion has an architecture, and every architecture has a cost.
--- question 1
How much of the Pagan Kingdom's land passed into the keeping of religious institutions?
--- question 2
How many souls dwelt at Bagan in its 13th-century peak?

=== place/baghdad
src: c5e5ba94f248
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Baghdad is a city on the Tigris River in Iraq, founded in 762 CE by the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur as a round capital, a circle drawn upon the land. For about five centuries it was the seat of the Abbasid caliphs and a leading center of learning, a lamp that drew scholars to it.
--- fact 1
Baghdad was founded in 762 by the caliph al-Mansur as a round city called Madinat al-Salam ("City of Peace"), near the older Sasanian capital Ctesiphon; no part of the Round City survives above ground, and the circle lives on only in words.
--- fact 2
Baghdad became the hearth of the Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom, and paper mills were at work there by the 790s.
--- fact 3
Population estimates for Baghdad in the 9th and 10th centuries run from several hundred thousand to over a million, a multitude that would have made it one of the largest cities in the world.
--- fact 4
Baghdad remained the seat of the Abbasid caliphs until 1258, when Mongol forces under Hulagu took the city after a siege and killed the caliph al-Musta'sim. Chronicles report 200,000 to a million dead, figures that modern historians regard as too high, though the massacre and destruction were severe.
--- fact 5
Baghdad was under Ottoman rule from 1534, with Safavid periods before and after, until British forces captured it in 1917; in 1921 it became the capital of the Kingdom of Iraq.
--- fact 6
In April 2003, after the US-led invasion, looting of the Iraq National Museum removed about 15,000 objects, some of which have since found their way back.
--- context
Baghdad was the capital of the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] and the home of the [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]]; it was taken in the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]] and later ruled by the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and its threads run through the themes of [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
A plan can become a crossroads. The Observer notes that Baghdad was a planned city that became a knot in an exchange-web: books, translators, paper and scholars from several languages made it a place where Greek, Persian and Indian sources were gathered and rewritten, memory-outsourcing at scale. Think of a library that is also a workshop, where a text arrives in one tongue and leaves in another. The Round City itself is now known only from texts, because later buildings covered it, while the losses of 1258 and 2003 show that stored knowledge is exposed to political violence. Historians debate how far the court and how far private patrons drove the translation work, and the reading would weaken if private patronage proves to have been the larger force. What a city gathers, it must also guard.
--- question 1
How many people died in the Mongol sack of 1258, and how much of Baghdad's book collection was lost?
--- question 2
How large did Baghdad grow at its peak?
