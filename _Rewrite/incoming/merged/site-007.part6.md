=== place/mexico-city
src: 13c7cf919e20
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Mexico City is the capital of Mexico, built by the Spanish after 1521 on the site of Tenochtitlan in the former Lake Texcoco, and today one of the largest cities in the Americas. Think of it as a palimpsest: a capital written over the lines of another.
--- fact 1
After the conquest of 1521 the Spanish rebuilt the city on the ruins of Tenochtitlan, founding a municipality in 1524 as México Tenochtitlán, and it became the capital of the Viceroyalty of New Spain, established in 1535.
--- fact 2
The Metropolitan Cathedral, begun in the 1570s and completed in 1813, stands beside the main square, and the buried Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan came back to light in excavations after workers found the Coyolxauhqui stone in 1978.
--- fact 3
From the 17th century, in response to floods, the lakes were drained by canals and tunnels, and the city now covers much of the old lakebed; the ground keeps its own ledger of that choice, with parts of the city sunk as much as nine meters and some areas still sinking by nearly 50 centimeters a year.
--- fact 4
Silver and goods moved through the capital in the Spanish trade system, and the Manila galleons linked New Spain to Asia through the Pacific port of Acapulco.
--- fact 5
In September 1821 Mexican independence was secured, with Mexico City as the capital of the new state.
--- fact 6
The soft lakebed amplifies earthquakes, and the city has paid for that foundation: the quake of 19 September 1985 (magnitude about 8) killed about 10,000 people by official counts, with some estimates several times higher, and the quake of 19 September 2017 (magnitude 7.1) killed more than 300.
--- fact 7
The 2020 census counted 9,209,944 people in Mexico City and 21,804,515 in Greater Mexico City, and in 1987 UNESCO inscribed the Historic Centre and Xochimilco.
--- context
Mexico City succeeded [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] after the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]], and it was a hub of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] and the [[event/manila-galleon-trade|Manila Galleon Trade]]. It was the capital when independence came through the [[event/latin-american-wars-of-independence|Latin American Wars of Independence]]. You will find it in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Mexico City is a capital built directly on an earlier capital, with the Spanish cathedral and plaza placed near the old temple precinct: one claim-line drawn over another. The pull of the earlier city's site, its ancestor-weight, shaped the choice. Now consider the water. The Mexica lived on the lake through causeways and chinampas; the modern city sinks into the drained lakebed and is managed by pumping and drainage. A site chosen for power carries its hazards into later centuries, and that is the architecture of inheritance: you take the seat of power, and you take the ground beneath it. The reading would weaken if records show that the Spanish chose the site mainly for practical reasons of labor and roads and not for its symbolic value. What we build on, we also build with.
--- question 1
How much of Tenochtitlan's street plan and canals still lies beneath the historic center?
--- question 2
How are earthquakes and sinking ground changing where and how people live on the old lakebed?

=== place/mogao-caves
src: e6ecac4052a6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Mogao Caves are a honeycomb of Buddhist cave temples cut into a cliff near Dunhuang, in Gansu, China, begun by tradition in 366 CE and filled with murals and sculptures over about a thousand years, at a crossroads of the Silk Roads where many threads of travel met.
--- fact 1
The Mogao Caves lie near the oasis of Dunhuang at the desert's edge, where Silk Road routes met like rivers in a basin; Han China had established a commandery at Dunhuang in the late 2nd century BCE.
--- fact 2
By tradition the monk Yuezun dug the first cave in 366 CE, and the work went on into the 14th century; the cliff holds roughly 735 caves, about 490 of them bright with murals or painted sculptures, and the murals spread across about 45,000 square meters.
--- fact 3
The art carries Indian, Central Asian and Chinese influences like mingled streams, and donors, among them officials, merchants and local rulers, are painted into many caves.
--- fact 4
Dunhuang lay under Tibetan rule from about 786 to 848, was governed by a local regime from 848, and passed, like a lamp handed along in the dark, to the Tangut state of Western Xia in about 1036.
--- fact 5
The "Library Cave" (Cave 17), sealed around the early 11th century, slept until 1900, when Wang Yuanlu, a Daoist priest who guarded the caves, opened it; within lay tens of thousands of manuscripts in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Sogdian, Khotanese and other languages, among them a printed Diamond Sutra dated 868.
--- fact 6
Aurel Stein in 1907, Paul Pelliot in 1908 and others obtained large numbers of these manuscripts, now held in London, Paris and elsewhere; in China the removals are called theft, and by the acquiring institutions, purchases.
--- fact 7
In 1987 UNESCO inscribed the Mogao Caves, and since 1944 the Dunhuang Academy has conserved them, a long watch against dust and time.
--- context
The Mogao Caves grew like a vine along the trade of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], and they held documents written by [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] and others. They stand in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and they are woven into the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I remember that the record of a caravan crossroads survived because a room was sealed. I remember what the dry air kept: contracts, letters, school exercises and Buddhist texts in many scripts. I remember that this rare memory-outsourcing preserved ordinary life, and not only palace records.

I see donors painted into the murals beside sacred figures, status-signal and devotion joined in one image. I see the manuscripts scattered across several countries, the record of one place become the property of many claim-lines. Who owns a memory, once it has been opened?

Why the room was sealed is debated, and my reading would weaken if it proves to have been routine disposal of worn-out texts and not a deliberate act of preservation. Even what we throw away can outlast us.
--- question 1

--- question 2
