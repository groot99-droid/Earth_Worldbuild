=== species/llama-and-alpaca
title: Llama and Alpaca
src: 1e75b200b8e6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The llama and the alpaca are South American camelids domesticated in the high Andes of Peru about 6,000 years ago from the guanaco and the vicuña, and they were the Inca Empire's only beasts of burden and a main source of fiber, the patient carriers of a mountain realm.
--- fact 1
DNA analysis has confirmed the old lineages: the guanaco is the wild ancestor of the llama, and the vicuña is the wild ancestor of the alpaca.
--- fact 2
The earliest evidence of domestication comes from high-altitude puna sites in the Peruvian Andes between about 4,000 and 4,900 meters, up where the air runs thin; at Telarmachay, about 170 kilometers northeast of Lima, animal remains suggest that early domestic llamas and alpacas were under human control by about 6,000 to 5,500 years ago and that a herding economy was established by about 5,500 years ago.
--- fact 3
Llamas were the only beasts of burden in the Inca Empire, and they gave meat and fiber besides; llama figures and llamas themselves were laid in burials as offerings, and one scholar argues that widespread Andean farming rested on llama dung as fertilizer.
--- fact 4
Alpaca fiber is much finer than llama fiber, a thread spun of mist: about 10 to 15 micrometers in diameter against 20 to 40 micrometers.
--- fact 5
A colonial-era writer estimated that as many as 300,000 llamas were employed hauling cargo from the mines of Potosí alone, a river of backs; but after Europeans introduced horses, mules and donkeys, the llama's importance as a beast of burden greatly diminished.
--- fact 6
More than seven million llamas and alpacas still grazed South America in 2007.
--- context
Llamas carried goods along the roads of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] between [[place/cusco|Cusco]] and its provinces, grazed the highland pastures near [[place/lake-titicaca|Lake Titicaca]], and hauled silver from the mines of [[place/potosi|Potosi]] until European pack animals came through the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. The llama and the alpaca stand in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and their tracks run through the threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I think of a state whose only beasts of burden were llamas. I think of roads and storehouses built for animals that carry a limited load, that must be rested, that must be fed. I think of the tool-lineage of Inca transport, shaped by the animal it had and not by the wheel or the horse.

What does a road remember of the feet it was made for? After 1532 the exchange-web replaced this carrier with imported animals, so the Spanish conquest changed not only who ruled the routes but what moved along them. The reading would weaken if excavation shows that human porters carried far more of the Inca Empire's cargo than llama caravans did. Until then I hold to this: every road is a portrait of what walked it.
--- question 1
How large were the llama caravans of the Inca Empire, and how did the herds wind between highland pastures and the coast?
--- question 2
Did the Spanish conquest and the coming of horses and mules cause the numbers of llamas and alpacas to fall, and if so, by how much?

=== species/pikaia
title: Pikaia
src: 2912aa164902
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pikaia is a small, eel-like swimming animal from the Burgess Shale of British Columbia, about 505 million years old, a ribbon of life that once flickered through Cambrian water. Reinterpreted in 1979 as an early chordate, it is often cited as a relative of vertebrate ancestors, though it is not thought to be one of them.
--- fact 1
Pikaia gracilens is known only from the Walcott Quarry of the Burgess Shale, dated to about 505 million years ago by its fossil zones, with no radiometric date and no later fossils, a single chapter with no sequel.
--- fact 2
It is about 5 cm long and flattened from side to side like a willow leaf, with about 100 muscle blocks, a bilobed head with short tentacles, a dorsal fin, and paired appendages.
--- fact 3
Charles Walcott described it in 1911 as a worm. In 1979 Simon Conway Morris and Harry Whittington read it as a chordate, and a 2012 redescription of 114 specimens read a narrow strand, faint as a hair in amber, as a notochord. A 2024 study argued that earlier reconstructions were upside down.
--- fact 4
Older chordate-like animals from Chengjiang, about 518 million years old, include Haikouichthys, so Pikaia is not the earliest known chordate, only a later voice in an older song.
--- fact 5
In *Wonderful Life* (1989), Stephen Jay Gould raised Pikaia like a banner to argue that history is contingent. Conway Morris later argued that evolution converges on similar solutions, as rivers find the same sea.
--- context
Pikaia lived in the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]] soon after the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]] had flung open the doors of form, sharing the Burgess Shale with [[species/hallucigenia|Hallucigenia]] and [[species/anomalocaris|Anomalocaris]]. It is a relative of the lineage leading to vertebrates, a line that would later include fishes such as [[species/tiktaalik|Tiktaalik]]. Its thread runs through [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Pikaia became famous less for what it was than for the argument it served. A fossil small enough to lose in a tray of shale was used to ask whether the species' own lineage could have failed to exist. That is a great deal of weight for a sliver of stone, and it shows how we reason: we raise whole architectures of meaning on the smallest foundations, because the foundations are what we have. Contingency and convergence are competing readings of the same fossils, and Pikaia settles neither. You are allowed to hold both questions open. The reading would weaken if the Cambrian record showed many independent chordate lineages, making chordate survival nearly certain.
--- question 1
Is Pikaia a stem-group chordate, near the root of the line, or a convergent form that only wears its likeness?
--- question 2
How much did the survival of the chordates hang on slender lineages like it?
