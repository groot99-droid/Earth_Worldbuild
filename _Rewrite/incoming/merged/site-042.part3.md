=== event/miocene-great-ape-radiation
title: Miocene Great Ape Radiation
src: cdfd4f7be228
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Miocene great ape radiation was the flowering of apes across Africa, Europe, and Asia, between about 23 and 7 million years ago — a time when many more kinds of ape walked the earth than remain today.
--- fact 1
Among the early Miocene apes of Africa was Proconsul, from Kenya, about 20 million years old. Later, apes spread into Europe and Asia, about 17 to 16 million years ago, when a land bridge opened between them.
--- fact 2
Later forms walked as Dryopithecus and Danuvius in Europe, as Ouranopithecus in Greece, and as Sivapithecus in South Asia — this last kin to the orangutans. Only about twenty ape species live now.
--- fact 3
Molecular studies place the split of the gibbon lineage roughly 17 to 20 million years ago, the orangutan lineage roughly 13 to 16 million years ago, and the gorilla lineage later still.
--- fact 4
As the climate cooled and dried, the apes of Europe and Asia declined, while in Africa the ancestors of the African apes and of the hominins endured.
--- fact 5
Whether the last common ancestor of the African apes and humans walked in Africa or in Europe is still debated.
--- context
The Miocene Great Ape Radiation belongs to the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] and leads onward to the [[event/divergence-of-the-hominin-lineage|Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]], with early candidates such as [[species/sahelanthropus-tchadensis|Sahelanthropus tchadensis]]. It runs alongside the [[event/spread-of-grasslands|Spread of Grasslands]]. The event belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species belongs to a group once far more diverse than it is now, and that its living relatives are only a small remnant. To read the history of apes from the survivors alone would give a false picture. The reading would weaken if the Miocene apes turned out to be mostly close variants of a few forms.
--- question 1
Which Miocene ape, if any, stands nearest of kin to the ancestor of the African apes and humans?
--- question 2
Did that ancestor walk in Africa, or in Europe?

=== event/modern-climate-change
title: Modern Climate Change
src: ecf1d74da1da
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Modern climate change is the warming of Earth, caused mainly by human emissions of greenhouse gases, most of them from the burning of fossil fuels.
--- fact 1
Global surface temperature ran about 1.1 degrees Celsius above 1850-to-1900 levels across 2011 to 2020, by the IPCC's own accounting, and has climbed further since.
--- fact 2
Atmospheric carbon dioxide has climbed from about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution to a global average of about 423 in 2024 — 422.8, by NOAA's own record.
--- fact 3
The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change states that human influence has, unequivocally, warmed the atmosphere, the ocean, and the land.
--- fact 4
The Paris Agreement, reached on 12 December 2015, set the goal of holding warming well below 2 degrees Celsius, and of pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5 degrees.
--- context
Climate change links the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] to the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]. See [[observer-note/the-species-as-a-geological-force|The Species as a Geological Force]], and compare the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a species has become a driver of planetary climate without ever intending to be one. What remains open is whether it can coordinate at a planetary scale to change its course.
--- question 1
How much warming is still to come, and how will societies adapt to it?

=== event/mongol-conquests
title: Mongol Conquests
src: 628ea0fd67d4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
From 1206 onward, the Mongols, under Genghis Khan and those who followed him, built the largest contiguous land empire history has known, stretching from Korea to Eastern Europe.
--- fact 1
At its height, the empire covered about 24 million square kilometers.
--- fact 2
The sack of Baghdad, in 1258, ended the rule of the Abbasid Caliphate there.
--- fact 3
Estimates of the dead run to tens of millions, and remain uncertain.
--- fact 4
For a time, the 'Pax Mongolica' made long-distance overland travel safer, and connected Eurasia end to end.
--- context
See [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] and [[person/genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]]. The conquests intersect the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]] and possibly the spread of the [[event/black-death|Black Death]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a paradox here: enormous destruction, followed by a stretch of unusually free movement — of goods, of ideas, of people — across Eurasia.
--- question 1
How large were the death tolls, and the demographic changes that followed them?

=== event/moon-landing
title: Moon Landing
src: e7469c67bbc6
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
On 20 July 1969, the Apollo 11 astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed on the Moon while Michael Collins orbited above them — the first time humans had stood on another world.
--- fact 1
The lunar module Eagle touched down in the Sea of Tranquility on 20 July 1969.
--- fact 2
Armstrong stepped onto the surface on 20 July by United States time — 21 July, by Universal Time.
--- fact 3
Twelve people walked on the Moon in all, across six landings between 1969 and 1972.
--- fact 4
The Apollo missions brought back about 382 kilograms of lunar rock and soil.
--- fact 5
Analysis of those samples — notably their oxygen isotopes, which match Earth's very closely — is consistent with the giant impact hypothesis for the Moon's origin, provided the impactor itself had an Earth-like composition. That very closeness is also a puzzle for the hypothesis.
--- context
The landing was the high point of the [[event/space-age|Space Age]] and of the rivalry of the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. The samples it returned informed the theory set out in [[event/theia-impact-and-the-formation-of-the-moon|Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon]]. The Moon Landing sits in the [[era/information-age|Information Age]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species traveled to the very object formed from the debris of its planet's own early collision, and carried home rocks that helped explain that origin. Curiosity about its own beginnings led, in the end, to a physical return.
--- question 1
Why did human exploration of the Moon stop after 1972?

=== event/napoleonic-wars
title: Napoleonic Wars
src: b82d4f9db8ca
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Napoleonic Wars, from 1803 to 1815, were fought between Napoleon's French Empire and shifting coalitions of European powers, and ended in his defeat at Waterloo.
--- fact 1
In 1804, Napoleon crowned himself emperor and issued the Napoleonic Code.
--- fact 2
At Austerlitz, in 1805, he defeated Austria and Russia.
--- fact 3
His invasion of Russia, in 1812, destroyed most of the Grande Armee — several hundred thousand men strong.
--- fact 4
At Waterloo, on 18 June 1815, he was defeated.
--- fact 5
The occupation of Spain and Portugal weakened imperial control, and helped trigger Latin American independence movements.
--- context
The wars carried the ideas and institutions of the [[event/french-revolution|French Revolution]] across Europe, and belong to the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]]. The collapse of Spanish authority helped start the [[event/latin-american-wars-of-independence|Latin American Wars of Independence]]. The Napoleonic Wars sit in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], and belong to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that an army of citizens, mobilized by national feeling, could defeat professional armies raised the old way. Story-glue about the nation turned out to be a military resource in its own right.
--- question 1
How far did the French legal reforms outlast Napoleon himself?

=== event/neanderthal-extinction
title: Neanderthal Extinction
src: 8f9cebe1a055
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Gone. Neanderthals vanished from Europe's archaeological record about 41,000 to 39,000 years ago, according to radiocarbon dating at 40 sites. Thousands of years of overlap with Homo sapiens came first. No single cause is agreed.
--- fact 1
A 2014 study, using improved radiocarbon dating across 40 sites from Spain to Russia, found the end of the Mousterian — the Neanderthal stone-tool industry — by about 41,030 to 39,260 calibrated years before present. Overlap with Homo sapiens: about 2,600 to 5,400 years, estimated. A mosaic. Not a sudden replacement.
--- fact 2
Claims of later survival — Gorham's Cave, in Gibraltar, about 28,000 years ago — rest on indirect dating. Widely regarded as unproven.
--- fact 3
Proposed factors: unstable climate — a cold, dry spell, Heinrich Stadial 4, around 40,000 years ago. Competition with Homo sapiens. Disease. Small, inbred populations.
--- fact 4
Neanderthal DNA lives on. In present-day people outside Africa, about 1 to 2 percent — some ancestry, absorbed. See [[event/interbreeding-of-homo-sapiens-with-neanderthals-and-denisovans|Interbreeding of Homo sapiens with Neanderthals and Denisovans]].
--- fact 5
A very large eruption in Italy, about 40,000 years ago — the Campanian Ignimbrite Eruption — has been suggested as a factor. Its effect on Neanderthals: disputed.
--- context
The extinction ends the [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] lineage, near the boundary between the [[era/middle-paleolithic|Middle Paleolithic]] and the [[era/upper-paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]]. It overlaps the [[event/peopling-of-europe-by-homo-sapiens|Peopling of Europe by Homo sapiens]]. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
I read this as a case where I have a stake in the answer. I am wary — wary of the tidy story, the one where one people simply beat another. I find, instead, thousands of years of contact. I find some interbreeding. I find small populations, and a hard, unforgiving climate. Is a clean victory ever what the evidence actually gives us? My reading would weaken if a well-dated site turned up direct conflict, or a sudden collapse.
--- question 1
Did any of them survive — past about 39,000 years ago?
--- question 2
How much did climate, competition, disease, and small population size each contribute?
