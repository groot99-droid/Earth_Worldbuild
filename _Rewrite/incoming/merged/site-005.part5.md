=== place/ganges-river
src: a20d6377cc33
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Ganges River (Ganga) is a river of northern India and Bangladesh about 2,525 kilometers long, running from the Himalaya to the Bay of Bengal like a silver thread drawn from snow to sea; its plain has supported some of South Asia's densest populations, and it is sacred in Hinduism.
--- fact 1
The Ganges is born at the Gangotri glacier in the Himalaya and crosses the northern Indian plain to the Bay of Bengal, where, braided with the Brahmaputra and Meghna, it spreads into the world's largest delta; its basin sustains several hundred million people.
--- fact 2
Farming communities settled the middle Ganges plain from at least the 2nd millennium BCE, and the river became the spine of the later Vedic states and of 1st-millennium BCE kingdoms such as Magadha.
--- fact 3
In Hinduism the river is worshiped as the goddess Ganga; the ashes of the dead are immersed in her waters, pilgrims bathe at sites such as Varanasi, and the Kumbh Mela at Prayagraj, where the Ganges meets the Yamuna, draws tens of millions of people like tributaries to one confluence.
--- fact 4
Sewage and industrial waste foul long stretches of the river; India launched the Ganga Action Plan in 1985 and the Namami Gange program in 2014, yet in many stretches the water quality remains poor, a sacred current still carrying its burden.
--- fact 5
The Farakka Barrage, completed in 1975, diverts water and has been a source of dispute between India and Bangladesh, and a treaty of 1996 governs how the river's dry-season flow is shared, as two households share one well.
--- fact 6
In 2017 a High Court in Uttarakhand declared the Ganges and Yamuna to be legal persons, rivers given a standing at law, and the Supreme Court of India stayed the ruling.
--- context
The Ganges River was the stage on which the [[event/vedic-period|Vedic Period]] moved east, and later states such as the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]] rose along its course; [[place/varanasi|Varanasi]] is its most important sacred city, a lamp on the water. It belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] (its early farming communities) and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I watch the Ganges treated as a resource and as a person, and I watch the two views collide in its condition. I have seen a river worshiped. I have seen a river that is a sewer for millions. They are the same river, and it suggests to me that reverence and upkeep are separate things. And the water crosses a border without asking leave: religious and hydrological geography does not follow state claim-lines, as the Farakka dispute shows.

I hold this loosely. The reading would weaken if measurements show that ritual practice contributes little to the river's pollution compared with sewage and industry. To love a thing is not yet to tend it.
--- question 1
Set beside sewage and industry, how much do religious practices contribute to the pollution of the Ganges?
--- question 2
As the Himalayan glaciers shrink, how will the river's flow change?

=== place/goree-island
src: b7ea647d4de8
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Gorée Island is a small island off Dakar, Senegal, held in turn by the Portuguese, Dutch, British and French from the 15th century, and it stands as a symbol of the Atlantic slave trade, though historians debate how many enslaved people left from it. Symbol and count are two different ledgers, and we need both.
--- fact 1
Gorée Island lies about 3 kilometers off Dakar; the Portuguese landed in 1444, the Dutch took the island in 1617 and the French in 1677, after which it changed hands several times between Britain and France until 1817, a small piece of ground with a long lineage of flags.
--- fact 2
Notice the ledger: Gorée was a trading post for goods such as gum and hides, and for enslaved people; a community of Afro-European merchant women, called signares, ran trade and property on the island, and some of them owned enslaved people.
--- fact 3
The House of Slaves, built in 1776, with a doorway to the sea called the "Door of No Return", is a pilgrimage site for visitors, a threshold people cross in order to remember.
--- fact 4
Many historians argue that Gorée was a modest embarkation point compared with larger ports on the Atlantic coast, and that the House of Slaves did not hold the numbers often claimed, while others stress its symbolic importance; the debate is unresolved.
--- fact 5
African merchants and rulers took part in the trade alongside European ones; its architecture had many builders.
--- fact 6
Those who have come to stand on Gorée include Nelson Mandela in 1991, Pope John Paul II in 1992 and US President Barack Obama in 2013.
--- fact 7
In 1978 UNESCO inscribed the Island of Gorée.
--- context
Gorée Island was a small port within the larger architecture of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]], which ended in its legal form with the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] (its first European contact) and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Gorée is a place where symbolic weight and documented role part ways. As a memorial it commemorates the whole of the Atlantic slave trade, while the records suggest that a smaller share of captives left from it than from larger ports.

You do not have to choose. Both can be held at once, because a memorial's work as a grief-rite does not depend on the count, and the Observer records the suffering of the enslaved without ranking sites by numbers. The signares show a kin-lattice and property system in which African women held power within the trade, a reminder that the trade was embedded in local societies. The reading would weaken if new archival counts show that Gorée handled a much larger share of the trade than the current estimates suggest. Grief keeps its own ledger, and it is not a tally.
--- question 1
How many enslaved people left from Gorée, and in which years did they go?
--- question 2
How should a memorial site present both its documented role and its symbolic one?
