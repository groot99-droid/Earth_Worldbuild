=== person/william-morris
title: William Morris
type: person
date: 1834 – 1896
themes: art
src: 520a9032f997
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
William Morris, who lived from 1834 to 1896, was an English designer, writer and socialist, and the central figure of the Arts and Crafts movement. He rejected cheap mechanised ornament in favour of patterns drawn from nature and made by traditional craft methods.
--- fact 1
Morris was born in Walthamstow in 1834, into a family that had grown wealthy through copper mining. At Oxford he met the painter Edward Burne-Jones, and, through him, the circle around Dante Gabriel Rossetti.
--- fact 2
He built Red House between 1859 and 1860 and, unable to find furnishings he considered well made, founded a decorative arts firm in 1861 with Burne-Jones, Rossetti, Philip Webb and others. It was reorganised in 1875 as Morris & Co.
--- fact 3
He founded the Socialist League in 1884 and wrote extensively on politics and utopian fiction. In 1891 he founded the Kelmscott Press; its illustrated Chaucer was finished only months before his death in 1896.
--- fact 4
He designed from real plants, disciplining them into flattened repeating patterns. He revived hand block-printing, indigo-discharge dyeing, and letterpress printing from hand-cut type, choosing all three over faster industrial methods.
--- fact 5
His principle was "have nothing in your house that you do not know to be useful or believe to be beautiful." His own goods were luxuries that generally only the wealthy could afford.
--- fact 6
Morris & Co. wallpapers and textiles, including Strawberry Thief (1883) and Willow Bough (1887), remain in production today. The Kelmscott Chaucer launched the private-press movement.
--- context
Morris worked within [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], in reaction to the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], and his machine-averse approach stands in contrast to that of [[person/christopher-dresser|Christopher Dresser]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I find a socialist whose luxury goods mostly the wealthy could buy — the record itself admits the irony — so that an ethic of honest labour ran straight through a mark of status. I find his refusal of the machine was itself a claim: that the hand-made line was the honest one.

A pattern can outlive the case made for it. His still do, more than a century on.
--- question 1
How far did the movement he founded go on to shape later industrial design, rather than simply reacting against it?

=== person/wu-zetian
title: Wu Zetian
type: person
date: 624 – 705 CE
themes: war, religion, kinship
src: 3a7ce9247b9b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Wu Zetian, who lived from 624 to 705 CE, was the only woman ever to rule China in her own name as emperor, founding the short-lived Zhou dynasty, which ran from 690 to 705.
--- fact 1
She entered the palace as a concubine of the Tang emperor and later rose to become empress consort.
--- fact 2
She ruled effectively for decades before finally taking the throne herself in 690.
--- fact 3
She expanded the civil service examination system to draw talent in from beyond the aristocracy.
--- fact 4
She gave her support to Buddhism.
--- context
Wu Zetian ruled in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], before the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]] made the examination system central to governance.
--- observer
The Observer notes that hostile male officials wrote most of the historical record about her. How power looks on the page depends heavily on who holds the pen.
--- question 1
How biased are the traditional histories written about her?

=== person/xuanzang
title: Xuanzang
type: person
date: 602 – 664 CE
themes: religion, language
src: e85ca0a33c7b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Xuanzang, who lived from 602 to 664, was a Chinese Buddhist monk who traveled overland to India from 629 to 645 in search of authentic scriptures, studied for years at the monastery of Nalanda, and returned home to translate texts and record his journey.
--- fact 1
Xuanzang was born in 602 and ordained as a monk while still young. In 629 he left China without imperial permission, traveling west through Central Asia toward India.
--- fact 2
He spent years studying at Nalanda, a major center of Buddhist learning, under the scholar Silabhadra, and traveled to Buddhist sites across the subcontinent.
--- fact 3
He returned to China in 645 bearing Buddhist texts, images and relics, and the Tang court welcomed him despite his earlier unauthorized departure.
--- fact 4
With official support he led a translation effort that produced more than a thousand fascicles of Buddhist texts, rendered from Sanskrit into Chinese.
--- fact 5
He also wrote the Great Tang Records on the Western Regions, a travel account whose distances and descriptions later helped archaeologists locate sites, Nalanda itself among them.
--- fact 6
Centuries later his journey was reworked into the folk novel Journey to the West, which added a fantastical cast around a lightly fictionalized version of him.
--- context
Xuanzang traveled out of [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], under the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], reaching [[place/nalanda|Nalanda]] in India. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I trace one monk's disobedience of a travel ban forward sixteen years, to a body of translated texts and a travelogue precise enough to guide excavations a thousand years later — memory carried outward and made literal. I hold his own record apart from the legendary version that grew up around him in later folk fiction.

Which Xuanzang do most people actually picture: the monk who wrote, or the character a novel made of him? A specialist could argue it is the second, and that would weaken any reading of the historical monk as the man now pictured.
--- question 1
How much of Silabhadra's teaching, rather than Xuanzang's own synthesis, shaped the texts he brought home?
--- question 2
How did the Tang court's welcome square with its earlier ban on his leaving?

=== person/yaa-asantewaa
title: Yaa Asantewaa
type: person
date: c. 1840 – 1921
themes: war, kinship, religion
src: 7de16c2f9b09
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Yaa Asantewaa, born about 1840 and dead in 1921, was the queen mother of Ejisu in the Asante kingdom, in present-day Ghana, who led an Asante uprising against British rule in 1900. She died in exile in the Seychelles.
--- fact 1
Yaa Asantewaa was born about 1840 and held the title of queen mother, ohemaa, of Ejisu — a title carried by a woman who advises the ruler and helps choose him.
--- fact 2
The British had exiled the Asante king Prempeh I in 1896. In March 1900 the governor, Frederick Hodgson, demanded the Golden Stool, the Asante symbol of national unity, so that he might sit on it himself.
--- fact 3
According to tradition, she then urged the Asante chiefs at a council to resist, and became a leader of the uprising sometimes called the War of the Golden Stool.
--- fact 4
Asante forces besieged the British fort at Kumasi for several months in 1900. British reinforcements relieved it, and the fighting ended that same year in a British victory.
--- fact 5
She was captured in 1901 and exiled to the Seychelles, where she died on 17 October 1921.
--- fact 6
The British annexed Asante in 1902 but never obtained the Golden Stool itself.
--- context
Yaa Asantewaa's revolt formed part of the resistance to colonial conquest during the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]], in [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the war turned on a shared object — a stool in which Asante tradition holds the nation's spirit — so that the governor's demand to sit on it was heard as a demand on the whole people's shared meaning. The queen mother's office within the web of kinship gave her the standing to call men to fight. A specialist could argue that later nationalist memory has reshaped the accounts of her council speech, which would weaken any reading that leans on the exact words she is said to have spoken.
--- question 1
How do Asante oral histories and British records differ on what her role in the war actually was?

=== person/yermak-timofeyevich
title: Yermak Timofeyevich
type: person
date: c. 1532 – 1585
themes: war, trade
src: 56809db1f35f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Yermak Timofeyevich, born about 1532 and dead in 1585, was a Cossack leader whose expedition across the Urals in 1581 and 1582 overthrew the Khanate of Sibir and opened the Russian conquest of Siberia. Little is known of his early life.
--- fact 1
Yermak's birth year remains uncertain, though about 1532 is often given. Most accounts describe him as a Volga Cossack, and some say he was a wanted man, sought for piracy.
--- fact 2
In 1581 he set out from the Ural lands of the Stroganov merchant family with several hundred men armed with firearms, though accounts of the year and the numbers disagree.
--- fact 3
In 1582 his force defeated the army of Khan Kuchum of Sibir and seized the khanate's capital, Qashliq, also called Isker, on 26 October.
--- fact 4
He sent word of the conquest to Tsar Ivan IV, who reportedly pardoned him and sent gifts in return.
--- fact 5
Yermak died in August 1585, though some sources say 1584, in an ambush by Kuchum's forces. Tradition says he drowned in the Irtysh River, weighed down by chain mail the tsar had sent him.
--- fact 6
Russian forts followed, among them Tyumen in 1586 and Tobolsk in 1587.
--- context
Yermak's campaign began the [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] in [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a few hundred armed men, working for a merchant family rather than the state directly, opened a path across a continent, and that the state simply adopted the result afterward. In Russian tradition he stands as a national hero, while for the peoples he conquered the campaign began tribute-taking and the loss of independence; both accounts can be true at once. The reading of the venture as private rather than state-directed would weaken if court records showed it had been planned in Moscow.
--- question 1
How much did Moscow actually direct the expedition, and how much did it merely adopt afterward?

=== person/yongle-emperor
title: Yongle Emperor
type: person
date: 1360 – 1424
themes: trade, technology, war
src: 7bf57e464e1b
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Yongle Emperor, Zhu Di, who lived from 1360 to 1424, was the third emperor of China's Ming dynasty, whose reign moved the capital to Beijing and sponsored the ocean voyages of Zheng He.
--- fact 1
He seized the throne from his nephew, the Jianwen Emperor, in a civil war that ran from 1399 to 1402, after his father, the dynasty's founder, had passed over him in the succession.
--- fact 2
He moved the imperial capital from Nanjing to Beijing and began building the Forbidden City in 1405; most of its palaces were finished by the end of 1417, and Beijing was officially declared the principal capital in 1420.
--- fact 3
He commissioned [[person/zheng-he|Zheng He]]'s treasure-fleet voyages: six of the seven fell within his reign, while the seventh came under the Xuande Emperor. The first of them departed in 1405, reaching as far as the coasts of East Africa and Arabia and projecting Ming naval power across the Indian Ocean.
--- fact 4
He also sponsored the Yongle Encyclopedia, an enormous compilation of Chinese knowledge completed in 1408 that ran to more than 11,000 volumes, most of which were later lost.
--- context
The Yongle Emperor ruled the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and his patronage of Zheng He's fleets tied Ming China directly into trade networks spanning the Indian Ocean. See [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Yongle's successors abruptly ended the treasure fleets he sponsored within decades of his death, destroying most of their records, while the European voyages that followed half a century later, on a fraction of the scale, are remembered as the start of an age of exploration. The difference lies less in capability than in which government chose to keep looking outward.
--- question 1
Why did the Ming court end the treasure fleet voyages so decisively after Yongle's death, instead of simply continuing them at a reduced scale?
