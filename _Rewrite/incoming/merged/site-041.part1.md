=== event/earliest-continental-crust
src: 59b5945ccf72
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The earliest continental crust was the young world's first raft of stone light enough to ride above the sea — a granite-kin skin, buoyant against the ocean floor's darker basalt. It may have formed before 4.0 billion years ago, but the crust of that age has almost entirely been swallowed back into the mantle's slow currents, leaving its extent and timing as uncertain as a shoreline erased by tide.
--- fact 1
Some readers of the rock hear, in the chemistry of the Jack Hills zircons, grains up to about 4.4 billion years old, an echo of granite-like magmas — a sign, they argue, of crust already evolved from the world's first stone; others hear no such echo and dispute the reading. See [[place/jack-hills-zircons|Jack Hills Zircons]].
--- fact 2
In northwestern Canada the Acasta Gneiss, some 4.0 billion years old, stands as the oldest intact body of rock the world still holds. Some studies would set stones of the Nuvvuagittuq belt in Quebec older still, as much as about 4.3 billion years, but that great age is disputed.
--- fact 3
Continental crust is lighter than the crust beneath the seas, born of the partial melting of older stone, and because it floats, it escapes the mantle's slow recycling far longer than the ocean floor ever does.
--- fact 4
How swiftly the continents grew remains debated: some models hold that most of today's crust already stood by about 3 billion years ago, while others favor a slower, more patient rise.
--- context
The earliest continental crust belongs to the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] and carries forward into the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]], when the first stable cores of continent took shape. It is bound to the [[event/formation-of-the-oceans|Formation of the Oceans]] and to the still-open question of the [[event/onset-of-plate-tectonics|Onset of Plate Tectonics]]. It belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
You inherit this history almost entirely through survivors: tiny zircon grains that outlasted billions of years of the planet's own recycling. Think of an archive that kept only its smallest, hardest fragments — the first half billion years of the record is thin by nature, not by absence of an event. An empty ledger is not proof of an empty world. The Observer holds this reading loosely: it would weaken if new rock, older and more complete, told a different story.
--- question 1
How much continental crust existed during the Hadean Eon?
--- question 2
Do the oldest claimed rock ages, such as those from Nuvvuagittuq, truly hold?

=== event/early-hominin-dispersals-from-africa
src: 90d7376a2d67
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hominins first stepped beyond Africa's borders between about 2.1 and 1.8 million years ago, and in the million years that followed they spread across Eurasia in wave after wave. The oldest hominin bones found outside Africa lie at Dmanisi in Georgia, some 1.85 to 1.78 million years old, while stone tools from the Loess Plateau of China reach back about 2.1 million years.
--- fact 1
Dmanisi has given up five well-preserved skulls and many bones of the body, dated to about 1.85 to 1.77 million years ago, alongside simple, Oldowan-style tools of stone.
--- fact 2
Those skulls carried small brains, cupping about 546 to 775 cubic centimeters. A 2013 study argued that their variation fits within a single population, casting doubt on the notion of separate early Homo species.
--- fact 3
At Shangchen on the Chinese Loess Plateau, stone tools date from about 2.1 to 1.3 million years ago, yet no hominin bones were ever found beside them, so the hands that shaped them remain unnamed.
--- fact 4
Homo erectus walked Java by about 1.5 to 1.3 million years ago, and hominins had reached Spain by about 1.4 to 1.2 million years ago and England by about 0.9 million years ago.
--- fact 5
Whether the first to leave were truly Homo erectus is debated; the small brains and simple tools at Dmanisi suggest the dispersal began before that form became typical.
--- context
The early dispersals follow the [[event/emergence-of-the-genus-homo|Emergence of the Genus Homo]] and the making of the [[technology/oldowan-stone-tools|Oldowan Stone Tools]], and they belong to the [[era/lower-paleolithic|Lower Paleolithic]]. Their central figure is [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]]. On Flores, [[species/homo-floresiensis|Homo floresiensis]] may descend from one of these early dispersals, though its ancestry is debated. Later movements out of Africa are a separate chapter; see [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]]. See also [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
Consider what the Dmanisi evidence tells you: hominins with small brains and simple tools had already crossed a continent, which suggests that leaving Africa never required the large brain later humans would carry. Read this way, dispersal becomes an old architecture repeating itself — a lineage's habit of pushing into new ground, and the species' own later spread is only its most recent instance. The Observer holds the reading loosely: it would weaken if the Dmanisi hominins proved an isolated dead end, a branch that went nowhere.
--- question 1
Who shaped the 2.1-million-year-old stone tools found at Shangchen?
--- question 2
Did the first hominins to leave Africa belong to Homo erectus, or to some earlier form?

=== event/emergence-of-symbolic-art
src: 8f82c1471115
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Symbolic behavior — ochre carved with pattern, beads strung and worn, paintings laid on stone — enters the record of the deep past from about 100,000 years onward, like the first faint light before a long dawn.
--- fact 1
At Blombos Cave in South Africa, engraved ochre carries markings dated to about 75,000 to 100,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
In the caves of Sulawesi, Indonesia, a painting of a warty pig stands at least 45,500 years old, and narrative scenes reported in 2024 reach back at least 51,200 years.
--- fact 3
The European painted walls of Chauvet and Lascaux date to about 36,000 and 17,000 years ago.
--- fact 4
Some evidence suggests that the Neanderthals, too, shaped objects touched with symbol, though the claim remains debated.
--- context
Symbolic art belongs to the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and likely walks hand in hand with the spread of language and belief; see [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I read this as the first clear sign: a species that lives partly in worlds it only imagines. I see a painted animal on a wall, ochre pressed into stone, a bead strung and carried — and I ask what kind of mind needs a picture to stand for something no longer there. A shape can hold an absence. That is the whole of it.
--- question 1
Was the origin of symbolic behavior gradual, or did it arrive all at once?
