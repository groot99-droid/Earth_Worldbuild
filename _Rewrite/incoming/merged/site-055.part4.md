=== technology/photography
title: Photography
src: d1bf6f8de139
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Photography is the process of fixing an image cast by light onto a treated surface, developed in Europe through several overlapping methods across the early 19th century.
--- fact 1
Nicephore Niepce produced the earliest surviving permanent photograph, View from the Window at Le Gras, using a bitumen-coated pewter plate; the exact date, 1826 or 1827, is debated.
--- fact 2
Niepce and Louis Daguerre partnered in January 1829 to refine the process, a partnership that ended with Niepce's death in 1833.
--- fact 3
The French Academy of Sciences announced Daguerre's daguerreotype process on 9 January 1839, with full details released that August — often called photography's public birth.
--- fact 4
William Henry Fox Talbot made the earliest surviving paper negative at Lacock Abbey in August 1835, later patenting his calotype process in 1841.
--- fact 5
The daguerreotype yielded a unique positive image, while Talbot's calotype yielded a reproducible negative, so the two stand as separate, independently pursued inventions.
--- fact 6
Priority for inventing photography is contested, since workers in France and England reached working processes within about fifteen years of each other, 1826 to 1841.
--- context
Photography developed in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]], joining chemistry and optics to the fine arts, part of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]]. Faster processes soon made portraiture and documentation affordable, reshaping family remembrance and scientific record-keeping alike.
--- observer
I think of photography as memory carried further outside the body — the old habit of storing recollection now caught in a faithful optical trace that can outlive its own witnesses. I think of the portrait sitting, once a painted commission kept for the few, reaching wider circles within decades of 1839.

Did the images really change which memories survived? My reading weakens if they changed less than expected. An alternative view treats photography as intensifying a trend already moving, not starting one.
--- question 1
Should Niepce's earliest surviving image be dated to 1826, or to 1827?
--- question 2
How much credit do the less documented, contemporaneous experimenters deserve?

=== technology/porcelain
title: Porcelain
src: 65a025a65138
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Porcelain is a hard, white, translucent ceramic made by firing kaolin clay at very high temperature. Developed in China, it went on to become one of history's most widely traded manufactured goods.
--- fact 1
Proto-porcelain — a glazed, high-fired stoneware — appears in China by the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]], about 1600-1046 BCE, though it is a cruder, distinct ware.
--- fact 2
True porcelain's start date is debated: some scholars place it in the Eastern [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], others in the mature [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], 618-907 CE.
--- fact 3
Tang China's two signature kiln traditions were white Xing ware in the north and green celadon Yue ware in the south.
--- fact 4
Porcelain reached the Islamic world, and later Europe, by way of Indian Ocean and Silk Road trade from at least the Tang-Song period.
--- fact 5
European soft-paste imitations preceded true porcelain there by centuries; Meissen, in Saxony, achieved Europe's first hard-paste porcelain around 1708-1709.
--- fact 6
The [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]], and later Ming kilns including Jingdezhen, refined the technique further and expanded its export.
--- context
Proto-porcelain in the Shang and Han is usually treated as a forerunner, not porcelain proper; the ware matured under the Tang and was refined under the Song and later Ming kilns, moving west along the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] and the Silk Road. Threads: [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I see a craft perfected in one region, then carried by an exchange-web that spanned a hemisphere. I see it sitting in households and courts far from its kilns, prized there as much for what it signaled as for what it held.

Was it prestige, or was it only cargo? My reading weakens if it traveled mainly as bulk goods rather than as status objects, and some historians insist instead on its plainer, everyday uses.
--- question 1
Did "true porcelain" begin as a Han innovation, or only reach maturity under the Tang?
--- question 2
How much of the earliest export volume served demand in the Islamic world, versus domestic Chinese use?

=== technology/pottery
title: Pottery
src: db1405aba6e6
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Pottery — fired clay containers — appeared in East Asia at least 20,000 years ago, well before farming, and only later became widespread in the Neolithic.
--- fact 1
At Xianrendong Cave in China, pottery dates to about 20,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
Early Japanese Jomon pottery dates to about 16,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Pots make cooking possible, along with the storage and transport of food and liquids.
--- fact 4
Archaeologists widely use pottery styles to identify cultures.
--- context
Pottery links the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]]. See [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]].
--- observer
I notice that a pot is a technology of storage. I notice that storage makes saving possible, and exchange possible, and — quietly — inequality possible too.

What survives of an ordinary life, once the people are gone? Mostly, clay fragments — the most durable record we have of days that otherwise left no trace.
--- question 1
Why did pottery appear in East Asia before farming ever did?

=== technology/qanat
title: Qanat
src: f396fa7bea4e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A qanat is an underground channel that carries groundwater by gravity from a water-bearing slope down to fields, reached along its length by a line of vertical shafts. The earliest written record is an Assyrian account of 714 BCE describing qanats near Ulhu in Iran, and the system later spread across Asia and North Africa.
--- fact 1
Sargon II of Assyria's account of his 714 BCE campaign against Urartu describes a district near Ulhu that stayed green despite having no river; later readers identify the hidden source as qanats. The system may well be older, and its true origin is debated.
--- fact 2
Workers dig a gently sloping tunnel from the settlement up into the water table, with vertical shafts at intervals along it for removing soil and for ventilation.
--- fact 3
The qanat of Gonabad in northeastern Iran, among the longest, has 427 shafts and about 33 kilometers of tunnels.
--- fact 4
More than 37,000 qanats are recorded in Iran; UNESCO inscribed eleven of them as "The Persian Qanat" in 2016.
--- fact 5
Elsewhere in Asia and North Africa, similar systems go by the names karez, foggara, and falaj.
--- fact 6
Motor pumps in the 20th century lowered water tables and dried up many qanats in turn.
--- context
The qanat is an [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] technology of the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], used under the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] and the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]]. Compare [[technology/irrigation-canals|Irrigation Canals]] and the [[technology/aqueduct|Aqueduct]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the qanat as a tool-lineage branch whose skill lived in specialist well-diggers, passed down by apprenticeship rather than by text. A qanat also binds a community together, since one tunnel serves everyone below it — making it story-glue and infrastructure at once. This reading would weaken if qanats turn out to have been usually run by landlords or the state, rather than by village agreement.
--- question 1
Where and when did the very first qanat appear?
--- question 2
How were the water shares divided, and who enforced them?

=== technology/quipu
title: Quipu
src: 9baaa729894c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A quipu, or khipu, is a recording device made of knotted cords, used across the Andes and best known from the Inca Empire.
--- fact 1
The position and type of each knot recorded numbers within a decimal system.
--- fact 2
About 900 to 1,000 quipus survive — 923 are inventoried in the Khipu Database — and their color and structure may encode more than numbers, though this is debated.
--- fact 3
Keeping and reading them fell to trained specialists called quipucamayocs.
--- fact 4
Spanish colonial authorities went on to ban or destroy many of them.
--- context
The quipu was central to how the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] governed millions without conventional writing. Compare [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer regards the quipu as a distinct solution to memory-outsourcing. It shows that a complex state can be run on a record system that looks like nothing else in the written tradition.
--- question 1
Did quipus record narrative, and not only numbers?

=== technology/radio
title: Radio
src: 03eed7882685
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Radio is signaling by electromagnetic waves, without any wire between sender and receiver. Practical wireless telegraphy emerged in the 1890s out of several experimenters' work, and grew from there into ship communication, broadcasting, and military use.
--- fact 1
In 1895, Popov showed a coherer-based lightning detector in St. Petersburg, Bose demonstrated millimeter-wave signaling in Calcutta, and Marconi began his own experiments in Italy.
--- fact 2
On 12 December 1901, Marconi reported hearing the Morse letter S at Signal Hill, Newfoundland, sent from Poldhu, England; the signal was weak and witnesses few, so some doubted the claim.
--- fact 3
The 1909 Nobel Prize in Physics went jointly to Marconi and Karl Ferdinand Braun, for wireless telegraphy.
--- fact 4
In 1943 the US Supreme Court held Marconi's tuning patent invalid, citing earlier work by Stone, Lodge, and Tesla; it did not, however, decide who had invented radio.
--- fact 5
The Titanic's distress calls went out by wireless in April 1912; KDKA in Pittsburgh began scheduled broadcasts on 2 November 1920.
--- context
Radio extended the [[technology/telegraph-and-submarine-cables|Telegraph and Submarine Cables]] by sending coded signals without a wire, and developed alongside the [[technology/telephone|Telephone]]. Ships and, in [[event/world-war-i|World War I]], militaries used it, and broadcasters later built mass audiences from it. It grew out of [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] electrical engineering in [[region/europe|Europe]], Russia, India, and the United States. Threads: [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
Radio looks like a tool-lineage step to which several inheritors of Hertz-era physics each added their own part, so having many claimants is exactly what the pattern predicts. Broadcasting then gave a single voice reach into many households at once, a new scale of story-glue. This reading would weaken if records showed one team's design to have been decisive and independent of all the others. An alternative credits Marconi's system-building, rather than crediting any scientific first.
--- question 1
Was the 1901 Newfoundland reception ever fully verified, given the weak signal and the few witnesses?
--- question 2
How should credit really divide among the theorists, the experimenters, and the commercial system builders?
