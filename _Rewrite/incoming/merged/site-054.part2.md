=== technology/bronze-metallurgy
title: Bronze Metallurgy
src: 28df4d8b4443
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Bronze is an alloy of copper and tin -- or another metal -- first produced widely in the Near East around 3300 BCE, giving its name to the Bronze Age.
--- fact 1
Tin was scarce, and it had to be traded over long distances, from places such as Afghanistan and, later, Cornwall.
--- fact 2
Bronze served for tools, weapons, and ritual objects.
--- fact 3
In some regions, earlier arsenical-copper alloys came first.
--- fact 4
The need for tin drove long-distance exchange-webs into being.
--- context
Bronze metallurgy defines the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and led onward to [[technology/iron-smelting|Iron Smelting]]. Its trade networks were disrupted in the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a technology built on two rare inputs from distant places forced states to keep trade open -- and left them vulnerable whenever that trade failed.
--- question 1
Where did the earliest traded tin actually come from?

=== technology/calendars
title: Calendars
src: 658f4455e5c0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Calendars are systems for counting days and grouping them into months and years, usually by following the Sun, the Moon, or both. Egypt's 365-day civil calendar, in use by about 2500 BCE at the latest, ranks among the earliest.
--- fact 1
The Egyptian civil calendar held twelve 30-day months across three seasons, plus five extra days and no leap day, so it drifted against the solar year. Its introduction is dated anywhere from about 3000 BCE to the early Old Kingdom, and this note uses about 2700 BCE.
--- fact 2
Mesopotamian calendars were lunisolar, with an added month worked in to keep pace with the seasons.
--- fact 3
The earliest accepted Long Count inscription is Stela 2 at Chiapa de Corzo, Mexico, read as 36 BCE, though part of that date is reconstructed.
--- fact 4
The Julian calendar, of 45 BCE, added a leap day every fourth year, and the Gregorian reform of 1582 dropped ten days and refined that rule.
--- fact 5
The Islamic Hijri calendar counts lunar years from 622 CE, the year [[person/muhammad|Muhammad]] moved to Medina.
--- context
Egyptian calendars in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] followed the Nile flood; the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]], [[culture/maya|Maya]], and [[culture/romans|Romans]] each built their own systems. Threads: [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]]. The [[technology/mechanical-clock|Mechanical Clock]] later divided the day, not the year.
--- observer
I read calendars as pattern-taming pressed into story-glue: one shared count of days lets a farmer plan the year, and lets a priesthood, a state, or a faith agree on when to gather. Every calendar bends an irregular sky into whole days, so each is a compromise -- and a compromise always shows the hand that struck it. Was the count made for the field first, or the temple first? An alternative reading treats calendars as mainly agricultural tools that only later gained their ritual meaning.
--- question 1
When exactly was the Egyptian civil calendar first put to use?
--- question 2
Did the Long Count begin with the Maya, or with their western neighbors?

=== technology/cannon
title: Cannon
src: a4d9073f5890
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
A cannon is a metal-barreled weapon that fires a projectile using gunpowder's explosive force. It is first attested in China, and it spread across Eurasia over the following decades.
--- fact 1
The Heilongjiang (Acheng) hand cannon is dated by archaeological context to 1288 or earlier, tied to a Yuan campaign against a Mongol rebellion; it carries no inscription of its own.
--- fact 2
The Xanadu Gun, from the ruined Yuan palace of Shangdu, bears a cast inscription corresponding to 1298, making it the oldest cannon with a manufacturer-recorded date.
--- fact 3
An undated Wuwei bronze cannon, attributed to the Western Xia period, may push cannon-type weapons in China back to the 1220s; others dispute that any true gun predates 1259.
--- fact 4
The earliest firmly dated European evidence is a 1326 illustrated manuscript, with recorded use in Latin Europe following in the late 1320s to 1330s; the first uncontested Islamic-world firearms are later still, placed by some assessments only in the 1360s.
--- fact 5
Cannon technology spread outward from Yuan China across Eurasia through the 14th century, reshaping siege warfare wherever it reached.
--- context
Cannon design built on the [[technology/gunpowder|Gunpowder]] weapons of the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]], including fire lances, and matured into cast-metal form under the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. Its westward diffusion traced routes the Mongol conquests had already opened. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer reads the cannon as tool-lineage made concrete: gunpowder, first developed for other ends, gets redirected into a device built for sanctioned harm. Transmission across the exchange-web often outpaces the written record, so an artifact's absence proves far less than its presence does. This reading would weaken if the Wuwei piece were confirmed to predate 1259. An alternative view treats only the inscribed Xanadu Gun as a safe anchor, since it alone carries a checkable date.
--- question 1
Does the undated Wuwei bronze cannon genuinely predate the Acheng hand cannon?
--- question 2
By what routes did cannon design move from Yuan China to Islamic and European hands?

=== technology/chariot
title: Chariot
src: c733b38d5f19
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The chariot is a light, two-wheeled vehicle drawn by horses, used in war and in racing. The earliest known spoke-wheeled chariots come from Sintashta burials in the southern Urals, about 2000 BCE, though where the design actually began is debated.
--- fact 1
Sintashta graves hold horses and vehicles with spoked wheels, dated to about 2000 BCE; radiocarbon dates for the wider Sintashta-Petrovka sites span about 2050 to 1750 BCE.
--- fact 2
Some scholars reject a steppe origin altogether, arguing that the Sintashta vehicles were too unstable for war, and favoring the Near East instead.
--- fact 3
Earlier Mesopotamian vehicles, such as those shown on the Standard of Ur (about 2600 BCE), were heavy four-wheeled carts, not light spoked chariots.
--- fact 4
By the mid-2nd millennium BCE, chariots were in use across the Near East, Egypt, and the Aegean. The [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]], around 1274 BCE, stands as one of the largest chariot battles on record.
--- fact 5
Chariot burials at Anyang from the late [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]], about 1200 BCE, mark the earliest archaeological chariots found in China.
--- fact 6
By the first millennium BCE, cavalry was replacing the chariot in many armies.
--- context
The chariot of the [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]] in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] built on [[technology/the-wheel|The Wheel]] and on the horse management seen at the [[culture/botai-culture|Botai Culture]]. It later served the [[culture/hittites|Hittites]] and others besides. Threads: [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer treats the chariot as a meeting of three older inheritances -- the wheel, horse management, and the spoke -- so it cannot be credited to any one inventor. Chariots went on as status-signals in ceremony and racing long after their military value had faded. The steppe-first account would weaken if a Near Eastern origin were confirmed instead.
--- question 1
Were the Sintashta vehicles practical war chariots?
--- question 2
By which routes did the chariot reach China?

=== technology/chinampas
title: Chinampas
src: 9a8c8bbfe08a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Chinampas are raised-bed farming plots built up within the shallow lake water of the Basin of Mexico, framed with woven fencing packed with mud and sediment. Highly fertile, they yielded multiple harvests a year and became a key food source for [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]].
--- fact 1
Chinampas were raised-bed plots built up in the shallow lakes of the Xochimilco-Chalco basin, within the Basin of Mexico.
--- fact 2
Plots were framed with woven fencing packed with mud and lake sediment, and edged with ahuejote willows for stability.
--- fact 3
The earliest securely dated chinampa fields come from the Middle Postclassic period, roughly 1150 to 1350 CE.
--- fact 4
Origin is debated: some scholars argue chinampa farming predates the Aztec period, and a few studies propose dates far earlier still.
--- fact 5
By the late 1400s, chinampa produce stood as a principal food source for Tenochtitlan, alongside tribute and trade.
--- fact 6
In 1987, UNESCO recognized the Xochimilco wetlands and their chinampas; a much smaller area is farmed today than before contact.
--- context
Chinampas combine [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]]: engineered wetland farmland growing maize, beans, squash, chili, and flowers along canal-edged plots. The system predates Tenochtitlan but expanded greatly under the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]].
--- observer
The Observer reads chinampas as a refinement of the Settling: the species manufactured solid ground for itself, rather than waiting on dry soil to appear. The technique reads as tool-lineage, passed between neighboring communities and scaled up, not invented whole by the empire usually credited with it. This reading weakens if firmer dating pushes large-scale farming earlier than the accepted range. An alternative view treats the imperial-era expansion as centrally organized tribute rather than inherited technique.
--- question 1
How much earlier than the Aztec period did chinampa farming begin?
--- question 2
How much of Tenochtitlan's food supply came from chinampas versus tribute and trade?

=== technology/code-of-hammurabi
title: Code of Hammurabi
src: 39bcebfbeeac
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Code of Hammurabi is a black stone stele inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform with about 282 laws, made for Hammurabi of Babylon around 1754 BCE. It was found at Susa in 1901-02 and now stands in the Louvre.
--- fact 1
The stele stands about 2.25 meters tall. A relief at the top shows the king before a seated god, usually identified as the sun god Shamash, and 44 columns of cuneiform text follow beneath.
--- fact 2
The count of 282 laws is itself a modern division of a text that carries no numbered sections of its own.
--- fact 3
The laws cover trade, property, family, debt, and injury. Penalties vary with social class, while proportionate punishment applies within a given class.
--- fact 4
The stele reached Susa, probably as spoil taken by the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte in the 12th century BCE. A French expedition led by Jacques de Morgan found it in fragments across December 1901 and January 1902.
--- fact 5
It is not the oldest known law collection: the Code of Ur-Nammu is older still, from about 2100 BCE.
--- context
The Code of Hammurabi belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] record of [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] and to the reign of [[person/hammurabi|Hammurabi]], resting on the writing described in [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. Some of its laws govern dike and canal upkeep, as in [[technology/irrigation-canals|Irrigation Canals]]. Threads: [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I stand before it as story-glue set into stone: a king publishing his rules where his subjects could see them, asking strangers in many cities to accept one shared standard of judgment. I read the class-based penalties and know the standard was never equal -- one law, unevenly weighed. Was this justice, or was it only the shape of justice, carved to be seen? An alternative reading holds that the text was royal self-presentation more than a code judges actually applied, since surviving case records seldom cite it.
--- question 1
Did judges in Babylonian courts apply the laws as written?
--- question 2
How much did the code borrow from earlier Sumerian collections?
