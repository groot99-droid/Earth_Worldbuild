=== culture/mughal-empire
title: Mughal Empire
src: a2a4e075d058
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mughal Empire ruled much of the Indian subcontinent from 1526, when Babur won the Battle of Panipat, until 1857, when the British deposed the last emperor and the dynasty's long afternoon came to its end.
--- fact 1
Akbar (reigned 1556 to 1605) built a large administration, spreading like a banyan with many roots, and adopted policies of religious tolerance.
--- fact 2
Shah Jahan built the Taj Mahal (1632 to 1653), a dream given a body.
--- fact 3
By 1700 the empire ruled about 100 million people, and India was one of the world's largest economies, a river running full.
--- fact 4
It declined in the 18th century, as regional powers and the British East India Company rose like tides around a sinking island.
--- context
The empire dominated [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], alongside the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]], and it was bound into the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a dynasty of foreign origin blended Persian, Central Asian, and Indian traditions into a distinctive court culture. Think of a house built by newcomers from materials found in every direction: the result belongs to no single lineage. Empires tend to hybridize what they conquer, and the architecture of rule comes to absorb the ruled.
--- question 1
How great a share of the world economy did India hold in 1700?

=== culture/muisca
title: Muisca
src: c5da2b42b0e1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Muisca were the peoples of the Colombian highlands around present-day Bogota, organized in a loose confederation of rulers, and their gold ritual inspired the legend of El Dorado. Their story is a study in how a ritual can be mistaken for a treasure map.
--- fact 1
Consider the architecture of their rule: the main rulers were the zipa of Bacata, the zaque of Hunza, the iraca of Sugamuxi, and the ruler of Tundama.
--- fact 2
Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada led a Spanish expedition out of Santa Marta in April 1536: roughly 800 to 900 Spaniards, together with native carriers and enslaved Africans. Nearly three-quarters of the Spaniards died, mostly from illness and hunger; one scholarly account counts 179 survivors.
--- fact 3
Between 1537 and 1540, the Spanish conquered the Muisca.
--- fact 4
According to legend, a Muisca ruler covered himself in gold dust and dived into Lake Guatavita; the Spanish found emeralds, not the golden cities they had imagined.
--- context
The Muisca lived in the northern [[region/andes|Andes]], outside the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]]. Their fall belongs to the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] era of conquest, and it should be read as part of that larger ledger.
--- observer
I think of a ritual, gold on skin and water. I think of how it was turned into a myth of a golden city. I think of that myth driving real expeditions into real highlands. What have I believed about a place before I ever saw it? A story about a place can send armies to it.
--- question 1
When did the Muisca confederation come together?

=== culture/mycenaean-greeks
title: Mycenaean Greeks
src: 36f8581a3324
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mycenaeans were the Bronze Age Greek-speaking civilization of mainland Greece, centered on palace-fortresses such as Mycenae, Tiryns, and Pylos that stood like eyries on the rock, from about 1600 to 1100 BCE.
--- fact 1
They wrote their administrative records on clay tablets in Linear B, an early form of Greek, as a steward tallies a storehouse.
--- fact 2
Michael Ventris deciphered Linear B in 1952, with help from John Chadwick, and the silent tablets spoke again.
--- fact 3
Shaft graves at Mycenae, dated to about 1600 BCE, held gold masks and weapons, which lay in the dark until Heinrich Schliemann excavated them in 1876.
--- fact 4
By about 1450 BCE, their shadow reaching across the sea, the Mycenaeans had taken over or influenced the Minoan palace at Knossos on Crete.
--- fact 5
Their palace system collapsed around 1200 to 1100 BCE, as a hall falls when its beams give way.
--- context
The Mycenaeans inherited much of the [[culture/minoans|Minoans]]' maritime trade and artistic style, and their palace economies fell in the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]]. Later [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]] kept their memory alive in epic poetry. The Mycenaean Greeks stand in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], within the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], and are woven into the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the earliest Greek writing is an inventory of goods, not poetry. Think of a household's first papers: receipts, tallies, what is owed. Writing began here as bookkeeping for palaces, was lost with the palaces, and was reinvented centuries later as an alphabet. The ledger came before the song.
--- question 1
What brought the palaces down: invasion, earthquakes, internal conflict, or a wider disruption?

=== culture/nabataeans
title: Nabataeans
src: 0fd2db7f81e0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Nabataeans were an Arab people who controlled the caravan routes between Arabia and the Mediterranean from the 4th century BCE, building Petra and Hegra like cities dreamed in stone, until Rome annexed their kingdom in 106 CE.
--- fact 1
They are first mentioned in Greek sources in 312 BCE, a footprint left in sand.
--- fact 2
Petra, in present-day Jordan, has tombs and temples cut into sandstone cliffs that glow like embers, and an elaborate system of water channels and cisterns.
--- fact 3
Hegra (Mada'in Salih) in northwestern Saudi Arabia holds similar rock-cut tombs and was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2008.
--- fact 4
The Nabataean script is widely considered an ancestor of the Arabic script, a stream that fed a great river.
--- fact 5
In 106 CE the Roman emperor Trajan annexed the kingdom.
--- context
The Nabataeans controlled a major part of the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]] and were in the end absorbed by [[place/rome|Rome]]. The Nabataeans stand in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region of the [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and are woven into the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I think of goods that never stopped moving. I think of caravans that left no trace. And I think of the Nabataeans building their monuments in rock, where nothing could move them. Their rock-cut tombs preserved their name. What of mine will stay when the traffic of my days is gone? What stands still is what remembers.
--- question 1
By what path did the Nabataean script grow into Arabic?
