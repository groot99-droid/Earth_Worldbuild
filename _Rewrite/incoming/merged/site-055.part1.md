=== technology/eyeglasses
title: Eyeglasses
src: 7583e2b67177
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Think of eyeglasses as a simple architecture built from glass and metal: corrective lenses held in a frame across the eyes. The device first appears in northern Italy in the late 13th century, then spreads across Europe within decades, giving people whose eyesight weakens with age a practical way to keep reading and doing close work.
--- fact 1
The earliest evidence shows framed convex lenses for correcting farsightedness in the Pisa-Florence area of northern Italy in the late 13th century, commonly dated about 1286-1290.
--- fact 2
The earliest firm evidence comes from a sermon the friar Giordano da Pisa gave in Florence on 23 February 1306, in which he called eyeglasses less than twenty years old and pointed to an unnamed maker.
--- fact 3
No inventor is confirmed. A claim naming "Salvino D'Armati" traces only to an unsupported 1684 book, a claim debunked in 1920.
--- fact 4
Historians debate whether the Pisan friar Alessandro della Spina—who died in 1313 and is credited with independently learning and spreading the technique—was an inventor or merely an early popularizer.
--- fact 5
Early lenses were convex, made for farsightedness; concave lenses for nearsightedness are not documented until the mid-15th century.
--- context
Eyeglasses took shape in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], grown out of Italian craft glassmaking and carried forward by rising literacy among clergy, merchants, and scholars into the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]]. Built from glass-working skill, the device sits on the boundary between [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]], arriving centuries before optical theory could explain why curved glass corrects vision.
--- observer
The Observer reads eyeglasses as tool-lineage doing its plain work: a solution assembled from inherited glass-craft skill, arriving centuries before any pattern-taming account explained why curved glass bends light. This reading weakens if the 1306 sermon turns out to describe something already established rather than a recent novelty. An alternative reading treats the invention as near-simultaneous diffusion among several Italian glassworking towns, rather than a single breakthrough.
--- question 1
Who made the earliest pair, and did Alessandro della Spina invent the technique or only spread it?

=== technology/gunpowder
title: Gunpowder
src: fd128e858f75
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Gunpowder is a mix of saltpeter, sulfur, and charcoal, discovered in China around the 9th century and turned into a military technology under the Song.
--- fact 1
Alchemists searching for elixirs probably discovered it by accident.
--- fact 2
Under the Song dynasty, gunpowder went into fire arrows, bombs, and early firearms.
--- fact 3
It traveled west through the Mongol empire and reached Europe by the 13th century.
--- fact 4
Cannons and guns went on to transform warfare and state power.
--- context
Gunpowder took shape in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], matured under the [[culture/song-dynasty|Song Dynasty]], and spread outward with the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a compound sought by people chasing immortality became instead an instrument of sanctioned harm. A discovery, it turns out, is never bound by its finder's intentions.
--- question 1
Who was the first to discover gunpowder?

=== technology/gutenberg-bible
title: Gutenberg Bible
src: 2d04f1101a9a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Gutenberg Bible is a Latin Vulgate Bible that [[person/johannes-gutenberg|Johannes Gutenberg]] printed in Mainz around 1455 on his movable metal-type press. Regarded as the first substantial European book printed with movable type, most surviving copies are set in 42 lines per column.
--- fact 1
Gutenberg printed it in Mainz, Germany, on the movable metal-type press he developed.
--- fact 2
Most surviving copies are set in 42 lines per column, the detail that gives the book its common name; a few copies use fewer lines.
--- fact 3
A March 1455 letter by the future Pope Pius II describes printed quires of the Bible seen at the Frankfurt fair in October 1454 — the earliest known reference to European printing.
--- fact 4
Exact completion is usually placed at about 1454-1455, though scholars debate the precise finishing date.
--- fact 5
Estimates of the original print run range from about 160 to 185 copies, often cited as roughly 180, printed on both paper and vellum.
--- fact 6
About 49 copies survive today, complete or in part, with roughly 21 considered complete, though exact counts vary by source.
--- context
It was made on Gutenberg's [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]], printed mostly on [[technology/paper|Paper]] with some copies on vellum, in [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] [[region/europe|Europe]]. Its Latin Vulgate text ties it to [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]]; its hand-added decoration links it to the manuscript traditions of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I read the Bible as a step in the tool-lineage. I see a mechanical way to multiply mark-code that once needed a scribe's hand, one copy at a time. I see a meaning-engine's central text, carried now to more households without the matching weight of copying labor.

Was this a mass-production prototype, or only a luxury commission that happened to survive? My reading weakens if it is the second. It weakens, too, if the earlier movable-type printing of East Asia proves the more decisive turn. A copy multiplied is still a story kept alive.
--- question 1
Was completion in 1454, or in 1455?
--- question 2
What are the precise counts for the print run and for the surviving copies?

=== technology/haber-bosch-process
title: Haber-Bosch Process
src: a934d3af564f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Haber-Bosch process combines nitrogen from the air with hydrogen, under high pressure and over a catalyst, to make ammonia. Developed in Germany between 1908 and 1913, it underpins the whole architecture of synthetic nitrogen fertilizer.
--- fact 1
Fritz Haber patented ammonia synthesis in October 1908, then demonstrated a working laboratory apparatus at Karlsruhe in 1909.
--- fact 2
Carl Bosch and engineers at BASF scaled the process up; the first industrial plant, at Oppau, began operating in 1913.
--- fact 3
Haber received the Chemistry Nobel for 1918, awarded in 1919; Bosch later shared the 1931 prize with Friedrich Bergius.
--- fact 4
When the Allied blockade cut off Chilean nitrate during World War I, synthetic ammonia supplied the nitrogen for German explosives instead.
--- fact 5
Haber also organized the first large-scale German chlorine gas release, at Ypres on 22 April 1915.
--- fact 6
Estimates of the share of humanity fed by synthetic nitrogen range from about one third to one half, depending on the assumptions used.
--- context
Synthetic nitrogen underwrote the fertilizer growth of the [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]] and the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]], altering the nitrogen cycle at planetary scale. Its role in explosives ties it to [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. The process arose in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. Threads: [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
Here the species turned the air itself into a tool-lineage input: one reaction now supplies both the Settling's harvests and sanctioned harm. Read this way, part of humanity's numbers rests on a factory chain. This reading weakens if the dependence estimates prove inflated by diet assumptions, or if manure and legumes could have sufficed instead. An alternative view stresses contingency: rival fixation routes existed, so any single-inventor story is too neat.
--- question 1
How much of the fertilizer-linked population gain could have come through other routes instead?
--- question 2
How quickly could low-carbon ammonia replace fossil-derived hydrogen?

=== technology/insulin
title: Insulin
src: 5a1a071147de
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Insulin is a pancreatic hormone that regulates blood sugar. Before a Toronto team purified it across 1921 and 1922, type 1 diabetes was fatal, often within a year or two of diagnosis.
--- fact 1
In the summer of 1921, Frederick Banting and Charles Best, working in J. J. R. Macleod's Toronto laboratory, obtained a glucose-lowering pancreatic extract from dogs.
--- fact 2
Leonard Thompson, aged 14, received the first human injection at Toronto General Hospital on 11 January 1922; James Collip's purer extract then succeeded on 23 January.
--- fact 3
Banting and Macleod won the 1923 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine; Banting split his prize money with Best, and Macleod with Collip.
--- fact 4
Banting, Best, and Collip sold their patents to the University of Toronto for one dollar each in 1923, and Eli Lilly began shipping commercial supply that October.
--- fact 5
In Romania, Nicolae Paulescu published his own pancreatic extract work in 1921 and patented it in 1922; priority between the two efforts is contested, and the Nobel committee did not recognize his claim.
--- context
Insulin belongs alongside [[technology/antibiotics|Antibiotics]], [[technology/vaccination|Vaccination]], and [[technology/anesthesia|Anesthesia]] among the medical technologies that changed survival and care. It grew out of laboratory [[theme/science|Science]] in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. It manages type 1 diabetes, but it does not cure it.
--- observer
Within the species' tool-lineage, insulin shows pattern-taming — animal experiments, chemical purification — yielding a molecule whose patents were handed over to a university. That gesture may work as a status-signal of public duty as much as a commercial choice; an alternative reading sees plain institutional pragmatism instead. Licensing records showing that royalty terms mattered more than the gesture would weaken this.
--- question 1
How should credit really be divided among Banting, Best, Macleod, Collip, and Paulescu?
--- question 2
How much did those one-dollar patents shape early access to insulin?

=== technology/internal-combustion-engine
title: Internal Combustion Engine
src: 1c3f427f57f9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
An internal combustion engine burns its fuel inside its own cylinder, so the expanding gas drives the piston directly, with no external boiler standing between them the way a steam engine needs.
--- fact 1
Etienne Lenoir, a Belgian-born engineer working in France, patented a noisy, inefficient coal-gas engine on 24 January 1860, and hundreds were sold across the 1860s.
--- fact 2
Alphonse Beau de Rochas patented the four-stroke principle in 1862, but he built no engine of his own.
--- fact 3
Nikolaus Otto built a practical four-stroke engine in Germany in 1876; his patent was revoked in 1886 once Beau de Rochas's earlier patent surfaced, though Otto had apparently worked independently.
--- fact 4
Carl Benz applied for a patent on his motor car on 29 January 1886, while Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach built a rival high-speed engine vehicle in those same years.
--- fact 5
Rudolf Diesel filed a German patent application in 1892; prototypes ran from 1893, and an efficient engine ran at Augsburg in 1897.
--- context
The engine displaced steam in road transport and, built lighter, went on to power the [[technology/airplane|Airplane]]. Diesel engines later replaced steam on many [[technology/railways|Railways]], and motor vehicles saw mass use in [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. It built on the [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]] and the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] in [[region/europe|Europe]]. Thread: [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
As tool-lineage, the engine has no single inventor. Lenoir, Beau de Rochas, Otto, Benz, Daimler, and Diesel each inherited and modified earlier partial solutions, and the patent disputes show how credit gets assigned after the fact. This reading would weaken if records showed one design copied unchanged by the rest. An alternative reading stresses cheap petroleum moving through the exchange-web, and machining capacity, more than it stresses any of the inventors themselves.
--- question 1
How much did Lenoir's engine actually shape Otto's later work?
--- question 2
When exactly did engines overtake steam in global freight?
