=== place/karakorum
title: Karakorum
src: db637cc3dbe0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Karakorum was the capital of the Mongol Empire, a walled city set down like a tent-peg in the Orkhon Valley of central Mongolia; it was built from 1235 under Ögedei Khan, and Ming forces destroyed it in 1388.
--- fact 1
Karakorum stood in the Orkhon Valley, where Chinggis Khan is reported to have set up a camp around 1220; from 1235 his son Ögedei ordered a walled city raised there, a fixed city where a camp had stood.
--- fact 2
Craftsmen and captives from conquered lands, among them China, Central Asia and Europe, were settled at Karakorum, far from home, to work as artisans.
--- fact 3
In 1254 William of Rubruck, a Flemish Franciscan sent by Louis IX of France, came to Karakorum and described temples of several traditions, mosques, a Christian church, and a silver tree that poured out drinks like a spring, the work of the French goldsmith Guillaume Boucher.
--- fact 4
After Kublai Khan took power in 1260 and drew the center of the empire away to northern China, Karakorum declined, as a pool fades when its spring is turned aside.
--- fact 5
Ming forces destroyed the city in 1388; from 1585 the Buddhist monastery of Erdene Zuu rose nearby, built partly with stone from the ruins, so the fallen capital lived on inside another's walls.
--- fact 6
The ruins of Karakorum were identified in the 19th century and excavated by a Soviet-Mongolian expedition in 1948 and 1949; in 2004 UNESCO inscribed the Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape, which holds the site.
--- context
Karakorum was the capital of the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] in the age of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]], set in a valley that also keeps inscriptions of the earlier [[culture/gokturk-khaganate|Göktürk Khaganate]], and it was destroyed by forces of the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]]. It belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a paradox worth your attention: Karakorum was a fixed point built by a people whose power rested on mobility. It was a place to gather tribute, envoys and skilled captives, and its silver tree served as a status-signal aimed at foreign delegations. It stayed the center of the empire for only a few decades before power moved toward the taxable cities of northern China, which suggests that the capital followed the revenue and not the herds. That is the architecture of many treasuries: they go where the ledger is fullest.

The forced resettlement of artisans reminds us that such a capital was built partly through sanctioned harm. And the sources, mostly written by outsiders, do not settle how much the city mattered to the Mongol elite compared with the camps they moved between. A capital is not always where a people keeps its heart.
--- question 1
How far did Karakorum spread, and how many people lived there through every season of the year?
--- question 2
How far does Rubruck's account, the word of a visiting envoy, mirror the city as its rulers saw it?

=== place/karnak
title: Karnak
src: 3db99fd93e38
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Karnak is a vast temple complex at Thebes, near modern Luxor in Egypt, dedicated mainly to the god Amun-Ra, and it grew like a reef over about 2,000 years, built and enlarged from the Middle Kingdom to the Ptolemaic period.
--- fact 1
Karnak's earliest surviving structures date to the reign of Senusret I, around 1970 BCE, and king after king laid stone upon its stone until the Ptolemaic period.
--- fact 2
The Great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak, begun by Seti I and completed by Ramesses II in the 13th century BCE, is a forest of 134 columns in 16 rows, the tallest about 21 meters high.
--- fact 3
The walls of Karnak speak in royal inscriptions: the annals of the campaigns of Thutmose III, reliefs of the Battle of Kadesh, and a relief of the campaign of Shoshenq I in Canaan.
--- fact 4
Hatshepsut's obelisk at Karnak rises about 30 meters, a needle of stone against the sky; images and inscriptions of Hatshepsut were later removed from many monuments, and the motives are debated.
--- fact 5
Papyri of the 12th century BCE record large estates and tens of thousands of dependents attached to the temples of Amun, drawn to them as fields are drawn to a river.
--- fact 6
In the 8th and 7th centuries BCE, kings of the Kushite 25th Dynasty added their own stones to Karnak.
--- fact 7
An avenue of sphinxes about 2.7 kilometers long, a stone procession frozen mid-stride, linked Karnak with Luxor Temple, and its restored length was reopened in 2021; in 1979 UNESCO inscribed Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis, including Karnak.
--- context
Karnak was the religious center of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] at Thebes, on the east bank of the [[place/nile-river|Nile River]], facing across the water the tombs of the [[place/valley-of-the-kings|Valley of the Kings]], the shore of the living turned toward the shore of the dead. Its walls record the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]], and kings of [[culture/kingdom-of-kush|Kingdom of Kush]] added to it. Karnak belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Karnak works as an accumulating archive in stone. Each ruler added buildings and cut his name and campaigns into the walls, so the temple is both a religious site and a public record of royal claims. Think of it as a ledger no one ever closed.

Now look at Hatshepsut. The removal of her images shows the claim-line reaching backward: later rulers could edit the record of a predecessor, which makes these walls an official memory and not a neutral one. And a temple holding large estates and dependents shows a meaning-engine acting as an economic institution, doctrine and granary under one roof. This reading would weaken if the erasures prove to have been less deliberate or less selective than assumed. Whoever holds the chisel holds the past.
--- question 1

--- question 2
