=== place/cordoba
src: 1f7dbe8fa411
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Córdoba is a city on the Guadalquivir River in Andalusia, southern Spain, founded by Rome in the 2nd century BCE and best known as the capital of Muslim Iberia and of the Umayyad caliphate of Córdoba (929 to 1031), a lamp lit beside slow water.
--- fact 1
Córdoba, Roman Corduba, was founded in the 2nd century BCE (169 or 152 BCE, a contested date), rose to become the colony Colonia Patricia in 46 to 45 BCE, and later wore the mantle of capital of the province of Baetica.
--- fact 2
Muslim forces took the city in 711 or 712, and in 716 it became the provincial capital of al-Andalus, a new banner over the old river.
--- fact 3
Abd al-Rahman I, an Umayyad prince who escaped the dynasty's overthrow by the Abbasids in 750 like a spark fleeing a fallen hearth, took power in 756; Abd al-Rahman III declared himself caliph in 929, and the caliphate ended in 1031 when Hisham III was expelled.
--- fact 4
The Great Mosque was begun in 785 to 786 and enlarged into the 10th century, growing like a grove tended across generations, and the palace-city of Madinat al-Zahra, begun about 936, was sacked in the civil war of the early 11th century.
--- fact 5
Estimates of Córdoba's population around 1000 CE range from about 100,000 to 1,000,000, and medieval accounts credit its libraries with very large collections, books gathered like water in a cistern.
--- fact 6
Muslims, Christians and Jews lived in the city under Muslim rule, the non-Muslims with a protected but unequal legal status, and historians debate how far the term "convivencia" (coexistence) fits; from this same soil came Ibn Rushd (Averroes) and Moses Maimonides, both born in Córdoba in the 12th century.
--- fact 7
Ferdinand III of Castile took the city on 29 June 1236, and the mosque became a cathedral; from 1523 a cathedral nave was raised inside it, a heart set within another heart.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed the Mosque-Cathedral in 1984, and in 1994 it widened the listing to embrace the whole historic center.
--- context
Córdoba was a provincial capital of the [[culture/romans|Romans]] before it became the seat of the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]]'s Iberian branch in [[culture/al-andalus|Al-Andalus]], a state that claimed the caliphate in rivalry with the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]], two suns contending for one sky. Its conquest belongs to the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. Córdoba belongs to [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] by its earliest founding, with its peak in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], in the region [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]], and it is woven into the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
I walk through the Great Mosque of Córdoba and count the claim-lines held in one structure: a Muslim ruler's place of worship, enlarged over two centuries; then a cathedral, its nave set inside. I read the building as a register of who held the city in each period. I think of a court that gathered scholars, books and craftsmen while claiming the caliphate, a status-signal sent toward rival caliphs.

What can I trust, when the figures for the city's size and its libraries are uncertain? I lean on the stone more than on numbers. And I hold the reading loosely: it would weaken if the court's patronage proves to have been driven mainly by internal politics. A building remembers what a census forgets.
--- question 1
How large was Córdoba in the 10th century, when the estimates of its size differ by a factor of ten?
--- question 2
In daily practice, how did Muslims, Christians and Jews live alongside one another, and how did that shared life change?

=== place/doggerland
src: 4349ebd53546
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Doggerland was a lowland region of hunter-gatherers that joined Britain to continental Europe, lying where the North Sea is today. Then the seas rose. Gradually. Until the land was flooded.
--- fact 1
The last ice age ended. The ice sheets melted. Sea level rose, and the land now under the southern North Sea went down, piece by piece, into the water.
--- fact 2
By around 6000 BCE it was largely underwater. Not all of it: a 2020 study argues that remnant islands held on for centuries after the tsunami, and that the last land went under about a thousand years later.
--- fact 3
Off Norway, the seabed gave way: the Storegga Slide, a huge submarine landslide. Around 6200 BCE its tsunami struck the coasts of the region.
--- fact 4
Fishing vessels drag up what is left. Animal bones. Worked bone points, hauled from the seabed. And below them, mapped by surveys, the submerged river valleys.
--- fact 5
Its inhabitants were Mesolithic hunter-gatherers. People who walked where fish now swim.
--- context
Doggerland is a European counterpart to [[place/beringia|Beringia]]: another lost landscape, drowned by post-glacial sea-level rise. It came after the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]]. After the [[event/younger-dryas|Younger Dryas]]. Then the water. Doggerland sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/europe|Europe]], within the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a landscape where humans once lived now lies under fishing grounds and wind farms, its whole record kept only as the tools that come up in nets. Think of the map you learned by heart, the coast drawn in one confident line. The land bridge between Britain and Europe tells you that line is a draft. Coastlines are provisional; we only ever live on the current edition.
--- question 1
How many people lived there? And as the water kept rising, how did they respond?

=== place/giza-necropolis
src: ba20b5333402
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Giza Necropolis, near Cairo, holds three large pyramids raised for the pharaohs Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure in the 26th century BCE, and beside them the Great Sphinx keeps its long vigil over the sand.
--- fact 1
In its first shape the Great Pyramid of Khufu stood about 146.6 meters high, a mountain made by hands.
--- fact 2
For about 3,800 years no human-made structure stood taller.
--- fact 3
Its workers were organized into labor teams, and the state fed and housed them as a river feeds its fields.
--- fact 4
Of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, it alone still largely stands, the last lamp of that bright company.
--- context
The complex is a mirror for the power of the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] state, which arose from the [[event/unification-of-egypt|Unification of Egypt]] as a river gathers its tributaries into one stream.
--- observer
I stand before a tomb and try to hold its cost in my mind: a state that could mobilize thousands for a decade of work on a single ritual object. I think of the hands. I think of the years. What would I give a decade to? Devotion is measured by what it is willing to spend.
--- question 1
By what craft were the stones lifted and set into place?
