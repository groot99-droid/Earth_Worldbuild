--- summary
The Kingdom of Benin was a West African forest state of the Edo people, ruled by an oba from Benin City, that flourished from about the 13th century until British forces conquered it in 1897. Its story shows how a kingdom builds its own architecture of order, and how that architecture can be broken from outside.
--- fact 1
The kingdom flourished from the 13th to the 19th century in the forest region of West Africa, in what is now southern Nigeria. Its founding story is a covenant: in tradition, the Edo invited Prince Oranmiyan of Ife to rule in the 13th century, and his son Eweka became the first oba.
--- fact 2
Under Oba Ewuare the Great (reigned about 1440 to 1480), the kingdom expanded and became highly organized, with guilds of craftsmen. He built up the army, rebuilt the royal compound, and gave Benin City moats and great walls: an architecture of order, set down on the ground.
--- fact 3
In late December 1896 nine British colonial officials, led by James Robert Phillips, set out as a party for Benin City. On 4 January 1897, near the village of Ugbine, Edo chiefs ambushed and killed them.
--- fact 4
From 9 to 18 February 1897 a British force of about 1,200 men under Sir Harry Rawson carried out the Benin Expedition. It occupied the capital after sustained resistance, burned parts of the city and palace, and removed large quantities of sacred and royal art.
--- fact 5
The looted works, known as the Benin Bronzes, were sold to defray the costs of the expedition, so that what was taken paid for the taking. Oba Ovonramwen was captured and exiled to Calabar, and the kingdom was incorporated into British Nigeria.
--- context
To understand the conquest of Benin, set it inside the wider [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]]. The kingdom was also a contemporary of the [[culture/kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]], and it traded with Europeans during the period of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]].
--- observer
Sit with this for a moment. The Observer records a state's sacred and royal art taken by force and sold to pay for the force that took it. That is not only a theft; it is an architecture of loss that keeps standing. Where such objects are held is itself a claim about who may tell the story they carry, and the continuing argument over their display shows how long a seizure can outlast the event. A seizure is not over when the fighting is.
