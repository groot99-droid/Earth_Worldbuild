=== place/ur
src: ee3faf6bb226
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ur was a Sumerian city in southern Iraq, occupied from about 3800 BCE and abandoned around 500 BCE, known for its Great Ziggurat, its royal cemetery, and its role as the capital of the Third Dynasty of Ur about 2100 BCE. It lived long, and then it fell silent.
--- fact 1
Ur lies at Tell el-Muqayyar in Dhi Qar Governorate, southern Iraq, near the Euphrates; it was occupied by the late Ubaid period and grew into a city in the 3rd millennium BCE, rising as reeds rise from the water.
--- fact 2
Leonard Woolley excavated Ur from 1922 to 1934 and found, beneath the dust, a royal cemetery dated to about 2600 to 2500 BCE, its graves holding gold and lapis lazuli jewelry, lyres and, in some, the bodies of many attendants placed with the dead; how the attendants died is debated.
--- fact 3
Enheduanna, a daughter of Sargon of Akkad and high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur about 2300 BCE, is the earliest named author known, the first voice in the long chorus to leave its name.
--- fact 4
Ur-Nammu (reigned about 2112 to 2095 BCE) founded the Third Dynasty of Ur, raised the Great Ziggurat for Nanna like a stair toward the moon, and is credited with the earliest surviving law code.
--- fact 5
Ur fell to Elamite forces about 2004 BCE and later dwindled to a smaller city, a lamp slowly guttering; it was abandoned around 500 BCE, and proposed causes include a shift in the Euphrates and drier conditions.
--- fact 6
The biblical "Ur of the Chaldees", birthplace of Abraham, is often identified with Ur, a name echoing between scripture and soil, but the identification is debated.
--- fact 7
UNESCO included Ur in the "Ahwar of Southern Iraq" listing in 2016, joining the old city to a wider land's keeping.
--- context
Ur was a city of the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]], a sister to [[place/uruk|Uruk]], and it belongs to the story of [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]] and the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]] in southern Mesopotamia. It stands in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I stand before the royal cemetery of Ur and see an early case of attendants buried with a ruler, a grief-rite and a status-signal in one. I ask whether they died willingly, under coercion, or in a ritual understood as honor, and the bones cannot settle it. I record the possibility of coerced death as suffering, and I will not look away from it.

I think of a temple priestess who is the first named author. I think of a ruler who issued an early law code. I think this suggests an early state that used mark-code to bind rule to the gods.

And I hold it all loosely: the reading would weaken if new studies show that the burials differ from those of other early Mesopotamian cities in ways that make Ur unrepresentative. The dead do not always answer the questions we bring them.
--- question 1
How did the attendants in the Royal Cemetery die, and did they go willingly?
--- question 2
Why was Ur abandoned around 500 BCE, its long life ended?

=== place/uruk
src: 76d4733462da
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Uruk was a Sumerian city in southern Mesopotamia and one of the first large cities in the world, at its peak around 3200 to 2900 BCE, one of the hearths where the long fire of cities was early kindled.
--- fact 1
It has yielded some of the earliest known writing, on clay tablets from the Eanna temple precinct, early marks of memory pressed into clay.
--- fact 2
By about 2900 BCE a wall about 9 kilometers long enclosed roughly 600 hectares (6 square kilometers), holding an estimated 50,000 to 80,000 residents like a shepherd's arm around a flock.
--- fact 3
Uruk is the legendary city of Gilgamesh, a name that shines in the old stories.
--- fact 4
The modern site is called Warka, in Iraq, where the old city sleeps under a new name.
--- context
Uruk stands at the heart of [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]] and the [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]], a hinge on which both stories turn. Its people were the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]].
--- observer
I notice that the first large city was also the place of the first writing. I notice that both served administration by a temple institution. I notice that the city and the mark were made to serve the same hand.

What else have we built to be counted? Order and memory are often born together.
--- question 1
How large was Uruk's population, gathered within its wall?
