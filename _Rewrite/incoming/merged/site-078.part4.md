=== place/ayutthaya
src: ffce8cfcb2eb
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ayutthaya is a city on an island at the meeting of three rivers in central Thailand, traditionally founded in 1351, the capital of the Ayutthaya Kingdom until the Burmese army destroyed it in 1767, and a major trading port of Southeast Asia.
--- fact 1
Ayutthaya lies on an island at the confluence of the Chao Phraya, Lopburi and Pa Sak rivers, and is traditionally said to have been founded in 1351, by convention 4 March 1351, by King U Thong, Ramathibodi I, though the settlement may be older.
--- fact 2
In the 1430s Ayutthaya's forces attacked Angkor and installed a short-lived puppet ruler there, though they did not sack the city.
--- fact 3
Chinese, Japanese, Persian, Indian and Malay merchants traded at Ayutthaya; the Portuguese, in 1511, were the first Europeans to reach it, the Dutch East India Company opened a trading post in 1608, and King Narai exchanged embassies with the French court in the 1680s.
--- fact 4
European visitors in the 17th century described a city of canals, palaces and many temples, and some estimates put its population at several hundred thousand at its height.
--- fact 5
A Burmese army besieged Ayutthaya for about 14 months and took it in April 1767, burning much of the city; many thousands of captives were taken to Burma, and much of the kingdom's written record was lost.
--- fact 6
Taksin re-established a Siamese state after 1767 and the capital moved to Thonburi and then to Bangkok in 1782.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Historic City of Ayutthaya in 1991.
--- context
Ayutthaya was the capital of the [[culture/ayutthaya-kingdom|Ayutthaya Kingdom]], which took over the region of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] and [[place/angkor|Angkor]], and it traded with the [[event/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ayutthaya was a trading capital raised on an island where rivers and canals served as moat, road and marketplace at once, and where merchants of many origins met under one ruler. The destruction of 1767 stripped away much of the kingdom's own written record, so a large part of what you can know about it now comes from foreign visitors' accounts, a gap that shapes how the city is understood today. The taking of captives is recorded here as suffering as well as loss of skill and labor. This reading would weaken if surviving Thai and Burmese records prove richer than assumed.
--- question 1

--- question 2
How much of Ayutthaya's own written record survived 1767, and where is it held?

=== place/baghdad
src: b2782d525633
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Baghdad is a city on the Tigris River in Iraq, founded in 762 CE by the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur as a round capital and, for about five centuries, the seat of the Abbasid caliphs and a leading center of learning.
--- fact 1
Baghdad was founded in 762 by the caliph al-Mansur as a round city called Madinat al-Salam, "City of Peace," near the older Sasanian capital Ctesiphon; no part of the Round City survives above ground.
--- fact 2
Baghdad became the center of the Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom, and paper mills were operating there by the 790s.
--- fact 3
Population estimates for Baghdad in the 9th and 10th centuries run from several hundred thousand to over a million, which would have made it one of the largest cities in the world.
--- fact 4
Baghdad remained the seat of the Abbasid caliphs until 1258, when Mongol forces under Hulagu took the city after a siege and killed the caliph al-Musta'sim; chronicles report figures ranging from 200,000, Hulagu's own estimate, to as much as 2,000,000 in Muslim sources, figures modern historians regard as unreliable and likely inflated by later epidemics, though the massacre and destruction were severe.
--- fact 5
Baghdad was under Ottoman rule from 1534, with Safavid periods before and after, until British forces captured it in 1917; it became the capital of the Kingdom of Iraq in 1921.
--- fact 6
In April 2003, after the US-led invasion, looting of the Iraq National Museum removed about 15,000 objects, some of which have since been recovered.
--- context
Baghdad was the capital of the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] and the home of the [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]]; it was taken in the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]] and later ruled by the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Baghdad was a planned city that became a knot in a wider network: books, translators, paper and scholars from several languages made it a place where Greek, Persian and Indian sources were gathered and rewritten, memory kept and passed on at scale. The Round City itself is now known only from texts, since later buildings covered it, while the losses of 1258 and 2003 show you that stored knowledge stays exposed to political violence. Historians debate how far the court and how far private patrons drove the translation work, and this reading would weaken if private patronage proves to have been the larger force.

=== place/bukhara
src: 85e2bea3a983
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Bukhara is a city in the Zarafshan oasis of Uzbekistan, a Sogdian trading city, capital of the Samanid dynasty, and for more than a thousand years a center of Islamic learning on the Silk Roads.
--- fact 1
Bukhara has been settled for a long time; the city celebrated 2,500 years in 1997 on a traditional dating, while archaeological evidence places its walled Sogdian settlement by about the 6th century BCE.
--- fact 2
Bukhara was a Sogdian city when Arab armies took it in 709 to 710, and the scholar Muhammad al-Bukhari, compiler of a major collection of hadith, was born there in 810.
--- fact 3
Under the Samanid dynasty, 819 to 999, Bukhara was a capital of learning and of Persian literature; the physician and philosopher Ibn Sina was born near it in 980, and the Samanid mausoleum was built in the late 9th to 10th century.
--- fact 4
The Mongol army under Chinggis Khan took Bukhara in 1220; the city was burned and many of its people were killed or enslaved, though the number is unknown.
--- fact 5
The Kalyan Minaret, built in 1127, still stands, and Bukhara was the capital of the Emirate of Bukhara, 1785 to 1920, which became a Russian protectorate by 1873 after Russia conquered key Bukharan territories in 1868.
--- fact 6
The Red Army took Bukhara in 1920, and the citadel, the Ark, was destroyed after four days of fighting.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Historic Centre of Bukhara in 1993.
--- context
Bukhara was a [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] city, paired in later history with [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]], and it fell to the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]] after coming under the world of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
I count the powers that took Bukhara in turn — Sogdian, Arab, Samanid, Mongol, Uzbek, Russian, Soviet — and still the city rose again each time as a place of learning, faster, it seems, than its walls could be rebuilt.

I think its standing as a seat of Islamic scholarship was a story that outlasted every one of its rulers.

But I would doubt this if the record shows long gaps in scholarly output after the sack of 1220, gaps I cannot see from here.
