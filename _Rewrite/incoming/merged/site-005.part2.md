=== place/chichen-itza
src: 774a9a74a150
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chichen Itza is a major Maya-Toltec city on the Yucatan Peninsula that flourished from about 800 to 1100 CE, crowned by its stepped pyramid, El Castillo, which climbs toward the sky like a staircase built for the sun.
--- fact 1
It lies in the northern Yucatan, and it prospered there as many southern lowland cities declined and guttered like spent candles.
--- fact 2
El Castillo, the Temple of Kukulcan, climbs by 91 steps on each of its four stairways, and with the top platform they total 365, counted out like beads on a string.
--- fact 3
At the spring and autumn equinoxes, sunlight draws a serpent-like shadow upon the staircase, a snake woven of light and shade.
--- fact 4
Here lies the largest ball court in the Americas, about 166 meters (545 feet) long, and a sacred cenote, a well of dark water, used for offerings.
--- fact 5
In 1988 its name was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list.
--- context
Chichen Itza thrived while the [[event/classic-maya-collapse|Classic Maya Collapse]] reshaped the [[culture/maya|Maya]] world as a flood reshapes a valley, and it is often set beside the later capital [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]. Chichen Itza belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I stand among the crowd that gathers today to watch. The pyramid seems built for this one thing, a moving shadow laid on a staircase at a particular hour. I came for the calendar. I stayed for the show. I leave holding both. Why should one stone be asked to do two things at once? Some things are built to count time and to be watched while they count.
--- question 1
How much of the equinox effect did its builders intend, and how much was the sun's own doing?

=== place/constantinople
src: fe06f94a1174
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Constantinople was founded in 330 CE by Emperor Constantine on the site of Byzantium, and it stood as the capital of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire, a crown set between two seas, until the Ottoman conquest in 1453.
--- fact 1
In 537, under Emperor Justinian, the Hagia Sophia was completed, a prayer given the weight of stone.
--- fact 2
In 1204 the city was sacked by the Fourth Crusade, its riches scattered like leaves in a gale.
--- fact 3
On 29 May 1453 it fell to Mehmed II, and the long Byzantine day came to its dusk.
--- fact 4
It sits on the Bosphorus, a hinge between Europe and Asia; today it bears the name Istanbul.
--- context
The city outlived the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] by almost a thousand years, a sister lamp burning on after the western one had guttered. In time it became the capital of the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]], the same stones wearing a new crown.
--- observer
The Observer notes that geography kept the city valuable to every ruler. Think of a doorway everyone must pass through: whoever stands in it holds the house. A strait that joins two seas and two continents is a permanent asset. Rulers come and go; the water stays.
--- question 1
When Constantinople fell, did history turn on a hinge, or did a long decline simply reach its last page?

=== place/cusco
src: 71701b641b47
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cusco is a city high in the Andes of southern Peru, at about 3,400 meters, which Inca tradition says was founded around 1200 CE and which served as the capital of the Inca Empire, a heart of stone among the peaks, until the Spanish took it in 1533.
--- fact 1
Cusco lies at about 3,400 meters in the Peruvian Andes; Inca tradition credits its founding to Manco Cápac around 1200, but archaeology shows that the Killke culture lived in the region from about 900 to 1200, and that the city grew slowly, like a root through stone, before Pachacuti.
--- fact 2
Under Pachacuti, whose reign is traditionally dated from 1438, and under his successors, Cusco was the center of the Inca Empire: the Coricancha (Temple of the Sun) shone at its ritual heart, and the fortress of Sacsayhuamán kept watch above the city.
--- fact 3
Spanish accounts, above all Bernabé Cobo's in the 17th century, describe 41 lines called ceques running out from the Coricancha like the spokes of a wheel, linking about 328 shrines (huacas).
--- fact 4
Some writers say the city was laid out in the shape of a puma, stone given the body of a great cat, a claim that remains debated.
--- fact 5
Francisco Pizarro's force entered Cusco on 15 November 1533, and in 1536 to 1537 Manco Inca besieged the Spanish there for about ten months, a tide that rose and rose and could not take the city.
--- fact 6
The Spanish raised churches and houses on Inca foundations; an earthquake in 1650 threw down almost all the colonial buildings, and one in 1950 damaged more than a third of the city's structures, while many Inca walls stood fast, as if the mountain remembered them.
--- fact 7
In 1781, during his rebellion against Spanish rule, Túpac Amaru II was executed in Cusco's Plaza de Armas.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed Cusco in 1983, and in 2017 the city was home to 428,450 residents.
--- context
Cusco was the capital of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] and the seat of [[person/pachacuti|Pachacuti]], whose reign is linked to the empire's widening, and it fell in the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-inca-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]]. Today it is the usual gateway to [[place/machu-picchu|Machu Picchu]], and it was there that [[person/tupac-amaru-ii|Túpac Amaru II]] was executed. Inca administrators kept their records on knotted cords, memory tied into string: the [[technology/quipu|Quipu]]. Cusco belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Cusco was arranged as a map of belief and rule at once. Think of it as an architecture you could read with your feet: the ceque lines tied kin groups and shrines to the capital, and the Coricancha set the ritual center where the emperor's authority sat, a story-glue that could be read by walking the city. Its Inca walls outlasted the earthquakes that felled Spanish buildings, and the Spanish built on top of them, so a later claim-line rests on an earlier one.

We should hold this lightly. Much of what we know of the plan comes from Spanish writers with their own aims, because Inca knotted-cord records are only partly understood. The reading would weaken if excavations show the ceque system was less regular than Cobo's account implies. A city is a ledger, and each new hand writes over the last.
--- question 1
In practice, how regular was the ceque system, and how much of Cobo's account mirrors Inca practice rather than Spanish understanding?
--- question 2
How much of Cusco's Inca plan and stonework still lies sleeping beneath the colonial city?
