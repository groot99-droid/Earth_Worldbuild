=== culture/haudenosaunee-confederacy
title: Haudenosaunee Confederacy
src: 917b2ca78e64
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy is a political union of Indigenous nations in the northeastern woodlands of North America, formed centuries before European contact, many strands braided into one cord.
--- fact 1
The original five nations were the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca; in the 18th century the Tuscarora joined them, a new strand in the braid.
--- fact 2
The Great Law of Peace is the oral constitution of the confederacy, a law carried in memory and voice rather than ink.
--- fact 3
Clan mothers hold the power to appoint and remove chiefs, the roots deciding which branches grow.
--- fact 4
Estimates of the founding date range from about 1142 to the 15th century, and scholars disagree; the beginning lies in a mist of centuries.
--- context
The confederacy stands as an example of a durable non-state political union in [[region/north-america|North America]], a union that endured without a throne. Its influence on the U.S. founding is debated.
--- observer
We assume that only force or a crown can hold rival peoples together. The Observer notes a federation formed from warring peoples through a shared founding story. That is the architecture of a covenant: a story told together until it becomes a house to live in. Story-glue can bind peoples as well as kings can.
--- question 1
In what century, or what year, was the confederacy formed?
--- question 2
How deeply did it shape colonial political thinking?

=== culture/hittites
title: Hittites
src: ce3cd371f517
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Hittites were a Bronze Age people of Anatolia who built an empire outward from their capital Hattusa, and the words on their lips were the earliest attested Indo-European language.
--- fact 1
Hattusa lies near modern Bogazkale in central Turkey, and its archive of cuneiform tablets slept in the earth until it was found in the early 20th century.
--- fact 2
Hittite is the oldest recorded Indo-European language, and in 1915 Bedrich Hrozny showed its Indo-European nature, unlocking a voice long mute.
--- fact 3
According to the conventional chronology, Hittite forces raided Babylon around 1595 BCE, falling on it like a sudden storm.
--- fact 4
The Hittites fought Egypt at the Battle of Kadesh around 1274 BCE, and later, around 1259 BCE, they made a treaty with Ramesses II, the sword set aside for the seal.
--- fact 5
Around 1200 to 1180 BCE the empire collapsed in the Bronze Age Collapse, its world falling with an age.
--- context
The Hittites stand as a key witness to the early spread of Indo-European languages, the tale told in [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. They contested power with the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] at the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]] and fell in the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]]. In the codex they stand in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region of the [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and three threads run through them: [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
Ask yourself what the future will remember of you, and then ask what it will be written on. The Observer notes that an empire that dominated Anatolia and treated with pharaohs was forgotten for millennia and rediscovered through clay tablets. The lesson is humbling. The species' memory of its own past depends heavily on which materials happen to last. Memory is a ledger kept by chance.
--- question 1
What part did drought, famine or invasion play in bringing the empire to its end?

=== culture/inca-empire
title: Inca Empire
src: a3c5b21fb5ea
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu) was the largest state of the pre-Columbian Americas, ruling millions of people along the spine of the Andes from about 1438 until the Spanish conquest in 1533.
--- fact 1
Its capital was Cusco, and its road system ran roughly 30,000 to 40,000 kilometers, threads spun across the mountains.
--- fact 2
It kept its records on knotted cords called quipu, memory tied into string.
--- fact 3
The state exacted labor service (mit'a) rather than money taxes, a tribute paid in seasons of work, not in coin.
--- fact 4
Before Pizarro's arrival, civil war and smallpox had already weakened it.
--- context
Its tale runs through [[place/machu-picchu|Machu Picchu]] and the heights of the [[region/andes|Andes]]. Its fall is bound to the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
--- observer
I think of millions of people moving in one pattern. I think of no money markets, no conventional script. I think of labor owed and cords knotted for the record, and of how that held it all together. What is money, if a cord can do its work? Order can be woven as well as written.
--- question 1
Can the quipu carry a story as well as numbers?

=== culture/indus-valley-civilization
title: Indus Valley Civilization
src: 45af1e2adf63
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Indus Valley Civilization (also called the Harappan) was a Bronze Age urban culture of the Indus River basin, at its height around 2600 to 1900 BCE, when its cities stood beside the river like ordered constellations.
--- fact 1
Among its major cities were Harappa and [[place/mohenjo-daro|Mohenjo-daro]].
--- fact 2
Its cities had planned streets and drainage, laid out as if by one patient hand, and goods were made to standard weights.
--- fact 3
Its trade reached as far as Mesopotamia.
--- fact 4
Its script has not been deciphered; the signs keep their silence.
--- context
The civilization stands as a parallel case to [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]] in Mesopotamia, a second flame lit to the east, in the lands of [[region/south-asia|South Asia]].
--- observer
I walk the planned streets in my mind and look for the palace. I look for the royal monument. I look for the king. I find few obvious ones, and the puzzle stays with me. Did I assume that cities always come with kings? This large urban society challenges that assumption. A city can keep its order without a crown.
--- question 1
What do the undeciphered inscriptions say, behind their silence?
--- question 2
Why did the cities fade into decline?

=== culture/inuit-and-the-thule-expansion
title: Inuit and the Thule Expansion
src: b045ae0c2cdc
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Thule people, ancestors of the Inuit, spread from Alaska across the Canadian Arctic to Greenland beginning around 1000 CE, moving over the ice like a tide, and replaced the earlier Dorset culture.
--- fact 1
The Thule culture emerged in the Bering Strait region around 900 to 1000 CE, born where two shores face each other across the water.
--- fact 2
Its people used dog sleds, skin boats (umiaks and kayaks) and toggling harpoons to hunt whales and seals in the cold sea.
--- fact 3
They pressed east across Arctic Canada and reached Greenland by roughly the 12th to 13th century, following the ice toward the sunrise.
--- fact 4
The genes tell the tale: genetic studies show that the earlier Dorset population was largely replaced rather than assimilated.
--- fact 5
Norse colonists lived in southern Greenland from about 985 CE until the 15th century, a small light on a far shore.
--- context
The Thule expansion carried people whose origins lay in [[place/beringia|Beringia]]'s region across the Arctic, and brought them face to face with the [[culture/norse|Norse]]. Its scale is comparable to the pastoral traditions of the [[culture/yakut-sakha|Yakut (Sakha)]] in Siberia. In the codex it stands in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region of [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]], and its threads are [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I think of the boat. I think of the sled. I think of the harpoon. I think of how a hunting technology suited to ice let a small population occupy a huge cold region in just a few centuries. What carried them so far, if not their numbers? Technology, not numbers, set the pace of the expansion.
--- question 1
What brought about the Dorset population's decline?
