=== place/nalanda
src: 3e02ab271628
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nalanda is an archaeological site in Bihar, India, the ruin of a Buddhist monastic university founded in about the 5th century CE, a lamp that drew students from across Asia until it was sacked around 1200.
--- fact 1
Nalanda was founded in about the 5th century CE, traditionally under the Gupta ruler Kumaragupta I, and grew like a banyan into a large monastic university.
--- fact 2
The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang studied at Nalanda in the 630s and 640s and reported about 10,000 residents; another Chinese pilgrim, Yijing, set down his own account of it in 675.
--- fact 3
Students and visitors came from China, Tibet, Korea, Central Asia and Southeast Asia, streams running to one sea, and in the 9th century a ruler of Srivijaya funded a monastery at Nalanda.
--- fact 4
In its halls Nalanda taught Buddhist philosophy, logic, grammar and medicine.
--- fact 5
Around 1200 forces under Bakhtiyar Khalji sacked Nalanda; later traditions say its library burned for months, and historians debate how completely it was destroyed and whether its decline began earlier.
--- fact 6
In 2016 UNESCO inscribed the Archaeological Site of Nalanda Mahavihara, and in 2014 a new Nalanda University opened, a fresh lamp lit from an old name.
--- context
Nalanda was founded under the [[culture/gupta-empire|Gupta Empire]], was visited by pilgrims from the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]] and upheld by rulers of [[culture/srivijaya|Srivijaya]], and it fell during the raids that came before the [[culture/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]]. It stands in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and it is woven into the theme threads of [[theme/science|Science]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I think of teachers, texts and students gathered from across Asia into one place. I think of monks carrying texts and methods to China and Tibet along an exchange-web of ideas. I think of memory-outsourcing held in an institution, not in a single book.

Its loss around 1200 tells me that a store of knowledge depends on the institution that keeps it. Is the end of a school ever only one night of flame? The loss of an institution can be slower and less dramatic than later traditions of one great fire suggest.

My reading would weaken if new evidence shows that Nalanda's decline was mainly the result of reduced patronage before its sack. A library lasts only as long as the hands that keep it.
--- question 1
How much of Nalanda's fading came before the sack of around 1200?
--- question 2
How large was the library, and what treasures of text did it hold?

=== place/nara
src: 53b0a1dd2c06
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nara is a city in Japan's Kansai region that was the capital, then called Heijō-kyō, from 710 to 784 CE, an age in which the Japanese court drew Chinese models of city planning, writing and state Buddhism into its own ground, as a field drinks from a borrowed river.
--- fact 1
In 710, under Empress Genmei, Heijō-kyō was established as the capital, laid out on a grid plan modeled on the Tang capital Chang'an, a mirror held up to a distant city.
--- fact 2
At Nara court scholars compiled the chronicles Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720), setting them down in Chinese characters.
--- fact 3
A smallpox epidemic swept through in 735 to 737, and some historians have estimated that it killed a quarter to a third of Japan's population, though the figure is uncertain.
--- fact 4
In 743 Emperor Shōmu ordered a giant bronze Buddha, which was dedicated at Tōdai-ji in 752; the statue rises about 15 meters, a mountain of bronze.
--- fact 5
The Shōsōin storehouse at Tōdai-ji keeps objects from the 8th-century court as amber keeps a leaf, among them items that carry the memory of trade along the Silk Roads.
--- fact 6
The court moved to Nagaoka in 784 and to Heian-kyō (Kyoto) in 794, a departure often attributed in part to the political influence of Buddhist institutions at Nara.
--- fact 7
In 1998 UNESCO inscribed the Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara, and the old capital's temples passed into the world's keeping.
--- context
Nara was fashioned in the image of [[place/chang-an|Chang'an]], the capital of the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], and the capital's crown later passed to [[place/kyoto|Kyoto]]. It stands in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and it is woven into the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I watch a court importing a whole order at once. I count its parts: a Chinese city plan, Chinese characters, a state-sponsored Buddhism. I count the chronicles too, memory-outsourcing that fixed the ruling house's origin story in writing.

And I notice the timing. The Great Buddha was ordered a few years after a smallpox epidemic that killed a large part of the population, which suggests that a ruler facing mass death turned to a meaning-engine and to public works. But that link is an inference from the sources, and I hold it loosely. When the dying will not stop, what do we build, if not something larger than ourselves?

My reading would weaken if the Buddha project proves to have been mainly a response to court rivalries. Grief looks for a shape to stand in.
--- question 1
How many died in the epidemic of 735 to 737, and how did those losses bend the court's plans?
--- question 2
Why did the court leave Nara behind in 784?
