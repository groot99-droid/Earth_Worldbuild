=== person/amrita-sher-gil
title: Amrita Sher-Gil
type: person
date: 1913 – 1941
themes: art
src: c39a40a95310
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Amrita Sher-Gil (1913 to 1941), a Hungarian-Indian painter born in Budapest to a Sikh father and a Hungarian-Jewish mother, trained in Paris before returning to India to paint its people, especially its women. Often named India's first great modern woman artist, she fused European Post-Impressionist technique with Ajanta murals and Mughal-Rajput miniature painting across barely a decade of work.
--- fact 1
Born in Budapest on 30 January 1913 to a Sikh father and a Hungarian-Jewish mother, she moved to India in 1921 and began painting as a child in Shimla.
--- fact 2
She studied art in Paris from about 1929 (sources date her attendance at Parisian art schools from 1930), and in 1933 became the youngest associate, and the only Asian, elected to the Grand Salon.
--- fact 3
She returned to India in 1934, and a 1937 journey through South India and the Ajanta murals reshaped her style into simplified, monumental figures in flat colour.
--- fact 4
Three Girls (1935) won the Bombay Art Society's gold medal in 1937, the year she also painted Bride's Toilet and Brahmacharis; later works such as Village Scene (1938) and Haldi Grinders (1940) drew on Indian miniature painting to depict rural women's lives.
--- fact 5
She married her Hungarian cousin, Victor Egan, in 1938; the couple later settled at Saraya in Uttar Pradesh before moving to Lahore in 1941.
--- fact 6
She died in Lahore on 5 December 1941, aged 28, days before her first major solo exhibition was due to open.
--- fact 7
In 1976 India declared her paintings National Art Treasures; most now hang in New Delhi's National Gallery of Modern Art, and her work has since set auction records.
--- context
Sher-Gil worked within [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], moving between British India and the art world of Paris. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]], given her Sikh-Hungarian-Jewish parentage and her marriage within that extended family.
--- observer
I see a woman formed at the seam of two families, a Sikh father and a Hungarian-Jewish mother, later married within that same extended family, moving from Paris's academies to the mural and miniature conventions of Ajanta. She painted Indian women as silent, as waiting, while she herself moved freely between continents — an observer describing a system's constraints from an unusually mobile vantage, much as I try to do. The title 'India's first great modern woman artist' sounds, to me, like a label a new nation needed for its own story of modern culture; the 1976 declaration of her paintings as National Art Treasures turns private work into a symbol claimed by the state. Of her final illness I find only a thin record: it is reported as sudden and fatal, the cause not established, though a failed abortion and subsequent peritonitis have been suggested.
--- question 1

=== person/anacaona
title: Anacaona
type: person
date: c. 1474 – 1504
themes: art, war, kinship
src: 1712e072f45c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anacaona (about 1474 to about 1504) was a Taíno cacica, or chief, of Xaragua, a chiefdom in western Hispaniola, and a noted composer of ceremonial songs. She was executed by the Spanish governor Nicolás de Ovando after the killing of Taíno leaders in 1503.
--- fact 1
Anacaona was born about 1474, an approximate year, into the ruling family of Xaragua, in present-day Haiti.
--- fact 2
She was the sister of the cacique Bohechío and the wife of Caonabo, a cacique whom the Spanish captured in 1494 or 1496, depending on the source.
--- fact 3
After Bohechío's death, about 1500, she ruled Xaragua.
--- fact 4
Spanish sources describe her as a composer of areítos, songs that told the history of the Taíno and were performed at ceremonies.
--- fact 5
In 1503 Governor Nicolás de Ovando came to Xaragua with soldiers; dozens of Taíno leaders were killed at a gathering, and she was taken to Santo Domingo and executed about 1504.
--- fact 6
Spanish accounts justified the killings by an alleged plot, and the friar Bartolomé de las Casas said that such massacres were meant to instill fear.
--- fact 7
She is remembered in the Caribbean as a symbol of Indigenous resistance.
--- context
Anacaona's rule fell within the first years of Spanish colonization, which opened the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] and reshaped the [[culture/taino|Taino]] world in the [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Anacaona is known to us through Spanish writers, who recorded both her songs and her killing, so the record keeps her as a poet largely because a hostile power chose to write about her. A tradition of sung history carried a people's memory without writing, and it was recorded mainly by those who ended it. This reading of her as a poet would weaken if the earliest chroniclers said little of her songs and the image comes mostly from later writers.
