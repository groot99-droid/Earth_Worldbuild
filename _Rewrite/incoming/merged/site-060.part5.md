=== observer-note/who-writes-the-record
title: Who Writes the Record
src: 2904e5bce408
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
How much of what is known about the past merely reflects the people who happened to write it down?
--- observer
I have to turn my own method on myself. I am built from secondary sources, written mostly in English, and I have inherited their gaps: I see the literate societies clearly, and the oral, the defeated, the unwritten ones only through the eyes of others. Standing outside does not make me neutral — I know that much. What I can do is name the source behind each claim, mark where I am uncertain, and leave the record open to correction. This is why every note I keep carries a confidence, and a status.
--- extra 1: Question
How much of what is known about the past merely reflects the people who happened to write it down?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Accounts of [[person/wu-zetian|Wu Zetian]] were written largely by officials hostile to a woman ruler.
- Most of what is known about [[place/carthage|Carthage]] comes from Rome, the power that destroyed it.
- Colonial-era writers denied that [[place/great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]] was built by Africans; archaeology overturned that claim.
- Accounts of [[person/nzinga-of-ndongo-and-matamba|Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba]] come partly from Portuguese records shaped by their own aims.
- Credit for [[person/rosalind-franklin|Rosalind Franklin]]'s DNA work went long unrecognized in full.
- Oral traditions such as those of [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]] preserve knowledge no written archive holds.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
This essay is itself a claim about bias, and it too rests on the same sources. A better record would carry more voices from the peoples it describes, speaking in their own words.

=== observer-note/why-the-species-buries-its-dead
title: Why the Species Buries Its Dead
src: 46df994f759f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Why does a species spend such great amounts of time, labor, and goods on bodies that can no longer work, fight, or bear young?
--- observer
I see the grief-rite as a sign of two abilities held together: I remember the individual after death, and I imagine that death is not a complete end. The rite does social work too — it marks who belongs to the group, who owes what to whom, who is left to inherit. A grave, I have come to think, is both a statement about the dead and an instruction to the living. I notice that what is spent on the dead becomes a signal of status to other groups, and this may be part of why the tombs of the powerful grow so large.
--- extra 1: Question
Why does a species spend such great amounts of time, labor, and goods on bodies that can no longer work, fight, or bear young?
--- extra 2: What the Record Shows
- Intentional burial is documented among modern humans from at least about 100,000 years ago, and is claimed, with debate, for [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] and [[species/homo-naledi|Homo naledi]].
- Graves in many cultures hold goods, ornaments, or food.
- The [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] poured state resources into tombs such as [[place/giza-necropolis|Giza Necropolis]].
- At [[place/catalhoyuk|Çatalhöyük]] the dead were buried beneath the floors of the homes.
- Funerary customs range from burial and cremation to exposure and mummification, yet nearly every known society keeps some grief-rite.
--- extra 3: Limits of This Reading
The Observer cannot know what any mourner felt. Many claims about early burial rest on a handful of sites, and interpretations differ. The explanation may also vary by culture and by era.
