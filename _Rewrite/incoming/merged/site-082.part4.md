=== culture/igbo-ukwu-culture
src: af380ca1730e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Igbo-Ukwu, a site in southeastern Nigeria, yielded in 1938 an extraordinary hoard of intricately cast bronze and copper objects dated to roughly the 9th and 10th centuries CE, evidence of a sophisticated early West African society and among the earliest documented bronze-casting traditions in the region.
--- fact 1
A Nigerian farmer, Isaiah Anozie, accidentally discovered the first Igbo-Ukwu bronzes in 1938 while digging a cistern on his property, and formal archaeological excavation led by Thurstan Shaw between 1959 and 1964 uncovered more than 700 additional high-quality copper, bronze and iron artifacts across three excavation areas.
--- fact 2
Radiocarbon dating placed the main excavated site at around 850 CE, and the surviving artifacts, dated broadly to the 9th and 10th centuries CE, include naturalistic bronze representations of animals such as a leopard's skull, flies and snails, along with vessels modeled after calabash gourd forms.
--- fact 3
The excavations also recovered an enormous quantity of glass, carnelian and stone beads, alongside pottery, textile remains and ivory, indicating that the society at Igbo-Ukwu maintained long-distance trade connections bringing exotic materials into the region.
--- fact 4
The people of Igbo-Ukwu, ancestors of the present-day Igbo people, are considered among the earliest metalworkers of copper and its alloys in West Africa, and among the earliest in the region to employ the lost-wax casting technique used to produce their distinctively detailed bronze sculptures.
--- fact 5
The wealth, craft specialization and sophistication evident at Igbo-Ukwu, achieved without evidence of a large centralized state or monumental architecture comparable to some other African civilizations, has led scholars to view it as an important example of complex social organization developing through a different, less centralized political structure.
--- context
Igbo-Ukwu's metalworking sophistication connects to the broader spread of [[technology/iron-smelting|Iron Smelting]] technology and metallurgical knowledge across Sub-Saharan Africa in this period. Igbo-Ukwu Culture sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I think of a farmer digging a cistern in 1938 and finding, by accident, a bronze-casting skill that rivaled contemporary traditions elsewhere in the world at the time — and I wonder how much of even an advanced people's history can stay unknown until a chance find brings out the proof.

No great palace stood here, no monument I can point to, and yet the wealth and standing this craft implies grew without the centralized state that this record's other studies of complex society often assume as a must.

A specialist could argue the missing monument is only missing from the dig so far, not from the truth of the place — that incomplete excavation, not a genuinely decentralized society, is why I see none here. If so, I should hold what I have said about the society more loosely.
--- question 1
What kind of social and political organization actually produced and distributed the wealth evident in the Igbo-Ukwu bronze hoards, given the absence of an obvious centralized state structure?
--- question 2
How far did Igbo-Ukwu's trade connections extend, based on the origins of the glass, carnelian and other imported materials found at the site?

=== culture/joseon-dynasty
src: 831474f5dd31
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Joseon Dynasty, founded by Taejo Yi Seong-gye in 1392 after overthrowing the preceding Goryeo dynasty, ruled Korea for over five centuries as the world's longest-lasting Confucian monarchy, transforming Korean society, law and culture around Confucian principles before ending with the 1897 declaration of the Korean Empire.
--- fact 1
General Yi Seong-gye overthrew the Goryeo dynasty and founded the Joseon Dynasty in July 1392, taking the throne name Taejo, and the new dynasty relocated its capital to modern-day Seoul by 1393, then known as Hanseong.
--- fact 2
Joseon rulers deliberately restructured Korean society, law, education and family life around Confucian principles, displacing the greater influence Buddhism had held during the preceding Goryeo period and establishing what became history's longest-lasting Confucian dynasty.
--- fact 3
King Sejong the Great, reigning in the 15th century, oversaw major cultural and scientific achievements, most notably the creation of Hangul, a Korean writing system designed to be easier to learn than the Chinese characters previously used to write Korean.
--- fact 4
Joseon Korea endured devastating foreign invasions, including major Japanese invasions in the 1590s and Manchu Qing invasions in the 17th century, both of which caused significant destruction and loss of life before Joseon rule was restored.
--- fact 5
King Gojong declared the end of the Joseon-era Kingdom of Korea and the founding of the Korean Empire in October 1897, formally ending the Joseon Dynasty after over 500 years, though the Korean Empire itself was subsequently annexed by Japan in 1910.
--- context
Joseon rulers restructured Korean society explicitly around [[culture/confucianism|Confucianism]], continuing a Korean dynastic tradition reaching back through [[culture/silla|Silla]], and the dynasty's most celebrated cultural achievement, the Hangul writing system, was created under King Sejong the Great. Joseon Dynasty sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I think of Sejong building Hangul letter by letter, on purpose, for people who had never found Chinese characters easy to hold — a rare, deliberate script raised from nothing, meant from the start to let ordinary Koreans read and write in ways the old characters had kept from anyone without an elite education.

I think, too, of this same dynasty organizing itself around Confucian kinship principles, reshaping family structure, inheritance and social hierarchy according to a single tradition's prescriptions, for over five centuries — proof that one religious tradition could restructure a society's most intimate arrangements when the state commits to it across many generations.

A specialist could argue that Hangul was taken up far more slowly and unevenly among ordinary Koreans than its later fame suggests, with Chinese characters still ruling among the learned for centuries after — and if so, I should not call its creation an immediately transformative, democratizing act.
--- question 1
How widely and how quickly was Hangul actually adopted by different social classes during the Joseon period itself, as against its later elevated status in modern Korean national identity?
--- question 2
How did the transition from Goryeo's Buddhist-influenced society to Joseon's Confucian restructuring actually unfold at the level of ordinary family and community life?
