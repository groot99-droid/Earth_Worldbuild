=== place/goree-island
src: 53ca11d8501d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Gorée Island is a small island off Dakar, Senegal, held in turn by the Portuguese, Dutch, British and French from the 15th century, and a symbol of the Atlantic slave trade, though historians debate how many enslaved people left from it.
--- fact 1
Gorée Island lies about 2 kilometers off Dakar; the Portuguese landed in 1444, the Dutch held the island by 1628, exactly when and how they replaced the Portuguese is unknown, since the early Dutch West India Company archive was largely lost, and the French captured it from the Dutch in 1677, after which control passed between Britain and France several times over the following century.
--- fact 2
Gorée was a trading post for goods such as gum and hides and for enslaved people; a community of Afro-European merchant women, called signares, ran trade and property on the island, and some owned enslaved people.
--- fact 3
The House of Slaves, built by Nicolas Pépin at a date given variously as about 1776 or 1780 to 1784, and having a doorway to the sea called the "Door of No Return," is a pilgrimage site for visitors.
--- fact 4
Many historians argue that Gorée was a modest embarkation point compared with larger ports on the Atlantic coast and that the House of Slaves did not hold the numbers often claimed, while others stress its symbolic importance; the debate is unresolved.
--- fact 5
African merchants and rulers took part in the trade alongside European ones.
--- fact 6
Visitors to Gorée have reportedly included Nelson Mandela and Pope John Paul II, and, most firmly documented, US President Barack Obama in 2013.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Island of Gorée in 1978.
--- context
Gorée Island was a small port within the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]], which ended in its legal form with the [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]]. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], its first European contact, and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Gorée is a case where a place's symbolic weight and its documented role differ: as a memorial it stands for the whole of the Atlantic slave trade, while the records suggest a smaller share of captives left from it than from larger ports. You can hold both at once, since a memorial's power to hold grief does not depend on the count, and the Observer records the suffering of the enslaved without ranking sites by numbers. The signares show a household and property system in which African women held power within the trade, a reminder that the trade was embedded in local societies. This reading would weaken if new archival counts show that Gorée handled a much larger share of the trade than current estimates suggest.
--- question 1
How many enslaved people left from Gorée, and in what years?
--- question 2
How should memorial sites present both their documented role and their symbolic one?

=== place/harappa
src: 225705f7bdc2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Harappa is an archaeological site in Punjab, Pakistan, on a dry bed of the Ravi River, occupied from about 3300 BCE and a major city of the Indus Valley Civilization until its urban phase declined after about 1900 BCE.
--- fact 1
Harappa was occupied from about 3300 BCE, and its urban "Mature Harappan" phase ran from about 2600 to 1900 BCE, with population estimates ranging from as many as 23,500 in site-specific studies to a wider 30,000 to 60,000 suggested for the largest Indus cities generally.
--- fact 2
Harappa had planned streets, brick drains and standardized weights, and its bricks followed a common size ratio used at other Indus sites.
--- fact 3
Harappa's seals, carved with animals and signs in a script that has not been deciphered, are found in many Indus cities, and Indus objects such as seals and carnelian beads have been found in Mesopotamian cities.
--- fact 4
Daya Ram Sahni began excavations at Harappa in 1921, after British engineers had taken bricks from the site in the 1850s for railway ballast.
--- fact 5
The city's urban phase declined after about 1900 BCE, and occupation continued at a smaller scale; proposed causes include changes in monsoon rainfall and river courses, and the causes are debated.
--- context
Harappa gave its name to the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], whose other great city was [[place/mohenjo-daro|Mohenjo-daro]], and its planned streets belong to the story of [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Harappa left thousands of inscribed seals no one can yet read, so its people's own account of themselves is missing, and what you can know of its order comes instead from bricks, weights and drains that suggest a shared administrative logic. The lack of obvious palaces or royal tombs has led some scholars to propose that its rule differed from that of contemporary Mesopotamia and Egypt, though the proposal is debated. This reading would weaken if the script is deciphered and shows a more conventional royal order.
--- question 1
What do the signs on the Harappan seals say?
--- question 2
Why did the urban phase of Harappa decline after about 1900 BCE?

=== place/hispaniola
src: 402f2b477a9e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hispaniola is the second-largest island of the Caribbean, in the Greater Antilles, divided between Haiti in the west and the Dominican Republic in the east, and the site of the first lasting European settlement in the Americas.
--- fact 1
Hispaniola covers about 76,000 square kilometers; its features include Pico Duarte, reported at about 3,101 meters, the highest peak in the Caribbean, and Lake Enriquillo, a saline lake whose depth below sea level is not firmly established by the sources consulted.
--- fact 2
The earliest evidence of human presence on Hispaniola is dated by many archaeologists to about 4000 BCE, though estimates vary; by 1492 the island was home to Taíno societies organized into several chiefdoms, and Spanish chroniclers report the names Ayiti, Quisqueya and Bohío for it.
--- fact 3
Columbus reached Hispaniola in December 1492, and the Spanish founded La Navidad, destroyed in 1493, La Isabela in 1493, and Santo Domingo in 1498.
--- fact 4
The Taíno population of Hispaniola declined sharply after 1492 under forced labor, violence and disease.
--- fact 5
In 1697 Spain ceded the western third of Hispaniola to France, which developed the sugar and slave colony of Saint-Domingue; the Haitian Revolution, 1791 to 1804, ended slavery and French rule there.
--- fact 6
The Dominican Republic became independent in 1844, after Haitian rule over the whole island from 1822.
--- context
Hispaniola links the [[culture/taino|Taino]], the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], the city of [[place/santo-domingo|Santo Domingo]], the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]], its earliest human presence, and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one island now holds two states, two languages and two colonial histories because a treaty between distant European powers drew a claim through it in 1697, with no reference to anyone already living there. Hispaniola compresses the whole Atlantic system into one place: an indigenous society, a first European base, a plantation colony built on enslaved labor and a successful revolution of enslaved people that founded a state, all within about three centuries. The pattern is not unique, since other Caribbean islands show parts of it, and a specialist could weaken this reading by showing that Hispaniola was more exceptional or more ordinary than it suggests.
--- question 1
When did people first reach Hispaniola, and from where?
--- question 2
How large was the Taíno population of the island in 1492?
