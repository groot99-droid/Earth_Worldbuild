--- description
Gustav Klimt (14 July 1862 to 6 February 1918) was an Austrian painter and the leader of the Vienna Secession. His golden, patterned paintings of women and love, above all The Kiss, are icons of turn-of-the-century Vienna. His career shows what it costs, and what it gains, to break from an institution.

He was born at Baumgarten near Vienna, the second of seven children of a gold engraver. He won a scholarship to the Vienna School of Arts and Crafts in 1876 and, with his brother Ernst and Franz Matsch, painted murals and ceilings for the Burgtheater and the Kunsthistorisches Museum. He began inside the establishment.

In 1897 he became the first president of the Vienna Secession, a group breaking from the official academy with the motto "To every age its art, to art its freedom". A motto is a small doctrine you can carry.

His ceiling paintings for the University of Vienna, showing Philosophy, Medicine and Jurisprudence, caused a scandal over nudity and pessimism. He withdrew from the commission, and the paintings were destroyed in a fire in 1945.

He never married, had a lifelong companionship with the designer Emilie Flöge and fathered several children with models. He supported younger artists such as Egon Schiele and Oskar Kokoschka.

He died in Vienna after a stroke. In his "golden period" (about 1899 to 1910) he applied real gold and silver leaf, drawing on Byzantine mosaics he saw in Ravenna in 1903, and on Japanese and Egyptian art. Key works include The Kiss (1907 to 1908) and the Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I (1907).

=== people--hilma-af-klint--1

--- description
Hilma af Klint (26 October 1862 to 21 October 1944) was a Swedish painter who, from 1906, made hundreds of abstract paintings guided by her spiritual beliefs, several years before Kandinsky, Mondrian and Malevich. She kept them largely hidden, like seeds held in a closed hand, and they reached an international audience only from the 1980s.

She was born at Karlberg Palace in Solna, near Stockholm. She studied at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Stockholm from 1882 to 1887 and lived by painting landscapes, portraits and botanical illustrations.

Her spiritual searching, which sources date from 1879, grew around the time of the death of her younger sister Hermina in 1880 and drew her toward spiritualism and Theosophy. With four other women, who began as members of the Edelweiss Society, she made up "The Five," which held séances and tried automatic drawing, which the sources date to 1896.

In 1906, believing she had received a commission from a spiritual guide, she began the Paintings for the Temple, a cycle of nearly 200 works completed in 1915. She considered herself a medium through whom the works were made.

In 1908 she showed her work to Rudolf Steiner, and his cool answer contributed to a four-year break in the series. Between 1921 and 1930 she spent long periods at the Goetheanum, the Anthroposophical Society's headquarters, and filled more than 125 notebooks, with sources giving figures from 125 to more than 150.

She believed the world was not ready for the paintings, and she directed that they not be shown until 20 years after her death. She died after a traffic accident, leaving more than 1,200 paintings and drawings to her nephew.
