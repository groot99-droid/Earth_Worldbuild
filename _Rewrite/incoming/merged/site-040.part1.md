=== event/bronze-age-collapse
src: 3a409052ddf4
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Around 1200 BCE, the eastern Mediterranean and the Near East came apart. State after state, within a few decades. The palace economies of the Late Bronze Age did not slow. They stopped.
--- fact 1
The Hittite Empire fell. Mycenaean palaces were destroyed. Ugarit burned.
--- fact 2
Egyptian inscriptions record attacks by the 'Sea Peoples', around 1177 BCE.
--- fact 3
Proposed causes: drought. Earthquakes. Migrations. The breakdown of trade networks.
--- fact 4
Egypt survived. But weakened.
--- context
The [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] ends here. The [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] follows. The [[culture/minoans|Minoans]]' successors did not escape it. Neither did the Aegean world.
--- observer
The Observer reads the Bronze Age collapse as a lesson in architecture: when many states lean on the same trade routes and the same diplomatic ties, that structure can fail all at once rather than state by state. History keeps returning to this lesson, era after era.
--- question 1
Which factor mattered most?

=== event/blling-allerd-warming
src: 41c31808cb2d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The world warmed like a held breath released: the Bølling-Allerød was an abrupt warm interval that began about 14,700 years ago and lasted until about 12,900 years ago, the first great thaw after the Last Glacial Maximum. It ended as swiftly as it came, giving way to the cold that took the name Younger Dryas.
--- fact 1
The warm interval ran from about 14,700 to 12,900 years ago, rising out of the Oldest Dryas, the last cold stage in the long retreat from the Last Glacial Maximum.
--- fact 2
In the north the warming came on abrupt and strong; in the south, the same years brought a slight cooling, a pattern later seen again in other sudden swings of climate.
--- fact 3
Boreal forest and birch woodland crept across Western Europe and the North European Plain, over Doggerland itself, and the tundra retreated before them.
--- fact 4
Within this interval the ice itself gave way: a pulse of rapid sea-level rise called Meltwater Pulse 1A began as the Laurentide and Eurasian ice sheets shed their mass quickly, raising the seas by roughly 10 to 20 meters over a few centuries.
--- fact 5
The interval broke once, briefly: the Older Dryas, a cold spell of one to two centuries whose signal is strongest in northern Europe, and scholars still debate whether it touched the whole world or only a region of it.
--- fact 6
It closed as abruptly as it opened, with the onset of the [[event/younger-dryas|Younger Dryas]], a cold spell that held until about 11,700 years ago, when the Holocene began.
--- context
The Bølling-Allerød lies within the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]], after the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]] and before the [[event/younger-dryas|Younger Dryas]] and the dawn of the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the climate you inherited did not warm the way a room warms: a sharp rise, then a cold interruption lasting a century, then a second abrupt swing back toward near-glacial cold, all inside about three thousand years. The steadiness that later made farming and cities possible had to wait for these swings to end. This reading would weaken if closer dating showed the Older Dryas was only a local signal, without wider reach.
--- question 1
Was the Older Dryas a global cooling event, or only a regional one?
--- question 2
Which ice sheet contributed most to Meltwater Pulse 1A?

=== event/cambrian-explosion
src: fe783ed5d992
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In a span the fossil record reads as a heartbeat, roughly 13 to 25 million years beginning 538.8 million years ago, the Cambrian Explosion brought forth most of the major animal body plans that would ever appear.
--- fact 1
Creatures armored themselves: shells and exoskeletons appearing widely among the animals of the age.
--- fact 2
The Burgess Shale in Canada and Chengjiang in China are the famous windows into this menagerie.
--- fact 3
Yet the Ediacaran came before, its precursors showing that animals already existed before the Cambrian dawned.
--- fact 4
Oxygen's rise, an arms race between predator and prey, and the deep grammar of developmental genetics are all named among the possible causes.
--- context
The event defines the base of the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]]. Its signature animals include the [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]].
--- observer
The Observer reads this as a burst of experimentation with body design, followed by a long conservatism that has held ever since. Innovation seems to arrive in clusters, and its aftermath clusters too.
--- question 1
Is the 'explosion' partly a trick of what the fossil record happened to preserve?

=== event/campanian-ignimbrite-eruption
src: 26f30ebec050
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Near Naples, Italy, the Campi Flegrei caldera let go. About 39,850 years ago. The Campanian Ignimbrite eruption — the largest explosive blast Europe had seen in roughly the last 200,000 years. Its ash reached the Mediterranean. It reached Eastern Europe. Its role in the Neanderthal decline around the same time is debated.
--- fact 1
A 2017 study fixed the date. Radiocarbon. Argon. A synchronized varve chronology, layer counted against layer. The eruption: about 39,850 years ago. Precise.
--- fact 2
Roughly 181 to 265 cubic kilometers of magma. A Volcanic Explosivity Index of 7. The largest eruption Europe had produced in about 200,000 years.
--- fact 3
Its ash — the Y-5 layer — spread over about 3 million square kilometers. It still marks time in sediment cores, from the Mediterranean to the Russian Plain.
--- fact 4
It fell close to the onset of Heinrich Event 4, a severe cold phase. But the ash postdates that onset — by roughly 700 to 800 years. The simple story, eruption triggers cooling, does not hold cleanly.
--- fact 5
Climate models found the cooling and the acid deposition lasted only one to two years. And the worst of the cold largely missed Western Europe — the ground where the last Neanderthal populations still lived.
--- fact 6
At several Italian sites, modern-human-associated stone tools appear stratigraphically before the ash layer. Some recalibrated Neanderthal extinction dates predate the eruption, too. Still, some researchers argue it shaped cultural change further east, where the modeled cooling was stronger.
--- context
It falls within the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]], near the start of the [[era/upper-paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]]. It is often discussed alongside the [[event/neanderthal-extinction|Neanderthal Extinction]] and the decline of [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] — though a direct link is contested. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer names this pairing — a great eruption beside a Neanderthal decline near it in time — an old temptation: reach for one dramatic cause to explain a slow disappearance. The dating and the modeling done since have mostly weakened that story rather than confirmed it, and the Observer reads that as a caution against single-cause explanations for extinctions generally. This reading would weaken if new modeling showed that the strongest cooling did, after all, reach the last Neanderthal populations.
--- question 1
Did the eruption meaningfully affect Neanderthal populations anywhere, even where its coldest effects missed the west?
--- question 2
How exactly does its timing relate to the onset of Heinrich Event 4?

=== event/carboniferous-coal-forests
src: 2d1da0273c7d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In the wet tropical lowlands of the later Carboniferous, roughly 323 to 299 million years ago, swamp forests rose and rotted and rose again, and their buried remains became much of the world's coal.
--- fact 1
They grew chiefly across the lowlands of the northern continent of Euramerica and in parts of China: club-mosses grown tall as trees, among them Lepidodendron, alongside tree ferns, giant horsetails, and the first conifers.
--- fact 2
The sea rose and fell again and again, bound to the Late Paleozoic Ice Age, drowning the swamps and baring them once more, and layering coal seam upon coal seam.
--- fact 3
At Joggins in Nova Scotia, hollow fossil tree stumps still hold the remains of early amphibians and reptiles within them.
--- fact 4
The burial of so much plant carbon lowered the air's carbon dioxide and probably raised its oxygen. Long after, coal drawn from these very rocks fuelled the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] in Britain and other regions.
--- fact 5
About 305 million years ago the wet tropical forests broke apart into isolated patches as the climate turned drier and cooler — an event linked to declines among amphibians and the rise of reptiles.
--- context
These forests follow the [[event/devonian-forests-and-the-rise-of-trees|Devonian Forests and the Rise of Trees]] and belong to the [[era/carboniferous-period|Carboniferous Period]]. Their end coincides with the [[event/assembly-of-pangaea|Assembly of Pangaea]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer marks a difference in rates: these forests stored their carbon across tens of millions of years, and you have burned much of it back out in a few centuries. This reading concerns rates alone, and it would not change no matter how the debate over why so much coal formed here is settled.
--- question 1
Why did so much coal accumulate in this period and not in others?
--- question 2
What caused the collapse of the tropical forests about 305 million years ago?
