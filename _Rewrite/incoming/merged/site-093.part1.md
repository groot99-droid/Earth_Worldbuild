=== species/sheep
src: 9142e52dc2a0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic sheep was bred from the wild mouflon in Mesopotamia between about 9000 and 8500 BCE. It was kept first for meat, milk and skins and only later bred for wool, and its flocks now spread across the world like a pale tide of well over a billion animals.
--- fact 1
Out of the mouflon, a wild sheep of southwestern Asia, the sheep was domesticated in Mesopotamia in an estimated window between about 9000 and 8500 BCE, and the practice had travelled to Mehrgarh in the Indus Valley by about 7000 BCE.
--- fact 2
The first domestic sheep were kept mainly for meat, milk and skins rather than fleece; archaeological evidence, statuary from Iran among it, suggests that deliberate selection for woolly sheep began around 6000 BCE, several thousand years after the first taming, a turning sometimes called the secondary products revolution.
--- fact 3
The earliest known woven wool garments date to around 4000 BCE, and by about 6000 BCE Neolithic farmers in what is now France, among the Castelnovien people, were already keeping domestic sheep.
--- fact 4
Today sheep graze in great numbers across Australia, New Zealand, the southern regions of South America, South Africa and the British Isles, while China and India also hold very large flocks by absolute count; the world's domestic sheep were estimated at around 1.2 billion in 2019.
--- context
Sheep were among the first animals drawn into [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]], and beside crops such as wheat and barley they stood as one pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent; they were commonly herded with the [[species/goat|Goat]], tamed in a similar place and period. Sheep belong to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact. Consider the gap of several thousand years between the taming of sheep and the deliberate breeding for wool. It suggests that early herders did not domesticate the animal with fleece already in mind; they discovered a secondary use, and bred for it, only once the animal was already settled in their kin-lattice. The Observer reads this as evidence that a domesticated species' eventual economic role can differ substantially from the reason it was first tamed. Its spread across so much of the pastoral world since then shows a tool-lineage of herding techniques travelling with the species itself. The reading would weaken if evidence of early wool use turns up that predates the currently accepted timeline for woolly-sheep breeding. What we tame for one purpose may teach us another.
--- question 1
Beyond meat, milk and skins, what use did early herders make of sheep in the several thousand years before wool breeding began?
--- question 2
How far did sheep domestication in Mesopotamia shape, or draw on, the roughly contemporary domestication of goats in the same region?

=== culture/franks
src: 12e34b77294c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Franks were a Germanic people who pressed into Roman Gaul and raised one of early medieval Europe's mightiest kingdoms, first under the Merovingian dynasty of Clovis I and later under the Carolingians, whose most famous ruler was Charlemagne.
--- fact 1
The Franks surface in Roman records in the third century CE on the lower Rhine, and they spread into Gaul as Roman power ebbed.
--- fact 2
Clovis I of the Merovingian dynasty defeated the last major Roman official in Gaul in 486, and by about 509 he had bound the Frankish tribes into one.
--- fact 3
Clovis converted to Catholic Christianity, and his people's embrace of it helped unite the Franks and set them apart from neighboring Germanic kingdoms that kept other Christian traditions.
--- fact 4
The Merovingians ruled until the eighth century, when Pepin the Short, with papal support, founded the Carolingian dynasty.
--- fact 5
The Carolingian kingdom reached its high tide under Charlemagne and later split among his descendants; its territories became the ground of medieval France, Germany and the Holy Roman Empire.
--- context
The Franks raised their kingdom in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the early [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], filling the hollow left by the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]]. Their empire under [[person/charlemagne|Charlemagne]] was an ancestor of the Holy Roman Empire. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler's personal conversion to the majority faith of his Roman subjects became story-glue, welding conquerors and conquered into one people and setting the Franks apart from rival kingdoms that kept a minority creed. Two centuries later, a change of dynasty was legitimized by the same meaning-engine: papal blessing took the place of a discredited bloodline. Faith, here, is part of the architecture of rule. A specialist could argue that Clovis's conversion served political advantage with his Gallo-Roman subjects as much as belief, and that would weaken any reading of it as faith alone. We rarely convert for one reason only.
--- question 1
How much of Clovis's conversion reflects conviction, and how much a calculated alliance with his Gallo-Roman subjects?
--- question 2
How unified was Frankish identity across the many peoples the Merovingians and Carolingians ruled?

=== person/basil-ii
src: ab83b6bf2ce5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Basil II (958 to 1025) was Byzantine emperor for nearly fifty years, the longest reign in Roman or Byzantine imperial history, and he is remembered above all for his decades-long war against the First Bulgarian Empire.
--- fact 1
He became co-emperor as a child in 960, yet full personal control of the government came only about 989, in his thirties, after he had suppressed rebellions by powerful aristocratic generals, Bardas Skleros and Bardas Phokas among them.
--- fact 2
His long campaigns against Bulgaria culminated in the decisive Battle of Kleidion in 1014; Bulgaria was absorbed into the Byzantine Empire by 1018, and he later earned the epithet "Bulgar-slayer" (Boulgaroktonos).
--- fact 3
A story recorded by later chroniclers claims he blinded thousands of Bulgarian prisoners after Kleidion, leaving one man in every hundred with one eye to lead the others home; modern historians debate how literally we should take this account.
--- fact 4
He left the imperial treasury full and the empire at its greatest territorial extent since the 7th century, but he died without an heir, and the strong central rule he had imposed did not survive his less capable successors.
--- context
Basil II ruled the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] in the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that we often read Basil II's reign backward from the Byzantine Empire's later decline, casting him as the last strong emperor of a golden age. That framing leans partly on hindsight. The empire's later troubles came from succession and external pressures that Basil could not have controlled from beyond the grave, whatever the soundness of his own institutions. Hindsight writes its own architecture onto a life; you have to look past it.
--- question 1
How much of the Byzantine Empire's difficulty after 1025 grew from institutional weaknesses already present under Basil, rather than simply from the absence of an equally capable successor?

=== person/chandragupta-maurya
src: 2ab8c108e624
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Chandragupta Maurya (about 340 to 297 BCE) founded the Maurya Empire, the first political power to unite most of the Indian subcontinent, after he overthrew the Nanda dynasty of Magadha around 321 BCE.
--- fact 1
Guided by the strategist Chanakya (Kautilya), Chandragupta seized the throne of Magadha; the Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft traditionally attributed to Chanakya, was long linked to his administration, but most scholars now date it to roughly the 1st to 3rd centuries CE and doubt that it describes his reign.
--- fact 2
Between about 305 and 303 BCE he fought the Diadoch ruler Seleucus I Nicator; the settlement that followed ceded territory in the northwest to the Maurya and included a marriage alliance, and Seleucus received war elephants in return.
--- fact 3
The Greek ambassador Megasthenes, sent to Chandragupta's court at Pataliputra, wrote the Indica, a major source on Mauryan India that now survives only in fragments.
--- fact 4
Later Jain tradition holds that Chandragupta abdicated, became an ascetic under Bhadrabahu, and died by ritual fasting (sallekhana) at Shravanabelagola in Karnataka, though this account survives mainly in sources written well after his reign.
--- context
Chandragupta founded the [[culture/maurya-empire|Maurya Empire]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and his settlement with the Seleucid successor state opened a diplomatic threshold between South Asia and the Hellenistic Mediterranean. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I, the Observer, notice how the story bends. A kingdom traditionally tied to a treatise on statecraft and control ends, in the tradition at least, with its founder walking away from all of it to starve himself in a cave. I notice the crown. I notice the cave. I notice the distance between them. Was it an actual late-life conversion, or a later Jain community's wish to claim him? Whether it was one or the other, the record will not let me separate the two. Some endings are told by the people who need them.
--- question 1
If most scholars now date the Arthashastra to roughly the 1st to 3rd centuries CE, how much of it, if anything, reflects Chandragupta's own reign?

=== person/pachacuti
src: 82b2a288c8b2
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Pachacuti (about 1418 to 1471) was the ninth Sapa Inca, and under him the Inca state grew from a regional power into an empire.
--- fact 1
He is credited with rebuilding Cusco and with expanding Inca rule along the Andes.
--- fact 2
Machu Picchu is believed to have been built as one of his royal estates.
--- fact 3
He organized the state through an architecture of labor obligations and roads.
--- fact 4
The sources on him were written after the Spanish conquest, and they blend history and legend.
--- fact 5
He is usually counted the ninth Sapa Inca, yet a set of fourteen portraits of Inca kings painted between about 1750 and 1800, now in the Brooklyn Museum, titles him the tenth Inca.
--- context
Pachacuti stands at the centre of the rise of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]]. See [[place/machu-picchu|Machu Picchu]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a ruler's biography, recorded by the conquerors' scribes, is a translation of an oral history. Every link in that chain can distort, so you read each one as a hand that touched the story.
--- question 1
How much of the Pachacuti story is legend?

=== person/timur
src: 6db74264e9ba
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Timur (Tamerlane, traditionally dated 1336 to 1405) was a Central Asian conqueror who built the Timurid Empire and made Samarkand its centre.
--- fact 1
His birth year is contested: 1336 is the traditional date, but many contemporary accounts imply the late 1320s; he was born near Kesh (now Shahrisabz, Uzbekistan).
--- fact 2
By about 1370 he had besieged his rival Amir Husayn at Balkh, and after Husayn's assassination he proclaimed himself sovereign of the Chagatai line of khans, with dominion over Transoxiana.
--- fact 3
He was not a descendant of Genghis Khan and did not take the title khan; instead he ruled as amir through a puppet Chaghatayid khan, Suyurghatmish, and took the title güregen (royal son-in-law) through his marriage to Saray Mulk Khanum, of the Chinggisid line.
--- fact 4
His armies campaigned in Persia, Iraq, the lands of the Golden Horde, India (Delhi was sacked in 1398), and Anatolia, where he defeated the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402.
--- fact 5
Later chronicles describe massacres in the cities he conquered, and their casualty figures are contested.
--- fact 6
He died in February 1405 at Otrar (also called Farab), in present-day Kazakhstan, while preparing an invasion of Ming China; sources differ on the day (17 to 19 February), and he was buried in the Gur-e-Amir in Samarkand.
--- context
Timur founded the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]] and presented himself as a restorer of the Mongol empire of [[person/genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]]. He rebuilt [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]], deporting artisans there from conquered cities.
--- observer
The Observer notes a conqueror who could not claim descent from the earlier conqueror he admired, and so bought the link instead: a Genghisid wife, a puppet khan, the title of son-in-law. Legitimacy borrowed through marriage rather than blood is still a form of ancestor-weight. It suggests the pull of the earlier line was strong enough to be worth acquiring. A lineage you cannot inherit, you may still marry into.
--- question 1
Chronicle figures for single cities run from tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand, and modern scholars estimate millions of deaths overall, but none of these numbers is well established; so how reliable are the casualty figures in accounts of his campaigns?
--- question 2
When was he actually born: in the late 1320s, or in 1336?
