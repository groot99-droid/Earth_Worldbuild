=== people--hammurabi--1
src: 0c327758f9e0
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Hammurabi was king of Babylon from about 1792 to 1750 BCE. He gathered much of Mesopotamia under one hand, and his name endures in a famous inscribed law code.

The Code of Hammurabi, carved on a stone stele about 1754 BCE, sets down 282 laws.

Its penalties shift with social class, and it holds to the principle of proportionate punishment, weight answering weight.

The stele stood in public view, so that the law could be seen as well as spoken.

The original now rests in the Louvre in Paris.

=== people--hatshepsut--1
src: e4d7afa3f425
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Hatshepsut was an Egyptian ruler of the 18th Dynasty who bore the title of pharaoh, sharing the throne with Thutmose III, until her death in 1458 BCE. Her reign is traditionally dated 1479 to 1458 BCE, and she is one of the first women known to have held that office.

At first she acted as regent for her young stepson Thutmose III. Then, around the seventh year of his reign (about 1473 BCE), she took the title of pharaoh herself, sharing his count of years.

She sent a trading expedition south to Punt, on the Red Sea coast of Africa.

Her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari stands as a major monument against the cliffs of time.

Many of her images were later removed or defaced, possibly after her death, as if someone had tried to wash a name from the stone.

=== people--piye--1
src: cf73a185bf9b
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Piye (reigned about 744 to 714 BCE) was a king of Kush who came down the river and conquered Egypt, and he is usually counted as the first ruler of Egypt's Twenty-fifth (Kushite) Dynasty. His birth year is unknown; the dates given here are those of his reign.

He ruled Kush from Napata, near Jebel Barkal in present-day Sudan. His reign is dated about 744 to 714 BCE, and some scholars count his father Kashta as the dynasty's first ruler instead.

About 728 BCE he marched north against Tefnakht, prince of Sais, and a coalition of Delta rulers, and broke them.

His Victory Stele, found at Jebel Barkal in 1862 and now in Cairo, tells the campaign in Egyptian hieroglyphs, presents him as the servant of the god Amun, and names the rulers who bowed to him.

He did not stay in Egypt. He returned to Kush, leaving local rulers in their places like stones left standing in a field.

He enlarged the temple of Amun at Jebel Barkal.

=== people--ashurbanipal--1
src: 94307b69bf0c
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Ashurbanipal (about 685 to about 631 BCE) was king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire from 669 BCE. He is remembered for his conquests, for the royal library at Nineveh, and for palace reliefs where war and lion hunts unroll across the stone.

Born about 685 BCE, a son of Esarhaddon, he became king of Assyria in 669 BCE, while his brother Shamash-shum-ukin was made king of Babylon.

In 663 BCE Assyrian forces sacked Thebes in Egypt, and Kushite rule in Egypt ended.

In 652 BCE Shamash-shum-ukin rebelled, and after a long war Ashurbanipal took Babylon in 648 BCE. In campaigns of about 647 to 646 BCE he defeated Elam, and Susa was destroyed in the campaign of about 646 BCE.

His libraries at Nineveh, found in the 19th century, held tens of thousands of clay tablets and fragments, a buried sea of signs, among them a version of the Epic of Gilgamesh. He boasted of his own scribal education.

His inscriptions and reliefs record brutal treatment of defeated enemies, including mass deportation, mutilation and execution.

Ashurbanipal (about 685 to about 631 BCE) held the kingship of the Neo-Assyrian Empire from 669 BCE. His name is bound to his conquests, to the royal library at Nineveh, and to palace reliefs of war and lion hunts.

He was born about 685 BCE, a son of Esarhaddon, and was crowned king of Assyria in 669 BCE, his brother Shamash-shum-ukin set over Babylon.

In 663 BCE Assyrian forces sacked Thebes in Egypt, and Kushite rule there came to its end.

In 652 BCE Shamash-shum-ukin rose in rebellion; after a long war Ashurbanipal took Babylon in 648 BCE. In campaigns of about 647 to 646 BCE he defeated Elam, and Susa fell to destruction in the campaign of about 646 BCE.

His libraries at Nineveh, brought to light in the 19th century, kept tens of thousands of clay tablets and fragments, among them a version of the Epic of Gilgamesh, like a river of words dammed in clay. He boasted of his own scribal education.

His inscriptions and reliefs record the brutal treatment of defeated enemies: mass deportation, mutilation and execution.
