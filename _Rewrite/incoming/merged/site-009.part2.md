=== place/xingu-garden-cities
title: Xingu Garden Cities
src: a93d36c2cbff
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Xingu garden cities are pre-Columbian settlements in the upper Xingu region of Brazil, dating from about 1200 to 1600 CE. Their plazas, roads and ditches, a pattern written into the land like veins in a leaf, were mapped by the anthropologist Michael Heckenberger with the Kuikuro people.
--- fact 1
Researchers documented about 28 towns, villages and hamlets, strung like beads on a cord, which may have supported up to about 50,000 people.
--- fact 2
The larger towns had ditches about 3 meters deep and 10 meters wide, plazas up to 150 meters across, and roads aligned with the summer solstice, straight as sunlight on the longest day.
--- fact 3
Around them the fields grew manioc, and earthen dams and ponds held the water where fish were farmed, silver in the brown shallows.
--- fact 4
Population estimates are debated, but the tale has no empty ending: the descendants of the builders still live in the region.
--- context
Their tale is bound to [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]]. After 1500 the settlements declined, as epidemics followed in the wake of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]].
--- observer
I notice who held the other end of the measuring line: the research was done together with the Kuikuro, whose descendants still live in the region. I remember that a ruin is not always an orphan. I remember that the living keep what the soil forgets. Who taught whom? Living communities and archaeologists can inform each other.
--- question 1
How many people lived in the Xingu network when it stood at its height?

=== place/yellow-river
title: Yellow River
src: cb94e64b3762
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Yellow River (Huang He) is a river of northern China, about 5,460 kilometers long, whose loess-laden floodplain cradled early farming and the Shang and Zhou states. Its floods and its wandering course have shaped Chinese history the way a restless hand shapes clay.
--- fact 1
The Yellow River is born in the Bayan Har Mountains of Qinghai and runs the long road through northern China until it gives itself to the Bohai Sea.
--- fact 2
From about the 7th to 6th millennia BCE, farming communities growing millet are documented in the Yellow River basin, their fields golden along the water, and in its middle and lower reaches the Shang and Zhou states arose.
--- fact 3
The Yellow River bears a heavy burden of loess sediment from the Loess Plateau, and that yellow freight has lifted its bed above the surrounding plain in the lower reaches, so that it must be held in by dikes like a beast on a high ledge; records list many breaches and several major changes of course.
--- fact 4
From 1194 until 1855 the river mostly ran south of the Shandong Peninsula into the Yellow Sea, until a breach in 1855 turned it back to a northern course, as a sleeper turns in the night.
--- fact 5
In 1887 the river rose in flood, and that flood is estimated to have killed between about 900,000 and 2 million people.
--- fact 6
In 1938, during the Second Sino-Japanese War, Nationalist forces breached the dikes at Huayuankou to slow the Japanese advance; several hundred thousand people died, with published estimates up to about 900,000, and millions were displaced.
--- context
The Yellow River runs through [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in northern China; through the [[culture/shang-dynasty|Shang Dynasty]] and the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], states that organized flood control; and through the [[event/second-sino-japanese-war|Second Sino-Japanese War]]. In the codex it belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region of [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and three threads are spun from its waters: [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
Think of a river that floods without warning. Whoever can hold it back holds more than water, and the Observer notes that the Yellow River helped tie authority to water control. The traditional story of Yu the Great, who is said to have tamed the floods and founded the first dynasty, is the story-glue here: a covenant joining rule to engineering. Then the ledger's darker page. The 1938 breach shows the same river used as sanctioned harm by a state against an invading army, and its main victims were civilians. Notice, too, that the image of the Yellow River as the sole cradle of Chinese civilization is itself a story, since sites on the Yangtze and elsewhere also show early farming and state formation. Hold all of this with care. The hypothesis that flood control drove state formation is contested, and specialists could weaken this reading by showing that other factors mattered more. A single river rarely writes a whole civilization.
--- question 1
How many died in the 1938 flood, and how many people were displaced from their homes?
--- question 2
Weighed against warfare and trade, how much did flood control contribute to early state formation?

=== place/catalhoyuk
title: Çatalhöyük
src: 7aab38ab9a30
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Çatalhöyük was a large Neolithic settlement in central Turkey, occupied from about 7100 to 5700 BCE, its houses packed together like the cells of a honeycomb and entered from the roof.
--- fact 1
At its height it held several thousand people, a hive of lives under one sky.
--- fact 2
Its walls bore paintings, and the dead were laid beneath the house floors, sleeping under the feet of the living.
--- fact 3
It had no streets: people walked across the roofs, a town whose roads were its rooftops.
--- fact 4
In 2012 it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, an ancient hearth entered into the world's keeping.
--- context
Çatalhöyük is a site of the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]], a hearth of that long age. Its tale runs beside [[place/gobekli-tepe|Göbekli Tepe]] and [[observer-note/the-settling-and-its-bargain|The Settling and Its Bargain]].
--- observer
I climb down through the roof into a house that touches another house, and another. I see a society living in one dense, continuous structure, with few signs of rank. Where is the palace? Where is the higher wall? Early towns did not always show the hierarchy assumed for later cities.
--- question 1
How equal, truly, were the people who shared its roofs?
