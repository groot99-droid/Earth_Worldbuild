=== person/j-m-w-turner
title: J. M. W. Turner
type: person
date: 1775 – 1851
themes: art, technology
src: bfea9c655cfb
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Joseph Mallord William Turner, born in April 1775 and dead by 19 December 1851, stands as the greatest British landscape painter and a bold colourist. He began as a precise topographical watercolourist, and his late paintings dissolve ships, trains and cities into light, mist and weather.
--- fact 1
Turner was born in Covent Garden, London, the son of a barber and wigmaker. At 14 he entered the Royal Academy Schools, and at 15 he exhibited his first watercolour. In 1802 he was elected a full Royal Academician, at 26 or 27 depending on the source.
--- fact 2
He travelled tirelessly through Britain, the Alps, France, Germany and Italy — especially Venice — filling hundreds of sketchbooks.
--- fact 3
He never married, but had two daughters with Sarah Danby. John Ruskin championed him in Modern Painters (1843); he died in Chelsea and was buried in St Paul's Cathedral.
--- fact 4
By the 1830s he was painting natural forces themselves — storms, fire, sunlight, steam — working thin washes over white grounds, scraping and rubbing his canvases, and applying paint thickly with knives.
--- fact 5
He painted steamships and railways alongside Venetian lagoons; Rain, Steam and Speed (1844) stands as one of the first great paintings of modern technology.
--- fact 6
The Slave Ship (1840) was possibly inspired by the Zong massacre of 1781, when enslaved Africans were thrown overboard so that owners could claim insurance; he showed it during the 1840 campaign to end slavery worldwide.
--- fact 7
He left around 300 oil paintings, over 19,000 sketches, drawings and watercolours, and some 200 sketchbooks to the nation, held mainly at Tate Britain. Monet and Pissarro studied his work in London in 1870 to 1871, and the Turner Prize has been awarded since 1984.
--- context
Turner painted the [[event/industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and its [[technology/railways|Railways]] in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and in The Slave Ship confronted the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I see a steam train and a slave ship inside the same body of work — the two great tool-lineages of his century, one celebrated, one condemned, both moving people and goods across water and land. I see critics who mocked his late style, not knowing they were watching a change in how a whole species learns to picture weather and speed.

He left his paintings to the nation, and a prize now carries his name: a reputation turned, over time, into an institution.
--- question 1
How did his late style change how contemporaries saw landscape, given the criticism it drew?

=== person/jadwiga
title: Jadwiga
type: person
date: c. 1373 – 1399
themes: religion, kinship, war
src: 2604265ea95c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jadwiga, born about 1373 and dead by 1399, was crowned "King" of Poland in 1384 at about age ten. She is often described as the first woman to rule Poland in her own right, and her marriage to the Grand Duke of Lithuania began a dynastic union between the two states.
--- fact 1
She was crowned with the male title "rex" (king) rather than "regina" (queen), underscoring that she held full sovereign authority in her own right, not merely as a royal consort.
--- fact 2
In 1386 she married Jogaila, the pagan Grand Duke of Lithuania, who converted to Christianity and was crowned co-ruler as Wladyslaw II Jagiello, founding the Jagiellonian dynasty and beginning the Christianization of Lithuania.
--- fact 3
She helped fund the restoration of the Krakow Academy, later the Jagiellonian University, through jewellery she donated and, under her will, had sold to finance it — reviving an institution that became a major center of learning in Central Europe.
--- fact 4
She died in 1399, about three and a half weeks after giving birth to a daughter who also died in infancy, four days before her — ending her direct line. The Catholic Church canonized her in 1997.
--- context
Jadwiga ruled Poland in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and her marriage began the Polish-Lithuanian union. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Jadwiga's reign is remembered mainly for a marriage alliance and a religious conversion she enabled, rather than for personal political acts — yet the union of Poland and Lithuania that resulted reshaped Central European power for centuries, an outsized consequence for a reign that ended before she turned thirty.
--- question 1
How much personal agency did the ten-year-old Jadwiga have in the political decision to crown her as king, versus her regency council?

=== person/johannes-gutenberg
title: Johannes Gutenberg
type: person
date: c. 1400 – 1468
themes: technology, language, religion
src: 0031e2ac7f8d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Johannes Gutenberg, born about 1400 and dead by 1468, was a German metalworker who developed a movable-type printing press in Mainz around 1440 and printed the Gutenberg Bible about 1455.
--- fact 1
He combined metal type, oil-based ink, and a press adapted from wine-making.
--- fact 2
His 42-line Bible stands as the earliest major book printed from movable type in Europe.
--- fact 3
Financial difficulties overtook him, and he lost control of his workshop to a creditor.
--- fact 4
Printing then spread rapidly across Europe within decades.
--- context
See [[technology/movable-type-printing-press|Movable-Type Printing Press]]. Gutenberg's work supported the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]] and religious reform.
--- observer
I think of memory made cheap, made copyable, made able to leave a single room and travel on its own. I think of every idea that started moving faster than any authority could catch it.

Once a thought can be copied, it no longer needs its author's permission to keep going.
--- question 1
How much did he build on earlier East Asian printing?
