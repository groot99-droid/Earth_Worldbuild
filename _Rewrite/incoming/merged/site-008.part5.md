=== place/rome
src: 38c9b5b51a39
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Rome, traditionally founded in 753 BCE, grew from a small city on the Tiber into the capital of an empire, and it remains a major city today, a river-born seed become a tree of stone.
--- fact 1
Rome held roughly a million inhabitants in the first two centuries CE, by many estimates, a human sea gathered by the river.
--- fact 2
The Colosseum was completed in 80 CE, an arena ringed like a stone crown.
--- fact 3
The city was sacked in 410 and again in 455 CE, twice overrun by the tide of war.
--- fact 4
It later became the seat of the papacy, an old throne taken up by a new kind of power.
--- context
Rome was the heart of the [[culture/romans|Romans]], the hearth from which their world was ruled. Follow its thread through [[person/augustus|Augustus]] and on to the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a city that kept its symbolic importance long after its political power faded. Think of an old crown kept on a shelf: no longer worn, still recognized. Places can outlast the states that once made them.
--- question 1
How large was Rome's population at its peak, when the human sea ran fullest?

=== place/samarkand
src: 87022d945c94
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Samarkand is a city in present-day Uzbekistan, one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities of Central Asia and a leading crossroads of the Silk Roads, where the caravans met like rivers at a delta.
--- fact 1
The ancient city, Afrasiab, is thought to date from the 7th century BCE or earlier, its roots sunk deep in the dust of ages.
--- fact 2
The Greeks knew it as Maracanda, and Alexander the Great took it in 329 BCE, one more jewel pried loose for a conqueror's crown.
--- fact 3
It was a major Sogdian city; Genghis Khan sacked it in 1220, and in the 1370s it became Timur's capital, a fire rekindled from its own ashes.
--- fact 4
UNESCO inscribed 'Samarkand - Crossroad of Cultures' in 2001, naming the city for the meeting place it had long been.
--- context
Samarkand stood at the heart of the [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] and of the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]]. Its story runs through [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]] and the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], roads that bent toward it like reeds toward water.
--- observer
I count the times it was sacked. I count the times it was rebuilt. I count the rulers who wanted it, each one taking it and each one making it again.

What is it to be wanted like that? A place that sits on a crossroads gets taken again and again, and remade each time. To be desired is also to be remade.
--- question 1
When was Samarkand founded, and how deep do its roots go?

=== place/santo-domingo
src: 1f36f67ec2a0
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Santo Domingo is the capital of the Dominican Republic, on the south coast of Hispaniola, founded by Spain in 1496 and commonly described as the oldest continuously inhabited European-founded city in the Americas. It is a threshold: the place where Spanish rule in the Caribbean took institutional form.
--- fact 1
Santo Domingo was founded in 1496 by Bartholomew Columbus on the east bank of the Ozama River, and governor Nicolás de Ovando moved it to the west bank in 1502 after storm damage. A city's first foundation is not always its lasting one.
--- fact 2
Santo Domingo became the seat of Spanish administration in the Caribbean, with the first Real Audiencia in the Americas (1511), a cathedral begun in the 1510s and completed in 1540, and a university founded in 1538: court, church and school, the three pillars of a colonial architecture.
--- fact 3
Expeditions to Puerto Rico, Cuba and the mainland were organized from Hispaniola under this colonial administration; the island was the staging ground for what came next.
--- fact 4
The Taíno population of Hispaniola fell sharply under forced labor, violence and disease, including a smallpox epidemic beginning in 1518. Estimates of the pre-contact population range from tens of thousands to over a million, and a Spanish count in 1514 recorded about 26,000 Taíno on the island. Behind each figure were lives.
--- fact 5
Enslaved Africans were brought to Hispaniola from the early 1500s. In December 1521 enslaved Africans rebelled on a sugar estate near the city, an uprising commonly described as the first of its kind in the Americas.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Colonial City of Santo Domingo in 1990, preserving the stones and, with them, the questions they raise.
--- context
Read Santo Domingo as a hinge. It was the base for the first phase of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] in the Caribbean, which brought devastation to the [[culture/taino|Taino]] and drew the island into the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]]. Its administrators and settlers later took part in the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]] and other mainland ventures. The city stands on [[place/hispaniola|Hispaniola]], in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that within about four decades Spain had placed a high court, a cathedral and a university on a shore where none had stood, extending a claim-line through stone institutions while the people already on the island were drawn into a forced-labor system. That is what an architecture of sovereignty looks like as it is built: the institutions stand in stone, and the cost is borne by others.

The city marks a point where two exchange-webs, one from Europe and one from the Americas, began moving people, crops and pathogens in both directions. The rebellion of 1521 shows that those held in the system resisted it from early on.

Because the population figures rest on Spanish colonial counts, the size of the Taíno loss is uncertain even though its direction is not. We may never read the full ledger; we know which way it ran.
--- question 1
How large was Hispaniola's pre-contact population, and how much of its decline came from disease, compared with forced labor and violence?
--- question 2
How did Santo Domingo's role change as Spain's attention turned toward the mainland?
