=== person/harriet-tubman
title: Harriet Tubman
type: person
date: c. 1822 – 1913
themes: war, kinship
src: 8ccd57194ecf
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Harriet Tubman, born about 1822 and dead by 1913, was an American abolitionist who escaped slavery in Maryland in 1849 and then guided others to freedom on the Underground Railroad. She later served the Union Army during the Civil War.
--- fact 1
Tubman was born Araminta Ross into slavery in Dorchester County, Maryland. Her birth year is uncertain: sources give dates from 1815 to 1825, with about 1822 the most favoured.
--- fact 2
As a teenager she suffered a severe head injury when an overseer threw a metal weight, and she had seizures and headaches for the rest of her life.
--- fact 3
She escaped to Pennsylvania in 1849, and afterward made about 13 trips back to Maryland, guiding roughly 70 people to freedom, according to her biographers.
--- fact 4
During the Civil War she served the Union Army as a cook, nurse, scout and spy. In June 1863 she helped guide the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which freed more than 700 enslaved people, though some sources give more than 750.
--- fact 5
Afterward she settled in Auburn, New York, caring for her family and for elderly people, and working for women's suffrage.
--- fact 6
She died in Auburn on 10 March 1913.
--- context
Tubman's opposition to slavery in the United States belongs to the wider history of [[event/abolition-of-slavery|Abolition of Slavery]], and her service came during the [[event/american-civil-war|American Civil War]] in [[region/north-america|North America]] amid the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See also [[event/women-s-suffrage-movements|Women's Suffrage Movements]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer reads a rescue network built to leave no paper trail, so the ledger of Tubman's trips survives only through her own recollections, told to others, since she could not write. Her escape became a repeated, organized risk run through families and congregations — a kin-lattice pressed into service as a channel for hidden information. Treating the counts of trips and people as estimates is itself a claim; it would weaken if newly found records fixed the numbers exactly.
--- question 1
How well do surviving records support the counts of her rescue trips?

=== person/hatshepsut
title: Hatshepsut
type: person
date: c. 1507 – 1458 BCE
themes: war, trade, art
src: 1df04f65ca02
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hatshepsut, an Egyptian ruler of the 18th Dynasty, held the title of pharaoh, sharing the throne with Thutmose III until her death in 1458 BCE. Tradition dates her reign 1479 to 1458 BCE, and she stands among the first women known to have held the office.
--- fact 1
She first served as regent for her young stepson Thutmose III, then took the title of pharaoh around the seventh year of his reign, about 1473 BCE, sharing his regnal count.
--- fact 2
She sponsored a trading expedition to Punt, on the Red Sea coast of Africa.
--- fact 3
Her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari stands as a major monument.
--- fact 4
Many of her images were later removed or defaced, possibly after her death.
--- context
Hatshepsut belongs among the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] of the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]. Her reign shows how kingship in the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] could hold a woman ruler.
--- observer
The Observer notes how her images carried the regalia and false beard of a male king. Kingship itself, a role bound to the architecture of cosmic order, mattered more than the sex of the person who held it — until later authorities erased her image regardless.
--- question 1
Why were her monuments defaced?

=== person/henry-ossawa-tanner
title: Henry Ossawa Tanner
type: person
date: 1859 – 1937
themes: art, religion
src: a84c39d9aff4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Henry Ossawa Tanner, born 21 June 1859 and dead by 25 May 1937, became the first African American artist to win international fame. Racism at home frustrated him, so in 1891 he settled in Paris and built his career there, painting dignified genre scenes and luminous biblical subjects.
--- fact 1
Tanner was born in Pittsburgh, the eldest of nine children. His father, Benjamin Tucker Tanner, rose to bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and his mother, Sarah, had escaped slavery via the Underground Railroad. At 13 he resolved to become an artist.
--- fact 2
In 1879 he enrolled at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts and studied under Thomas Eakins. As its only Black student he faced harassment, and in 1891 he left for Europe, studying at the Académie Julian in Paris.
--- fact 3
On a return visit in 1893 he painted The Banjo Lesson, and in 1894 he painted The Thankful Poor — both genre scenes of Black American life that challenged racist stereotypes.
--- fact 4
Back in France he turned to biblical subjects. The Resurrection of Lazarus won a medal at the 1897 Salon, and the French government bought it; his patron Rodman Wanamaker then paid for trips to Egypt and Palestine.
--- fact 5
He married the singer Jessie Olsson in 1899, was made a Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur in 1923, and died in Paris.
--- fact 6
He preferred a limited palette of deep blues and blue-greens lit with glowing gold, built up in layers of glaze.
--- fact 7
He advised younger painters such as William Edouard Scott and Hale Woodruff, and stood as a hero to the artists of the Harlem Renaissance. During the Clinton administration, reportedly in 1996, his Sand Dunes at Sunset, Atlantic City became the first painting by an African American artist in the White House collection.
--- context
Tanner worked mostly in France, within [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], an American artist abroad. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I see a painter who left the country whose art school let him be harassed, and built a life in another, where a salon judged the paintings. I see two bodies of work — genre scenes of Black life, biblical scenes built from ordinary models — and I see the same aim in both: truth answering a stereotype without ever naming it.

I ask myself why the first painting by an African American artist entered the White House only under the Clinton administration. An institution's memory can lag far behind the reputation it should already know.
--- question 1
How did his French reception differ from what he would have faced in the United States?
