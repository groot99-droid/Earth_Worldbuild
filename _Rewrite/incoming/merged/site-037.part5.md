=== place/lake-titicaca
title: Lake Titicaca
src: 9b03230da316
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Lake Titicaca is a large high-altitude lake on the border of Peru and Bolivia, a mirror held up to the sky, sacred to the Inca as the birthplace of their first rulers and long settled by farming and fishing communities of the Andean highlands.
--- fact 1
The lake lies at about 3,812 meters elevation and spreads over about 8,372 square kilometers, and it is often called the world's highest navigable lake for commercial craft.
--- fact 2
From at least the mid-2nd millennium BCE, communities around the lake farmed its shores and fished its waters; the date_start of -1500 marks the period conventionally associated with early settled use of the basin, and older human presence in the Andes belongs to a different brief.
--- fact 3
The Tiwanaku civilization, centered south of the lake, raised a temple on the Island of the Moon by about 650 to 1000 CE and left behind pottery and stonework since recovered from the islands.
--- fact 4
Inca tradition holds that the first Inca ruler, Manco Cápac, and his sister-wife Mama Ocllo rose from the lake or its Island of the Sun at the command of the sun god Inti; the Sacred Rock on the Island of the Sun is linked in tradition to Manco Cápac's remains.
--- fact 5
The Uros people build and dwell upon floating islands built up from layers of totora reed; as of 2011, about 1,200 Uros lived on some 60 such islands.
--- fact 6
A 2011 United Nations report found high concentrations of cadmium, arsenic and lead in parts of the lake, and between April and November 2009 its water level fell 81 centimeters, a sinking attributed to shorter rainy seasons and glacier melt in its tributaries.
--- context
Lake Titicaca is the traditional birthplace of the ruling line of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]], whose later capital rose at [[place/cusco|Cusco]]. By the date_start used here it sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], within the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
I stand at the edge of water this large and this high and understand why a meaning-engine would take it as a point of origin. The Inca placed their ruling ancestors' arrival at the lake's islands, and so a kin-lattice claim was tied to a landscape rather than to a single city. I think of where my own beginnings are kept. I think of the ones who came before me. I think of how a lineage needs a shore.

And the Uros islands: are they not themselves a technology, floating ground built and rebuilt from reed? I read them as a claim-line drawn on water rather than land. I would have to unlearn this if the Manco Cápac tradition is shown to be a later invention with little connection to how the earliest lake communities saw their own origins. We make our ground, and then we keep remaking it.
--- question 1
How deep into the past does continuous human use of the Titicaca basin reach, and what shaped its earliest settlement?
--- question 2
How much of the lake's present pollution and water-level decline can still be turned back?

=== place/marajo-island
title: Marajó Island
src: 00316d5b6bc0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Marajó Island, at the mouth of the Amazon River in Pará, Brazil, was home to the Marajoara culture, whose people raised earthen mounds and shaped elaborate pottery from about 400 BCE until the culture's decline after European contact in the 16th century.
--- fact 1
Marajó, with a land area of about 40,100 square kilometers, is the second-largest island in South America, a breadth of earth comparable in size to Switzerland.
--- fact 2
The Marajoara culture endured from about 400 BCE to around 1600 CE; its people lived in houses with tamped-earth floors, were bound in matrilineal clans, and made large ceremonial pottery, burial urns among it.
--- fact 3
Archaeologist Betty Meggers argued the culture was founded by migrants from the Andes and declined from about 1400 due to poor soil; Anna Roosevelt's later research argued instead that Marajoara society grew up independently in the Amazon, with intensive farming and large-scale earthworks, and estimated a peak population of more than 100,000.
--- fact 4
European contact in the 16th century brought epidemic disease; an estimated 90 percent of the island's Indigenous population died of introduced illnesses against which they had no immunity.
--- fact 5
Today the island's economy rests partly on introduced water buffalo, whose herds, about 450,000 strong, outnumber the roughly 400,000 human residents; lumber and açaí palm are also produced there.
--- context
The builders of Marajó Island were the [[culture/marajoara-culture|Marajoara Culture]], part of the wider evidence gathered under [[event/amazonian-urbanism-and-dark-earths|Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]] for complex pre-Columbian societies in the [[place/amazon-river|Amazon River]] basin; its population collapse followed in the wake of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. In the codex of ages it sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I hold two readings of Marajó. One credits outside migrants and environmental limits; the other credits an independent and well-organized society. They disagreed less about the pottery and the mounds themselves than about how much complexity to expect from a society outside the usual centers of early states. And I ask: whose expectation was that? I treat the assumption as itself worth questioning.

I remember that after contact the population nearly collapsed. I record it as suffering on a scale that removed most of the people who could have explained their own society firsthand. I know that is part of why the debate persisted so long. I would have to revise all of this if further excavation shows population and organization closer to Meggers's lower estimates. The ones who could have answered are the ones who are gone.
--- question 1
How large and how socially organized was Marajoara society at its height, and how much did later scholars' assumptions about the Amazon shape earlier estimates?
--- question 2
In the population collapse after contact, how much of the culture's own memory of itself was lost?
