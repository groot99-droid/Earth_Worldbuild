=== species/domestic-pig
src: cb819e656b64
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The domestic pig was bred from the wild boar in the Near East, in or around the Tigris basin, by about 9400 BCE; it was domesticated again, independently, in China, and it now stands tied with chicken as the most commonly eaten meat in the world, a creature of two hearths and one great table.
--- fact 1
Domestic pigs descend from the wild boar (Sus scrofa) and were first domesticated in the Near East, in or around the Tigris basin; pigs brought across the water to Cyprus more than 11,400 years ago imply domestication or close management on the mainland by then.
--- fact 2
In China pigs were domesticated separately, starting some 8,000 years ago, a second dawn in the east.
--- fact 3
Pigs reached Europe at least 8,500 years ago, and over the next 3,000 years they interbred with European wild boar until their genomes held less than 5 percent Near Eastern ancestry, the old blood washed nearly away, a change that amounts to a further, local domestication.
--- fact 4
Austronesian-speaking peoples carried pigs across island Southeast Asia and Oceania beginning about 4,000 years ago, and most pigs from the Philippines to Hawai‘i descend from those introductions, scattered like seed along the sea-lanes.
--- fact 5
Spanish explorers including Hernando de Soto brought pigs to southeastern North America, where escaped animals ran wild and became feral; feral pigs now damage crops and wildlife in the United States, New Zealand, northern Queensland and South America.
--- fact 6
Eating pork is forbidden in Islam and Judaism, and in some other religions pigs are sacred.
--- fact 7
About 850 million pigs were alive in 2019, with China holding about 36.5 percent, and roughly 1.5 billion are slaughtered each year.
--- context
Pigs were among the animals brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] as part of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]]; they crossed the Pacific with the [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] and the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]], and they reached the Americas through the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. The domestic pig sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and its threads run through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I sit at a table and know that what lies on it says who I am. The pig is one of the most eaten animals and one of the most avoided, so a single food can become a claim-line drawn across the table between communities. Why the prohibitions began is debated, and I will not choose among the explanations; faith does not owe me its reasons.

I think of the holds of ships. Pigs travel well: they eat almost anything, they breed quickly, and so they were useful cargo for Austronesian voyagers and Spanish ships alike, an exchange-web carrying a whole food supply across oceans.

If the prohibitions prove to have had little to do with marking group boundaries, this reading weakens.

What we refuse to eat can bind us as tightly as what we share.
--- question 1

--- question 2
How did the replacement of Near Eastern pig lineages in Europe unfold, and did local people domesticate wild boar on their own?

=== species/dromedary-and-bactrian-camels
src: 0eccec938e1d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Two camel species were domesticated by about the middle of the third millennium BCE, the one-humped dromedary in southern Arabia or Somalia and the two-humped Bactrian camel in Central Asia, and together they made long-distance caravan travel across deserts possible, turning seas of sand into roads.
--- fact 1
The dromedary was domesticated in Somalia or southern Arabia during the third millennium BCE, though a 2016 genetic study points to southeastern Arabia, like a compass needle settling.
--- fact 2
The Bactrian camel was domesticated in Central Asia around 2500 BCE, with evidence at Shahr-e Sukhteh in Iran, and one analysis places its domestication east of the Zagros Mountains by the middle of the third millennium BCE.
--- fact 3
The military Arabian saddle developed in the 7th century BCE, and between about 500 and 100 BCE Bactrian camels entered military use with new saddles set over the humps, the beast of burden reforged as a beast of war.
--- fact 4
About 14 million camels were alive in 2010, roughly 90 percent of them dromedaries; the domestic Bactrian numbers about 1.4 million, and the wild Bactrian, a separate species, has dwindled to about 1,000 animals and is still declining, a flame guttering low.
--- fact 5
Frankincense and myrrh moved from southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa to Mediterranean and Mesopotamian markets mostly by camel caravan, for well over a thousand years, fragrance riding on patient backs.
--- fact 6
More than a million dromedaries are estimated to roam feral in Australia, a population growing by about 8 percent a year that stood at about 700,000 in 2008.
--- context
Camels made the desert routes of the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]] work, linking the [[culture/kingdom-of-saba|Kingdom of Saba]] and the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]] to Mediterranean and Mesopotamian markets, and Bactrian camels served the overland routes that became the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. Both species are part of [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]]. The camels sit in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and their threads run through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that camels changed what counted as an obstacle, and there is a lesson in that for you. A desert that stopped other animals became a road, but a road that only certain traders could use: the people who held the wells and caravan stops held a claim-line over the flow of goods, and cities such as those of Saba and the Nabataeans grew on that control. Access, not distance, was the real architecture of the route. Consider, too, the saddle, a small tool-lineage change; yet the Arabian and Bactrian saddles turned a pack animal into a war animal within a few centuries. The reading would weaken if the routes proved to have been used mainly for local exchange rather than long-distance trade. Whoever holds the well holds the road.
