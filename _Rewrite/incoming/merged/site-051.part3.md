=== person/ramesses-ii
title: Ramesses II
src: dcd9d4792f08
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ramesses II (about 1303 to 1213 BCE), also called Ramesses the Great, was the third pharaoh of Egypt's Nineteenth Dynasty. He ruled for about 66 years, fought the Hittites at Kadesh, made one of the earliest surviving peace treaties, and built temples across Egypt and Nubia.
--- fact 1
Ramesses II came to the throne in 1279 BCE by the most widely used chronology and reigned until 1213 BCE, though other chronologies differ by several years.
--- fact 2
About 1274 BCE he fought the Hittite king Muwatalli II at Kadesh, in present-day Syria. Both sides claimed victory, and Ramesses had it carved on temple walls, though most historians think the Hittites gained more.
--- fact 3
About 1259 BCE he made a treaty with the Hittite king Hattusili III. It survives in Egyptian and Hittite versions, each saying the other side asked for peace first.
--- fact 4
He built the capital Pi-Ramesses, the Ramesseum and the temples at Abu Simbel, moved in the 1960s to escape the Aswan High Dam reservoir; many monuments carry his name over those of earlier kings.
--- fact 5
He is estimated to have had more than 90 children and to have lived to about 90. His thirteenth son, Merneptah, succeeded him.
--- fact 6
Later tradition links him to the pharaoh of the Exodus. Scholars dispute this, and no Egyptian source records such an event.
--- context
Ramesses II ruled in the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]. See [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]], [[culture/hittites|Hittites]], [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a battle with no clear winner was carved on several temples as a triumph, while the treaty that ended the rivalry survives in two versions, each claiming the other side asked first. Building on a huge scale, and carving his name over predecessors', worked as status-signal and story-glue alike. A specialist could argue that royal inscriptions simply followed conventions expected of any pharaoh, which would weaken a reading of monumentality as personal ambition.
--- question 1
How did Kadesh look from the Hittite side, given that most Hittite records are fragmentary?
--- question 2
Which monuments bearing his name did he build, and which did he rework?

=== person/rani-lakshmibai
title: Rani Lakshmibai
src: f4557caa4b4e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi (about 1828 to 1858) was an Indian queen who became a leader of the 1857 rebellion and a national symbol of resistance.
--- fact 1
She ruled Jhansi as regent after her husband's death, and the British refused to recognize her adopted heir.
--- fact 2
She led the defense of Jhansi against British forces in 1858.
--- fact 3
She and other rebels seized the fortress of Gwalior on 1 June 1858.
--- fact 4
She died in battle on 17 June 1858.
--- context
She is a central figure of the [[event/indian-rebellion-of-1857|Indian Rebellion of 1857]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how someone killed in a lost war can still become a founding figure in a later nation's story. Defeat, in this architecture of memory, can be a source of legitimacy for the future.
--- question 1
What were her actual aims in 1857?

=== person/razia-sultana
title: Razia Sultana
src: e923082f58a7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Razia Sultana (about 1205 to 1240) ruled the Delhi Sultanate from 1236 to 1240, the only woman to hold the throne in its history. She was the daughter of Iltutmish, who by tradition had named her his successor.
--- fact 1
Razia was a daughter of the sultan Iltutmish, who reigned from 1211 to 1236. Tradition holds that he named her his heir over his sons, though he seems to have chosen Ruknuddin Firuz shortly before his death.
--- fact 2
After Iltutmish died in 1236 her half-brother Ruknuddin Firuz took the throne but was deposed within months, and Razia became sultan with the support of the people of Delhi and some nobles.
--- fact 3
According to chroniclers she held court in public and led armies, and coins were issued in her name.
--- fact 4
She appointed Jamal ud-Din Yaqut, an Abyssinian former slave, master of the stables, a promotion Turkic nobles resented.
--- fact 5
In 1240 Malik Altunia, the governor of Bhatinda, rebelled. She was imprisoned at Bhatinda and then married him in September 1240; they were defeated at Kaithal and killed in October 1240, and accounts differ on the details.
--- fact 6
The main contemporary account is by the chronicler Minhaj al-Siraj Juzjani, who praised her abilities but wrote that her sex counted against her.
--- context
Razia ruled the [[culture/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the complaints recorded against Razia concern who held high posts and who displayed the status-signals of rule, more than how she governed. Her only detailed account comes from a court chronicler who admired her and still described her sex as a defect. A specialist could argue that court histories overstated gender as the cause, since a quarrel between nobles and a strong sultan would look the same in the record.
--- question 1
How much of the opposition to her was about her sex, and how much about court factions?

=== person/rembrandt-van-rijn
title: Rembrandt van Rijn
src: 267fae5f4760
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rembrandt Harmenszoon van Rijn (15 July 1606 to 4 October 1669) stood as the greatest artist of the Dutch Golden Age: a painter, etcher and draughtsman. His roughly 100 self-portraits build an extraordinary autobiography, running from early fame and wealth to bankruptcy and loss.
--- fact 1
Rembrandt was born in Leiden, the ninth child of a prosperous miller, and trained locally and for six months in Amsterdam with Pieter Lastman; about 1631 he moved to Amsterdam.
--- fact 2
The Anatomy Lesson of Dr Nicolaes Tulp (1632) came early in his successful career as a portraitist in the city. In 1634 he married Saskia van Uylenburgh; three of their children died in infancy, and only Titus, born 1641, survived. Saskia died in 1642, the year he completed The Night Watch.
--- fact 3
Debts led to bankruptcy in 1656, and his house and collection were sold, though he kept working; his partner Hendrickje Stoffels died in 1663 and Titus in 1668.
--- fact 4
On 4 October 1669 he died, and was buried in a rented grave in the Westerkerk.
--- fact 5
He used chiaroscuro to direct attention and mood, and his late paintings are built from thick, rough layers applied with brush, palette knife and even fingers.
--- fact 6
As a printmaker he combined etching with drypoint and varied each impression. Key works include The Hundred Guilder Print (about 1647 to 1649) and The Return of the Prodigal Son (about 1661 to 1669).
--- fact 7
He ran a large workshop, and since 1968 the Rembrandt Research Project has reattributed many paintings; Goya is said to have named him one of his three masters.
--- context
Rembrandt worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], in Amsterdam, then a booming commercial capital and home port of the [[event/dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I think of the merchants, the guilds, the civic companies, all paying once for a group portrait, and then, years on, so few buying his late work, though he kept painting through bankruptcy and through grief. I think of the self-portraits, a private face outsourced onto canvas, on a scale unusual for his age. I ask how much of any decade's "Rembrandt" is the master's own hand, since he ran a large workshop and the scholarly line between his brush and his pupils' has moved since 1968. What a name signs is not always what a hand made.
--- question 1
Which paintings once credited to Rembrandt belong to his pupils, and which reattributions will hold?

=== person/robert-the-bruce
title: Robert the Bruce
src: 8f7dd2b3590f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Robert the Bruce (1274 to 1329) was King of Scotland from 1306 to 1329, leading Scottish resistance to English rule and securing recognition of Scottish independence shortly before his death.
--- fact 1
He claimed the Scottish throne in 1306 after killing a rival claimant, John Comyn, in a church at Dumfries, an act that led to his excommunication by the pope.
--- fact 2
Early in his reign he suffered several defeats and periods as a fugitive, including, according to a later popular legend, drawing perseverance from watching a spider repeatedly try to build its web.
--- fact 3
He decisively defeated a much larger English army under Edward II at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314, the most significant Scottish military victory of the wars of independence.
--- fact 4
The Treaty of Edinburgh-Northampton in 1328 saw England formally recognize Scottish independence and Robert's kingship, a year before his death in 1329, reportedly from leprosy.
--- context
Robert the Bruce led Scotland, in [[region/europe|Europe]], during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] wars of independence against England. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Robert's path to the throne began with a killing in a church, not a conventional succession, and that his later reputation as Scotland's founding hero sits somewhat uneasily beside that origin; national founding stories often smooth over the more compromised beginnings of the figures they celebrate.
--- question 1
How much of the spider legend and similar popular stories about Robert's perseverance date to his own lifetime versus later national mythmaking?
