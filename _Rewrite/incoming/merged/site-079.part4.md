=== place/mekong-river
src: 46db8f69dcc7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mekong River is a river of Asia with an estimated length of about 4,900 kilometers, rising on the Tibetan Plateau and flowing through China, Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia and Vietnam, whose annual flood supports rice farming and one of the world's largest inland fisheries.
--- fact 1
The Mekong rises on the Tibetan Plateau and flows through China, along the borders of Myanmar, Laos and Thailand, and through Cambodia and Vietnam into the South China Sea, where it forms a large delta.
--- fact 2
Settlement along the Mekong is documented from at least 210 BCE, and the early port of Oc Eo in the delta, part of the state called Funan, traded from about the 1st century CE; the region's much earlier prehistory is not well fixed by the sources consulted here.
--- fact 3
The Tonlé Sap, a lake in Cambodia joined to the Mekong, changes its direction of flow with the season: floodwater flows into it in the wet season and back out in the dry season, supporting a major fishery.
--- fact 4
The lower basin has about 60 million people, and the river is among the world's most productive inland fisheries.
--- fact 5
A French exploration commission in 1866 to 1868 found that rapids made the Mekong unnavigable as a route to China, and the Mekong Delta was a major area of fighting in the Vietnam War.
--- fact 6
China has built a cascade of dams on the upper Mekong since the 1990s, and Laos began operating the Xayaburi dam on the lower river in 2019; the Mekong River Commission, formed in 1995, coordinates the lower-basin countries, and the effects of the dams on fish, sediment and flood timing are debated.
--- context
The Mekong River was the corridor of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] and its capital [[place/angkor|Angkor]], and its delta was a theater of the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. It sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], its earliest documented settlement, and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Mekong is a river whose annual pulse feeds fish and rice for tens of millions, and that dams upstream change that pulse for people downstream, a relation in which those who control the flow can alter conditions for others across claims the river itself ignores. The reversing flow of the Tonlé Sap shows you a natural rhythm that a whole society organized its seasons around. This reading would weaken if measurements show that the dams' net effect on downstream fish and sediment is smaller than the models suggest.
--- question 1
How much have upstream dams changed the flood pulse, fish migration and delta sediment?
--- question 2
How did early states such as Funan use the river for trade?

=== place/merv
src: df3061c0573a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Merv was a major oasis city on the Silk Roads in what is now Turkmenistan, a city of the Achaemenid Persians, a capital of the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun and of the Seljuk sultans, and sacked by the Mongols in 1221.
--- fact 1
Merv lies in the Murghab delta oasis near the modern city of Mary; Bronze Age towns of the region, such as Gonur Depe, date to about 2000 BCE, and the walled city of Erk Kala dates from the Achaemenid period, from about the 7th century BCE.
--- fact 2
Arab forces took Merv in 651, after the last Sasanian king, Yazdegerd III, was killed near it.
--- fact 3
Abu Muslim launched the Abbasid Revolution from Merv in 747 and declared the new Abbasid dynasty there in February 748; the caliph al-Ma'mun ruled from Merv from about 813 to 818 before moving to Baghdad.
--- fact 4
Merv was a Seljuk capital, notably under Sultan Sanjar, reigned 1118 to 1157, whose mausoleum survives, and some estimates suggest it was among the largest cities in the world in the 12th century.
--- fact 5
The geographer Yaqut, who studied in Merv's libraries in the early 13th century, described them.
--- fact 6
The Mongol army took Merv in 1221; Persian chronicles report hundreds of thousands to over a million killed, figures that modern historians regard as greatly exaggerated but that reflect a severe massacre and destruction.
--- fact 7
Merv was rebuilt on a smaller scale in the 15th century and was finally abandoned in the late 18th century.
--- fact 8
UNESCO inscribed Ancient Merv in 1999.
--- context
Merv was a satrapy center of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]], the political base of the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] at its start, and a victim of the [[event/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]]. It lay on the routes of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. Merv sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Merv's fortunes followed the claims drawn by others: an irrigated oasis city depends on which empire's network runs through it, and Achaemenids, Seleucids, Arabs, Seljuks and Mongols each held it in turn. The events of 1221 show that a city, unlike a mobile camp, cannot leave, and the reported loss of its libraries suggests that a city's stored writing stays as exposed as its people, though those reports are hard to verify. This reading would weaken if the true death toll and the extent of the library losses prove much smaller than the chronicles say.
--- question 1
How many people lived in Merv at its 12th-century peak, and how many died in 1221?
--- question 2
What did Merv's libraries hold, and what happened to them?

=== place/mogao-caves
src: e2743ea6a53a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Mogao Caves are a complex of Buddhist cave temples cut into a cliff near Dunhuang, in Gansu, China, begun by tradition in 366 CE and decorated with murals and sculptures over about a thousand years at a crossroads of the Silk Roads.
--- fact 1
The Mogao Caves lie near the oasis of Dunhuang at the edge of the desert, where Silk Road routes met; Han China had established a commandery at Dunhuang in the late 2nd century BCE.
--- fact 2
By tradition the first cave was dug in 366 CE by the monk Yuezun, and work continued into the 14th century; the site has roughly 735 caves, 487 known since the early 1900s plus 248 more found between 1988 and 1995, several hundred of which hold murals or painted sculptures, with murals covering about 46,000 square meters.
--- fact 3
The art shows Indian, Central Asian and Chinese influences, and donors, including officials, merchants and local rulers, are painted into many caves.
--- fact 4
Dunhuang was under Tibetan rule from about 787 to 848, was governed by a local regime from 848, and passed to the Tangut state of Western Xia in about 1036.
--- fact 5
The "Library Cave," Cave 17, sealed around the early 11th century, was opened in 1900 by Wang Yuanlu, a Daoist priest who guarded the caves; it held tens of thousands of manuscripts in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Sogdian, Khotanese and other languages, including a printed Diamond Sutra dated 868.
--- fact 6
Aurel Stein in 1907, Paul Pelliot in 1908 and others obtained large numbers of these manuscripts, now held in London, Paris and elsewhere; the removals are described in China as theft and by the acquiring institutions as purchases.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Mogao Caves in 1987, and the Dunhuang Academy has conserved them since 1944.
--- context
The Mogao Caves grew with the trade of the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]], under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] and the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]], and held documents written by [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]] and others. They sit in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belong to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I think of a caravan crossroads whose record survived because one room was sealed: contracts, letters, school exercises and Buddhist texts in many scripts, kept dry and dark, an ordinary life preserved by accident where only palace records usually last.

I see the donors painted beside the sacred figures, devotion and standing joined in a single picture.

I notice the manuscripts now scattered across several countries, and how the record of one place became the claim of many hands.

Why the room was sealed is debated, and this reading would falter if it proves to be routine disposal of worn-out texts rather than a deliberate act of keeping.
--- question 1
Why was the Library Cave sealed, and by whom?
--- question 2
How should the manuscripts taken from the Mogao Caves be held and shared today?
