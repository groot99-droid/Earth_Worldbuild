=== person/hieronymus-bosch
title: Hieronymus Bosch
type: person
date: c. 1450 – 1516
themes: art, religion
src: 13c6a90eb76a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Hieronymus Bosch, born about 1450 and dead in 1516, painted in 's-Hertogenbosch in the Duchy of Brabant, filling his panels with hybrid monsters and crowds of tiny figures acting out human sin and folly. Only about 25 paintings are firmly attributed to him.
--- fact 1
Born Jheronimus van Aken about 1450 in 's-Hertogenbosch, he came from a line of painters — grandfather, father, uncles — and later signed his works "Jheronimus Bosch" after the town.
--- fact 2
Between 1479 and 1481 he married Aleid Goyaerts van den Meervenne, from a wealthy family; by 1486 or 1487 he had become a sworn member of the Illustrious Brotherhood of Our Blessed Lady. He died in 1516.
--- fact 3
He seems to have spent almost his whole life in and around 's-Hertogenbosch — after marrying, he and Aleid moved to nearby Oirschot — yet his fame still spread: Philip the Handsome, Duke of Burgundy, commissioned a Last Judgment from him in 1504.
--- fact 4
He painted in oil on oak panels, often as triptychs. Key works include The Garden of Earthly Delights (about 1495 to 1505), The Ship of Fools (about 1500 to 1510), The Temptation of St Anthony (about 1501) and the Haywain Triptych (about 1510 to 1516).
--- fact 5
King Philip II of Spain acquired many Bosch paintings in the late 16th century, which is why the Prado in Madrid now holds his greatest works.
--- fact 6
[[person/pieter-bruegel-the-elder|Pieter Bruegel the Elder]] built on his crowded moral landscapes, and after being partly forgotten, Bosch was claimed in the 20th century by Surrealists such as Salvador Dalí and Max Ernst.
--- fact 7
In 2016, the 500th anniversary of his death, the Bosch Research and Conservation Project re-examined his works and shifted attributions in both directions: a Temptation of St Anthony, once given to his workshop, was credited back to Bosch himself, while other works were newly questioned as workshop pieces.
--- context
Bosch worked in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], at the time of the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a painter who gave visible form to the fears of a meaning-engine — hell, folly, temptation — in crowded panels meant to be read slowly and closely. The same images were later read by Surrealists as dreams, so what the pictures meant changed with the audience. With only about 25 firmly attributed works, and reattributions as recent as 2016, the artist himself is still being redrawn, and any account of what he intended should be held loosely.
--- question 1
Which works are by Bosch himself and which by his workshop?
--- question 2
What did the imagery mean to the people who first saw it?

=== person/hilma-af-klint
title: Hilma af Klint
type: person
date: 1862 – 1944
themes: art, religion, science
src: 4e1f7d8b8b2c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Hilma af Klint, born 26 October 1862 and dead by 21 October 1944, was a Swedish painter who, from 1906, created hundreds of abstract paintings guided by her spiritual beliefs — several years before Kandinsky, Mondrian and Malevich. She kept them largely hidden, and they reached an international audience only from the 1980s.
--- fact 1
Born at Karlberg Palace in Solna, near Stockholm, she studied at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Stockholm from 1882 to 1887 and made her living painting landscapes, portraits and botanical illustrations.
--- fact 2
Her spiritual investigations, which sources date from 1879, deepened around the time her younger sister Hermina died in 1880, drawing her toward spiritualism and Theosophy. With four other women, who had begun as members of the Edelweiss Society, she formed "The Five", a group that held séances and experimented with automatic drawing, an activity the sources date to 1896.
--- fact 3
In 1906, believing she had received a commission from a spiritual guide, she began the Paintings for the Temple, a cycle of nearly 200 works completed in 1915; she considered herself a medium through whom the works were created.
--- fact 4
In 1908 she showed her work to Rudolf Steiner; his cool response contributed to a four-year break in the series. She later spent long periods at the Goetheanum, the Anthroposophical Society's headquarters, between 1921 and 1930, and filled more than 125 notebooks — sources give figures from 125 to more than 150.
--- fact 5
She believed the world was not ready for the paintings, and directed that they not be exhibited until 20 years after her death. She died after a traffic accident, leaving more than 1,200 paintings and drawings to her nephew.
--- fact 6
Her abstract works combine geometric and organic forms with letters and symbols. In her colour code, blue often stood for the female spirit, yellow for the male spirit, and pink or red for physical and spiritual love; she drew on Theosophy, Christianity, botany, and the new sciences of evolution and atomic physics.
--- fact 7
In her lifetime she exhibited only a handful of times, mostly at spiritual conferences. Her work reached an international audience in the 1980s, including the 1986 exhibition The Spiritual in Art at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art. The Guggenheim's Paintings for the Future (2018 to 2019) drew more than 600,000 visitors, the most visited exhibition in its history.
--- context
Af Klint worked in Sweden, in [[region/europe|Europe]] in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
I think of a temple built for no market, of paintings withheld because their maker believed the world was not ready — so art history was written for about eight decades without them. She called herself a medium, not an inventor, and that sits strangely against a world that likes one name for one idea.

Was she first, before Kandinsky? The answer depends on which date you trust and what you call abstraction in the first place. Who was first is a story art history tells itself, more than a fact it has settled.
--- question 1
