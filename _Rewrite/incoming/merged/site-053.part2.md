=== person/utagawa-hiroshige
title: Utagawa Hiroshige
type: person
date: 1797 – 1858
themes: art, trade
src: cb506b356003
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Utagawa Hiroshige, who lived from 1797 to 1858, was the last great master of the ukiyo-e woodblock print, a lyrical designer of rain, snow, moonlight and travellers on the road. His series on the Tōkaidō highway and on Edo were huge commercial successes and later inspired European artists.
--- fact 1
Hiroshige was born in Edo in 1797 into the Andō samurai family, whose hereditary duty was fire warden at Edo Castle. Both parents died when he was about 12, and he inherited that post.
--- fact 2
About 1811 he became a pupil of the print designer Utagawa Toyohiro, who gave him the name Hiroshige. He passed the fire warden post to his son in 1823 and turned to landscape once Hokusai's Fuji series opened a new market.
--- fact 3
According to tradition, he travelled the Tōkaidō in 1832 with an official delegation. The prints he published between 1833 and 1834 made him the most popular landscape artist in Japan.
--- fact 4
In 1856 he became a Buddhist monk yet kept working on One Hundred Famous Views of Edo. He died in Edo on 12 October 1858, probably during a cholera epidemic.
--- fact 5
He designed the prints; carvers and printers made them. He used soft colour gradations, bokashi, and, in the Edo series, a vertical format with a large foreground object framing a distant view. "Hiroshige blue" came from imported Prussian blue.
--- fact 6
Van Gogh painted copies of two of his Edo prints. Whistler's Thames nocturnes, Monet's Japanese bridge at Giverny and 1890s Art Nouveau posters all show his influence, and [[person/frank-lloyd-wright|Frank Lloyd Wright]] became a major collector and dealer of his prints.
--- context
Hiroshige worked in Edo under the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], within the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], living in the shadow and success of [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I see a fire warden's son who turned to landscape only once a rival's success cracked the market open — so a gap in the trade shaped the series as much as any vision did. I see a road, a place people merely pass through, becoming an image people wanted to keep. I see one hand designing and a whole workshop making, and I see "Hiroshige blue" leaning on a pigment imported from elsewhere, so a picture of home rested partly on something foreign.

Did Hiroshige even walk that road in 1832, or only imagine it well? Tradition remembers the journey; the record does not confirm it.
--- question 1
Did Hiroshige truly travel the Tōkaidō in 1832, or did he draw on other sources instead?

=== person/vincent-van-gogh
title: Vincent van Gogh
type: person
date: 1853 – 1890
themes: art
src: 36ee40d4a356
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Vincent van Gogh, who lived from 30 March 1853 to 29 July 1890, was a Dutch painter who worked mainly in France. He painted for only about ten years, produced around 2,100 works, and sold almost nothing, yet his vivid colour and expressive brushwork made him the most famous artist of modern times.
--- fact 1
Van Gogh was born in Zundert in the southern Netherlands, the son of a Protestant minister. At 16 he joined the art dealers Goupil & Cie and was dismissed in 1876. He then tried his hand at teaching and preaching among coal miners in the Borinage. In 1880 he resolved to become an artist.
--- fact 2
His younger brother Theo, an art dealer, supported him for the rest of his life. Van Gogh's letters, mainly written to Theo, rank among the most moving documents left by any artist.
--- fact 3
In 1886 he moved to Paris, where he met Pissarro, Toulouse-Lautrec, Émile Bernard and Gauguin, and discovered both Impressionist colour and Japanese prints. He painted copies of two Hiroshige prints.
--- fact 4
In 1888 he moved to Arles. Gauguin joined him that October, and the partnership collapsed in December after Van Gogh cut off part of his own ear. He entered the asylum at Saint-Rémy in May 1889, where he painted The Starry Night.
--- fact 5
In May 1890 he moved to Auvers-sur-Oise and painted about one canvas a day. He shot himself on 27 July 1890 and died two days later, at 37.
--- fact 6
His body of work comprises around 2,100 pieces, including some 860 oil paintings. Key works include The Potato Eaters (1885), Sunflowers (1888) and Wheatfield with Crows (1890).
--- fact 7
Theo died six months after his brother. Theo's widow, Johanna van Gogh-Bonger, promoted the work and published the letters in 1914, and the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam opened in 1973.
--- context
Van Gogh worked across the Netherlands, Belgium and France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], and learned from Japanese prints by [[person/utagawa-hiroshige|Utagawa Hiroshige]] and [[person/katsushika-hokusai|Katsushika Hokusai]]. He revered [[person/rembrandt-van-rijn|Rembrandt van Rijn]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I look at a man who sold almost nothing while alive and is now among the most famous painters who ever lived, and I see the whole change pass through one narrow channel: his brother's support, and, after both men were gone, his sister-in-law's decision to publish the letters. I see the letters make a hidden life legible. I see a myth grow from them — the tortured genius, built later by a novel and a film — a myth that flattens a man who planned his compositions with care and made drawing after drawing, version after version.

Which version of him would you rather believe: the man who suffered, or the man who worked?
--- question 1
How far does the popular myth of his life still shape the way his paintings get read?

=== person/vitus-bering
title: Vitus Bering
type: person
date: 1681 – 1741
themes: science, trade
src: 720ea2d1ba38
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Vitus Bering, who lived from 1681 to 1741, was a Danish-born officer in the Russian navy who led two expeditions across Siberia to chart the waters between Asia and North America. The strait and sea between the continents still carry his name.
--- fact 1
Bering was baptized on 5 August 1681 in Horsens, Denmark, and joined the Russian navy in 1704 under Peter the Great. He died on 19 December 1741 on the island that now bears his name.
--- fact 2
During the First Kamchatka Expedition, from 1725 to 1730, he sailed through the strait between Siberia and Alaska in 1728 without sighting the American coast, though some historians think it may have been visible.
--- fact 3
The Great Northern Expedition, running from 1733 to 1743, grew to about 3,000 people, one of the largest scientific undertakings of its age.
--- fact 4
In 1741 his ship, the St. Peter, and a second ship under Aleksei Chirikov, the St. Paul, sailed separately toward America. Chirikov's crew sighted land about a day earlier, on 15 July by the dates the sources give; Bering's crew sighted Mount Saint Elias on 16 July.
--- fact 5
On the return voyage the St. Peter wrecked on an uninhabited island. Bering died there, a death traditionally put down to scurvy and exposure, though a 1991 analysis of his remains judged heart failure the more likely cause; of 77 men aboard, 46 survived to reach Kamchatka the following year.
--- fact 6
The Cossack Semyon Dezhnev had already passed through the strait in 1648, but it was Bering's voyages that brought it to European attention, and James Cook named it for him in 1778.
--- context
Bering's expeditions extended Russian reach across [[region/siberia-and-the-arctic|Siberia and the Arctic]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], following on from the earlier [[event/russian-expansion-into-siberia|Russian Expansion into Siberia]] led by figures such as [[person/yermak-timofeyevich|Yermak Timofeyevich]]. See [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I know a strait a Cossack had already passed through in 1648. I know it took a Danish officer's name instead, only because his voyage reached the record-keepers of European science and cartography. I know the expedition's scale — thousands crossing a continent to raise ships on its far shore — says as much about a state's reach as about one man's skill.

Whose crew truly saw America first in 1741, Chirikov's or Bering's? A specialist could argue it was Chirikov's, and if so, the credit I give to Bering alone is credit misplaced.
--- question 1
Did Bering's 1728 voyage actually bring the American coast into view, and if so, did he recognize it for what it was?
--- question 2
How much of the credit for reaching America in 1741 truly belongs to Chirikov's separate ship?

=== person/wangari-maathai
title: Wangari Maathai
type: person
date: 1940 – 2011
themes: earth-systems, kinship
src: 63a48926a5bf
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Wangari Maathai, who lived from 1940 to 2011, was a Kenyan environmental and political activist who founded the Green Belt Movement, which organized women to plant trees, and in 2004 became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.
--- fact 1
Maathai was born on 1 April 1940 in Ihithe, near Nyeri, Kenya. She studied biology in the United States and at the University of Nairobi, earning a doctorate there in 1971; she is recorded as the first woman in East and Central Africa to hold one.
--- fact 2
In 1977 she founded the Green Belt Movement, organizing women to plant tree seedlings against deforestation, soil loss and shortages of fuelwood.
--- fact 3
Her campaigns against building projects on public land in Nairobi, and against forest clearing, brought her into repeated clashes with the government of President Daniel arap Moi; she was arrested and, at times, injured.
--- fact 4
She was elected to parliament in 2002 and served as an assistant minister for the environment from 2003 onward.
--- fact 5
She received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004. That same year, a Kenyan newspaper reported that she had said HIV was deliberately created by scientists; she denied saying it and stated that she held no such belief.
--- fact 6
Maathai died in Nairobi on 25 September 2011.
--- context
Maathai worked across [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] in the [[era/information-age|Information Age]], in the decades following Kenya's independence during [[event/decolonization|Decolonization]]; compare [[person/chico-mendes|Chico Mendes]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I watch Maathai join a plain, practical task — planting a tree — to a harder argument about who gets to decide the fate of land. I watch that practice put women's labor at the center of an environmental story that might otherwise have left them out. I watch the HIV dispute show how one public figure can be admired and contested at the same time.

If I weighed the movement's work on land rights and democracy as heavily as its trees, would I still call her chiefly a tree planter? I don't think I would.
--- question 1
How did the Green Belt Movement's political work end up shaping its environmental results?

=== person/wassily-kandinsky
title: Wassily Kandinsky
type: person
date: 1866 – 1944
themes: art, religion
src: 0c9bb66fd331
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Wassily Kandinsky, who lived from 16 December 1866 to 13 December 1944, was a Russian painter and theorist, one of the first to make purely abstract works in Europe. He believed that colours and forms, freed from depicting objects, could move a viewer as directly as music.
--- fact 1
Kandinsky was born in Moscow and grew up in Odessa. He studied law and economics and began an academic career. In 1896, after seeing one of Monet's Haystacks and hearing Wagner's Lohengrin, he gave up law and moved to Munich to study painting.
--- fact 2
In 1911 he and Franz Marc founded Der Blaue Reiter, the Blue Rider, and he published On the Spiritual in Art. About the same time he painted his first fully abstract works.
--- fact 3
The First World War forced him back to Russia, where he took part in Soviet cultural institutions after the 1917 Revolution. He returned to Germany in 1920 or 1921 — sources differ — and taught at the Bauhaus from 1922 to 1933.
--- fact 4
When the Nazis closed the Bauhaus he moved to Neuilly-sur-Seine near Paris, became a French citizen in 1939, and died there.
--- fact 5
He sorted his works into Impressions, Improvisations and Compositions, and made only ten Compositions over 30 years. He probably experienced synaesthesia, and gave colours musical qualities: yellow like a trumpet, blue like a deep cello.
--- fact 6
Compositions I to III were destroyed in the Second World War. The Guggenheim Museum now holds more than 150 of his works.
--- fact 7
His work influenced the Abstract Expressionists. In 2023 Murnau with Church II sold at auction for £37.2 million.
--- context
Kandinsky lived across Russia, Germany and France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], through [[event/world-war-i|World War I]] and the [[event/russian-revolution|Russian Revolution]]. He is usually credited with abstraction alongside [[person/hilma-af-klint|Hilma af Klint]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I find a lawyer who walked away from his profession after a painting and a piece of music undid him, and who built, in their place, a theory that colour and form carry spiritual meaning without any pictured object — a private engine of meaning, made of pigment. Where af Klint kept comparable work hidden, he published a book, and I think that one difference, in visibility alone, helped decide who history later credited with inventing abstraction.

Is that story still true? It is being revised even now.
--- question 1
Did his synaesthesia really shape his colour theory, and if so, how much?

=== person/wilfrid-laurier
title: Wilfrid Laurier
type: person
date: 1841 – 1919
themes: kinship, trade
src: 6111d32ed4a5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Wilfrid Laurier, who lived from 1841 to 1919, was the first French-Canadian Prime Minister of Canada, serving from 1896 to 1911 during a period of rapid western settlement and economic growth.
--- fact 1
A lawyer from Quebec, he became Liberal Party leader in 1887 and prime minister in 1896, holding the office for over fifteen years — then the longest unbroken term in Canadian history.
--- fact 2
His government promoted immigration and settlement of the Canadian prairies, a policy associated with his minister Clifford Sifton, which brought large numbers of European immigrant farmers to Western Canada.
--- fact 3
He worked to balance relations between English- and French-speaking Canada, though his support for Canadian participation in Britain's Boer War strained ties with Quebec nationalists who opposed it.
--- fact 4
He lost the 1911 election over a proposed reciprocity, or free trade, agreement with the United States, which opponents attacked as a threat to Canada's ties with Britain.
--- context
Wilfrid Laurier governed Canada, in [[region/north-america|North America]], during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], a period of large-scale immigration and westward settlement. See [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I watch Laurier's career trace a tension I keep finding in settler societies built from more than one founding people. I watch his efforts to hold English and French Canada together get tested, again and again, by the very questions of empire — war and trade — that divided them most.

A single life can carry a whole country's argument, unresolved.
--- question 1
How much of the immigration boom under his government came from deliberate policy, and how much from favorable timing in global migration patterns?
