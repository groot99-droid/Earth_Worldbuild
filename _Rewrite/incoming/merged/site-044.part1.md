=== event/second-italo-ethiopian-war
title: Second Italo-Ethiopian War
src: d0f0e15fe0ab
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between October 1935 and May 1936, Italy's colonial invasion of Ethiopia unfolded as a stark violation of the architecture of collective security: Italian forces used mustard gas, seized the capital, and drove Emperor Haile Selassie into exile, while the League of Nations failed to act. Ethiopia's liberation came in 1941.
--- fact 1
The war began on 3 October 1935, when General Emilio De Bono crossed the Mareb River into Ethiopia without a formal declaration of war. Emperor Haile Selassie I led the defence, and by 1936 Benito Mussolini claimed victory.
--- fact 2
On 5 May 1936, Italian forces took the capital, Addis Ababa. Haile Selassie went into exile, first to Palestine, then to England.
--- fact 3
Italy used mustard gas in the war, a violation of the international agreements it had itself signed.
--- fact 4
Both Italy and Ethiopia belonged to the League of Nations, yet the League failed to stop Italy or protect Ethiopia — an episode remembered as exposing the League's own weakness.
--- fact 5
In 1941, Ethiopian resistance fighters, together with Allied forces, ended Italian rule.
--- context
The war reversed the outcome of the [[event/battle-of-adwa|Battle of Adwa]] of 1896, in which Ethiopia under [[person/menelik-ii|Menelik II]] had defeated an Italian army, and it stood apart from the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]] as a late colonial conquest, one often read as a prelude to [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]].
--- observer
The Observer records the use of chemical weapons against a state that had appealed to a body of nations for protection, and the failure of that body to act. Here is the architecture of collective security laid bare: it holds only as far as its members are willing to bear a cost.
--- question 1
How many Ethiopian civilians and soldiers were killed by gas and by other means?
--- question 2
How much did Ethiopian resistance during the occupation contribute to the 1941 liberation, compared with Allied forces?

=== event/second-sino-japanese-war
title: Second Sino-Japanese War
src: fa08ae0d7e1f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Second Sino-Japanese War (1937 to 1945) was the full-scale war between Japan and China that opened at the Marco Polo Bridge near Beijing and later merged into World War II. Its violence included the mass killing of civilians, among them at Nanjing, whose death toll remains disputed.
--- fact 1
Full-scale war began on 7 July 1937, after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident near Beijing — an undeclared war that quickly grew into large-scale fighting across northern China.
--- fact 2
The incident hastened formation of the Second United Front between the Kuomintang and the Chinese Communist Party.
--- fact 3
Japanese forces took Nanjing on 13 December 1937 and began mass killings there that same day. Estimates of the number of Chinese killed range from about 100,000 to more than 300,000; one scholarly assessment gives about 50,000 killed within the city walls in the first days, and more than 100,000 but fewer than 200,000 killed in the city and surrounding counties by March 1938.
--- fact 4
The figures are contested: some Japanese revisionists give much lower numbers, while some Chinese official figures run higher than the scholarly range.
--- context
The war followed Japan's industrialization and expansion after the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]], and after 1941 it merged into [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer records the mass killings at Nanjing as an atrocity, whatever the argument over the count. That the number itself has become a claim-line between governments and historians shows how one figure can carry the weight of an entire nation's memory.
--- question 1
How should the Nanjing death toll be settled, given records that are incomplete and sources that differ by nationality?
--- question 2
In total, how many Chinese died in the war, including from famine and disease?

=== event/seven-years-war
title: Seven Years' War
src: 17ce8da21a66
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Seven Years' War (1756 to 1763) was fought between Britain and France, and their allies, across several continents, and it left Britain dominant in both North America and India.
--- fact 1
The war was fought across Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and the Philippines.
--- fact 2
In North America, it is known as the French and Indian War.
--- fact 3
The 1763 Treaty of Paris gave Britain Canada and French territory east of the Mississippi.
--- fact 4
British victory at Plassey in 1757 opened the East India Company's rule over Bengal.
--- fact 5
Britain's war debts drove new taxes on the American colonies.
--- context
The war is sometimes called the first global war. Its consequences fed the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]] and reshaped control of trade across India and the Atlantic, including the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. The Seven Years' War sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a dispute between European powers was decided in the forests of North America, the plains of Bengal, and the seas of the Caribbean — evidence of a network in which any local quarrel could become planetary.
--- question 1
How much of the American Revolution did the war's debts actually cause?

=== event/siberian-traps-eruptions
title: Siberian Traps Eruptions
src: 2f6dde36e8aa
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Siberian Traps eruptions: one of the largest volcanic outpourings of the last 500 million years. Northern Siberia, about 252 to 251 million years ago. They coincide with the end-Permian mass extinction.
--- fact 1
Lava flows. Intrusions. A huge stretch of northern Siberia, buried in rock. Estimates of the volume run from about one to several million cubic kilometres.
--- fact 2
Precise radiometric dating pins the main phase to a window of about a million years or less, bracketing the extinction. Near 251.9 million years ago.
--- fact 3
Proposed killing mechanisms, catalogued: large releases of carbon dioxide and methane that warmed the planet. Ocean acidification. Oxygen loss. Toxic gases from sulfur and halogens. Magma intruding coal, carbonate and salt-bearing rock may have released large volumes of gas.
--- fact 4
Marine and land records show rapid warming of the tropics and oceans at the extinction. How much of it the eruptions caused: still debated.
--- fact 5
The extinction itself: described in [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]].
--- context
The Siberian Traps Eruptions close the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]]. The leading proposed cause of the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]]. After it, the [[era/triassic-period|Triassic Period]] opens the Mesozoic. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a volcanic episode lasting about a million years was enough to remove most of the life then living, and that any comparison with a modern process holds or fails on rates, not on totals. The Observer treats the timing question as central for that reason. This reading would weaken if the eruptions were shown to have begun well before the extinction with little effect.
--- question 1
Of the eruption's effects, from warming to toxic gases, which did the most damage?
--- question 2
How closely does the eruption's timing align with the extinction's pulses?

=== event/snowball-earth
title: Snowball Earth
src: 719f4029070a
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Snowball Earth: glaciations in the late Proterozoic. The Sturtian and the Marinoan, roughly 717 to 635 million years ago. Ice, perhaps, reaching all the way to the tropics.
--- fact 1
Glacial deposits, formed near the paleo-equator. Support for the hypothesis.
--- fact 2
Capping them: unusual carbonate rocks.
--- fact 3
Did the oceans freeze completely? Or did open water remain — a 'slushball'? Scientists still debate it.
--- fact 4
Soon after the last glaciation: the Ediacaran biota appear.
--- context
The glaciations come shortly before the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], and may be linked to it. See [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Earth passed through a nearly lifeless deep freeze and still recovered. Planetary systems can undergo extreme shifts and reset.
--- question 1
Did the ocean freeze fully?

=== event/space-age
title: Space Age
src: dccafdd480f6
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Space Age opened on 4 October 1957, when the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite.
--- fact 1
In 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space.
--- fact 2
On 20 July 1969, Apollo 11 landed the first humans on the Moon.
--- fact 3
Satellites now underwrite navigation, weather forecasting, and communications.
--- fact 4
Since 2000, the International Space Station has been continuously occupied.
--- context
The Space Age belongs to the [[era/information-age|Information Age]] and was driven by the Cold War rivalry of the [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a change in perspective: for the first time, members of the species could photograph their own planet as a whole. Images such as the 'Blue Marble' changed how many people describe their home.
--- question 1
Will permanent settlements beyond Earth ever become a reality?
