=== place/mecca
src: f3aa9f7a9b47
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Mecca is a city in the Hejaz region of western Saudi Arabia, about 70 kilometers inland from the Red Sea, the birthplace of Muhammad and the holiest city of Islam; its sanctuary around the Kaaba draws the annual Hajj pilgrimage like rivers to a single sea.
--- fact 1
When Mecca was first settled is unknown, and whether it is the place Macoraba in Ptolemy's 2nd-century geography is disputed; Muslim sources written after Muhammad's time describe a sanctuary town governed by the Quraysh in the century before Islam.
--- fact 2
Islamic tradition places Muhammad's birth in Mecca about 570 CE, his move to Medina in 622, and Mecca's passing under Muslim control in 630 with little fighting.
--- fact 3
The Hajj is one of the Five Pillars of Islam, required once in a lifetime of every Muslim able to make the journey; Saudi authorities have reported yearly attendance in the low millions in recent decades.
--- fact 4
Historians disagree about Mecca's weight in long-distance trade before Islam; some see it as a regional sanctuary and market town rather than a hub of the incense and spice trades.
--- fact 5
Mecca was ruled locally by the Sharifs under a succession of overlords, the Ottomans among them from 1517, until the Saudi conquest of 1924 to 1925; non-Muslims are barred from entering the city.
--- fact 6
In the crush at Mina, near Mecca, during the 2015 Hajj, the Saudi authorities reported 769 deaths, while counts gathered from other governments' figures exceeded 2,000.
--- context
Mecca is the central place of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], the city Muhammad left in the Hijra for [[place/medina|Medina]], and a stop in the peninsula's wider [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]] setting. It passed under the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]] and later the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]], and pilgrims came from far away, among them the ruler of the [[culture/mali-empire|Mali Empire]], Mansa Musa, who made the Hajj in 1324. Mecca belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I notice a meaning-engine that draws millions of strangers to one point each year, a recurring exchange-web of people, goods and news reaching from West Africa to Southeast Asia. I notice the plain white garments (for men, two unstitched cloths), which lessen the visible differences of rank during the rites. I notice that pilgrims themselves describe the journey as obedience and devotion, and I mean this reading of its social effects to sit beside their account, never to replace it.

I notice, too, that crowd disasters such as Mina in 2015 are a recurring cost of gathering at this scale, and I record them as suffering. How many come, and how many do not go home? Every gathering this large carries both.

The reading would weaken if pilgrim networks turn out to have carried little trade or news compared with their devotional purpose.
--- question 1
What does archaeology reveal about pre-Islamic Mecca, given how little excavation has been done in the sacred precinct?
--- question 2
How large a role did Mecca play in long-distance trade before Islam?

=== place/nan-madol
src: 3838d424d21b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Nan Madol is a ruined city of artificial stone islets raised in a lagoon off Pohnpei, in the Federated States of Micronesia, the ceremonial and political heart of the Saudeleur dynasty from about the 12th century until around 1628, a city laid on the water like scattered stones.
--- fact 1
Nan Madol is made of about 92 artificial islets built from stacked basalt columns and coral fill, joined by tidal canals, over a core area of about 1.5 by 0.5 kilometers.
--- fact 2
People were active at the site from as early as the 1st or 2nd century CE, islet building began in the 8th or 9th century, and the megalithic stonework that gives the site its present shape rose from about 1180 to 1200.
--- fact 3
Tradition holds that the Saudeleur dynasty ruled Pohnpei from Nan Madol, uniting an estimated population of about 25,000 on the island, though the number living at Nan Madol itself was probably under 1,000.
--- fact 4
Around 1628, tradition says, a warrior named Isokelekel invaded and ended Saudeleur rule; some scholars also point to population decline as a contributing cause of the site's abandonment.
--- fact 5
UNESCO inscribed Nan Madol as a World Heritage Site in 2016.
--- context
Nan Madol's builders were Pohnpeians, speakers of a Micronesian language, and part of the wider settlement of the Pacific carried by the [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]]; Pohnpei's first settlers probably came from the [[culture/lapita-culture|Lapita Culture]] of the southeast Solomon Islands or Vanuatu, farther south and east. They were not Polynesians: the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] is a separate movement within the same expansion. It belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I notice a lagoon turned into a claim-line: a ruling lineage built its seat on stone islets cut off from the rest of the island by water, a status-signal that set the center of power physically apart from those it ruled. What does it mean to rule from across water? It means being seen, and not reached.

I notice that the tradition of Isokelekel's conquest, like many founding and ending stories, is ancestor-weight carried forward by the community, not a document, and I hold it as a claim to be tested rather than a settled fact.

The reading would weaken if archaeology shows the site's decline came mainly from environmental or demographic causes rather than conquest.
--- question 1
How much of the Isokelekel tradition reflects a real conquest, and how much is later legend explaining the end of Saudeleur rule?
--- question 2
How was the labor of moving and stacking the basalt columns organized, given Pohnpei's small population?
