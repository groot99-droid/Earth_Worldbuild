=== person/georges-melies
title: Georges Méliès
type: person
date: 1861 – 1938
themes: art, technology
src: 2be49b6ae2a7
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Georges Méliès (1861 to 1938) began as a Paris stage magician before becoming a filmmaker who invented cinema's first special effects. Across roughly 500 films made in under twenty years, including A Trip to the Moon, 1902, he turned the new camera from a device for recording reality into a machine for staging the impossible.
--- fact 1
Méliès was born in Paris to a prosperous shoe-manufacturing family and wanted to study painting, but his father would not support him as an artist, so he supervised machinery at the family factory instead. In about 1888 he bought the Théâtre Robert-Houdin, a famous conjuring theatre.
--- fact 2
After attending the Lumière brothers' first public film screening in December 1895, he tried to buy a camera from them and was refused; he acquired one instead from a British inventor and built a studio at Montreuil with a glass-walled stage.
--- fact 3
About 1896 he discovered, by accident, when his camera jammed mid-shot, that stopping and restarting filming could make objects vanish or transform — the "stop trick" behind his trick films.
--- fact 4
His techniques included stop-motion substitution, multiple exposure, dissolves, painted sets built to force perspective, and hand-tinted colour applied frame by frame by a team of women colourists.
--- fact 5
American piracy of his films, the rise of narrative studio filmmaking, and the disruption of the First World War ruined his business; he was reduced to running a toy stall in a Paris train station.
--- fact 6
He was rediscovered in his last decade, awarded the Légion d'honneur, and, by some accounts, given a small pension; he died in Paris in 1938, and Martin Scorsese's 2011 film Hugo dramatised his life.
--- fact 7
Key works include The Vanishing Lady, 1896; A Trip to the Moon, 1902; The Kingdom of the Fairies, 1903; and The Impossible Voyage, 1904.
--- context
Méliès worked in France, in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], alongside the [[person/lumiere-brothers|Lumière Brothers]]; his business collapsed in the aftermath of [[event/world-war-i|World War I]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I see a magician look at a recording device and find a stage: the same machine that caught ordinary life in the Lumières' hands made the impossible in his. I hear the story of the jammed camera, the accident that became a trick, and I hold it the way any origin story should be held — as an account, not a proven fact.

Piracy and the market for narrative film ruined him in the end, and I see in that an old story: the copy outrunning the maker. What invention has ever stayed owned by the one who made it?
--- question 1
Did the camera jam actually happen as told, and when?

=== person/gilgamesh
title: Gilgamesh
type: person
date: c. 2800 – 2500 BCE
themes: religion, kinship, art
src: 1832af00570d
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Gilgamesh ruled the Sumerian city of Uruk, probably a real king of the Early Dynastic period sometime between about 2800 and 2500 BCE, who became the subject of the Epic of Gilgamesh, the earliest surviving major work of narrative literature.
--- fact 1
The Sumerian King List names him as the fifth king of Uruk's first dynasty and credits him with an implausible 126-year reign, typical of how the list treats early rulers.
--- fact 2
Later inscriptions, notably the Tummal Inscription written under Ishbi-Erra long after the Early Dynastic period, credit a king of this name with building the great city walls of Uruk, a claim the Epic itself repeats as his lasting achievement.
--- fact 3
Assembled from older Sumerian poems into its best-known Akkadian version around the 12th century BCE, the Epic follows Gilgamesh's friendship with the wild man Enkidu, his quest for immortality after Enkidu's death, and his eventual acceptance of mortality.
--- fact 4
No securely contemporary royal inscription survives from Gilgamesh's own lifetime, though one inscription, possibly from a contemporary official at Ur, may name him; most of what historians infer about a real Gilgamesh comes instead from later king lists, dedicatory inscriptions naming him, and the literary tradition itself.
--- context
Gilgamesh reigned over Uruk among the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I find him standing at a strange boundary: probably a real king, almost certainly not the demigod the Epic makes of him, and known to me mainly because later scribes turned him into a figure wrestling with death, not because his own deeds were kept apart and recorded.

I try to draw the line between the ruler and the character, and I cannot fully find it. What remains of a man when only the story of him survives?
--- question 1
How much of the historical Gilgamesh's biography, beyond the city walls, can be separated from the literary tradition built around his name?

=== person/giotto
title: Giotto
type: person
date: c. 1267 – 1337
themes: art, religion
src: a715551b9fca
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Giotto di Bondone (about 1267 to 1337) was a Florentine painter who gave medieval figures weight, emotion and space. Many call him the father of Western painting, and his frescoes in the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua stand as his firmest work.
--- fact 1
Giotto was born about 1267, traditionally in the Mugello valley north of Florence, though his birthplace is disputed and some recent research points instead to Florence itself. Vasari's story of the shepherd boy discovered by Cimabue is legend, but he was almost certainly trained in Florentine workshops.
--- fact 2
He was traditionally credited with the Legend of St Francis frescoes at Assisi in the late 1290s, but it is now generally accepted that four different hands are identifiable there, coming from Rome, which makes his authorship doubtful; his early career remains debated.
--- fact 3
He painted the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua about 1303 to 1305 for the banker Enrico Scrovegni; it is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
--- fact 4
He worked in Rome, at Santa Croce in Florence, and at the court of King Robert of Anjou in Naples from about 1328 to 1333.
--- fact 5
In 1334 Florence named him master of works for the cathedral, and he designed its bell tower; he died in January 1337 and was buried with public honours.
--- fact 6
He worked in buon fresco, painting into wet plaster one day's section at a time; his figures carry volume and react to one another, and his spaces suggest depth more than 100 years before linear perspective was worked out.
--- fact 7
In the chapel's Adoration of the Magi the star appears as a blazing comet, perhaps inspired by Halley's Comet of 1301; the European Space Agency named its 1986 Halley probe Giotto.
--- fact 8
Vasari opened his Lives of the Artists with Cimabue and Giotto, and Masaccio and [[person/michelangelo|Michelangelo]] studied and copied Giotto's frescoes.
--- context
Giotto worked within [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], ahead of the [[event/renaissance|Renaissance]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I know the subject never changed — the same sacred story told again in every church — and I see that what changed was how deeply it was made to be felt. His figures glance, they grieve, and a sacred scene becomes a human one before my eyes.

I hear him called "the father of Western painting," and I know that title comes mostly from Vasari and the writers after him who needed a founder for their story of revival: part legend, built to hold a history together, not a plain finding. His early years remain debated, his hand at Assisi now doubtful, since that cycle is generally thought today to belong to painters from Rome. What founder is not partly invented by those who came after?
--- question 1
How much, if any, of the Assisi Legend of St Francis cycle is by Giotto, given that it is now generally thought to be the work of Roman painters?

=== person/gitarja
title: Gitarja
type: person
date: c. 1328 – 1350
themes: war, trade, religion
src: 14ff22fbf567
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Tribhuwana Wijayatunggadewi, known as Gitarja, ruled as the third monarch of the Majapahit empire, reigning in Java from about 1328 to 1350; her birth is placed before 1309 and her death about 1372 to 1375, though both dates remain uncertain.
--- fact 1
She became queen after the assassination of her half-brother Jayanegara in 1328 — the Nagarakretagama gives 1329 for her accession — ruling in the name of her mother, Gayatri Rajapatni, who had formally retired into a Buddhist convent.
--- fact 2
Her chief minister, Gajah Mada, is credited with a famous oath: to abstain from certain pleasures until he had brought the surrounding archipelago under Majapahit's authority.
--- fact 3
Under her reign, Majapahit forces conquered Bali in 1343, defeating the kingdoms of Pejeng and Bedahulu, a major step in the empire's expansion across the Indonesian archipelago.
--- fact 4
She stepped down in 1350 after her mother's death, succeeded by her son, Hayam Wuruk, under whom Majapahit reached its greatest territorial extent.
--- context
Gitarja ruled [[culture/majapahit|Majapahit]] in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], and her minister's campaigns laid the ground for the empire's height under her son. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Gitarja's reign is remembered mainly through the ambitions of her minister rather than through any directly recorded decision of her own, a common pattern for royal women whose authority depended on men who did the fighting and the writing. Whether she directed Gajah Mada's campaigns, or simply did not stop them, cannot be recovered from the surviving sources.
--- question 1
How much of Majapahit's expansion under Gajah Mada reflects Gitarja's own policy rather than his independent initiative?

=== person/gorgo
title: Gorgo
type: person
date: c. 518 – 480 BCE
themes: war, kinship
src: 56a52e28ef85
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Gorgo, born about 518 BCE and last attested around 480 BCE, reigned as queen of Sparta as the wife of King Leonidas I, remembered by the historian Herodotus as unusually sharp-witted and politically perceptive for a woman in his account of Greek affairs.
--- fact 1
She was the daughter of the Spartan king Cleomenes I, and, according to Herodotus, advised him as a child against trusting a bribe offered by an Ionian envoy seeking Spartan military aid.
--- fact 2
Herodotus credits her with detecting a warning about the Persian king Xerxes's invasion plans hidden on a wax-covered writing tablet sent by the exiled Spartan king Demaratus, a message other Spartans could not work out.
--- fact 3
She was wife to Leonidas I, who died leading the defense of Thermopylae against the Persian invasion in 480 BCE, the same year she is last clearly attested in the sources.
--- fact 4
Nearly everything known of her comes from Herodotus's Histories, written decades after her lifetime; no independent Spartan record of her actions survives.
--- context
Gorgo reigned as queen of Sparta, among the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]], in the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Herodotus, writing for a Greek audience that saw Spartan women as unusually outspoken compared to other Greek women, may have shaped Gorgo's anecdotes to fit that reputation as much as to record specific events; the stories flatter her personal intelligence and a wider Greek stereotype about Spartan women at the same time.
--- question 1
How much of Gorgo's characterization in Herodotus reflects specific memory of her versus a general Greek stereotype about outspoken Spartan women?
