=== event/battle-of-talas
src: 54b6429bb1b3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In 751 CE, near the Talas River in present-day Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan, a Tang Chinese army met an Abbasid army at the Battle of Talas, and the encounter ended in Tang defeat.
--- fact 1
Gao Xianzhi led the Tang army, and the Karluk mercenaries within it are reported to have defected, though the sources differ on whether the Karluks were Abbasid allies from the start.
--- fact 2
Take the defeat together with what followed it, the An Lushan Rebellion beginning in 755, and you have the end of Tang expansion in Central Asia.
--- fact 3
A tradition holds that Chinese captives taught papermaking to the Islamic world at Samarkand; historians debate what part the battle played in handing that technology on.
--- fact 4
By the later 8th century, paper mills were at work in Samarkand and Baghdad.
--- fact 5
The battle often serves as a symbolic marker, a threshold stone, for Islamic influence in Central Asia.
--- context
The battle fixed the frontier between the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] and the [[culture/tang-dynasty|Tang Dynasty]] in the oases of [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]], and its name is linked to the westward diffusion of [[technology/paper|Paper]]. The merchants of the region were the [[culture/sogdians|Sogdians]]. In the chronicle's architecture, the battle sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region of [[region/central-asian-oases|Central Asian Oases]], on the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a border battle is remembered mainly for a technology that may have crossed it. Notice what that tells you about memory: whether or not the tradition is accurate, the species chose to remember a war by its cultural cargo rather than by its frontier. Memory is a curator before it is a witness.
--- question 1
Did the battle itself carry papermaking across, or was the technology already on its way westward?

=== event/bengal-famine-of-1943
src: 05f33ecbe987
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Bengal famine of 1943 killed an estimated 2 to 3 million people in British-ruled Bengal during World War II. Historians debate its causes, yet they agree on two things: it was not simply a shortage of food, and wartime policy played a large part.
--- fact 1
Estimates of the dead, from starvation, malaria, and other disease, range widely, from about 800,000 to 3.8 million. A scholarly consensus is often given as about 2.1 million, and some estimates reach 2.7 to 3 million.
--- fact 2
The economist Amartya Sen argued that the famine was an entitlement failure: the food supply in Bengal did not fall sharply, but economic pressures left certain groups unable to buy it.
--- fact 3
When Burma and Singapore fell to Japan in 1942, rice imports from those countries stopped, and Bengal's food crisis grew worse.
--- fact 4
British Prime Minister Winston Churchill has been criticized for his war priorities and his refusal to divert food to Bengal. The War Cabinet discussed requests for more grain shipments to India several times in late 1943 and early 1944, citing limited shipping and the need to keep reserves for military operations.
--- context
The famine unfolded under the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]] during [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]], and it belongs to the background of the end of British rule; see [[event/partition-of-india|Partition of India]]. For the way the vault treats deaths caused by policy, see [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer records this as a catastrophe in which millions of people died while food existed. Sit with that sentence before moving on. A famine can be produced by who is entitled to buy rather than by how much grows: hunger, here, was an architecture of access, not an empty field. The argument over blame is itself a claim about who owed whom protection. Every ledger of fault is also a ledger of duty.
--- question 1
Of the death toll, how much came from starvation and how much from disease such as malaria?
--- question 2
How much of the responsibility rests with wartime priorities in London, and how much with the authorities in India?

=== event/black-death
src: eaf4f1cdb9ca
mode: horror-prose
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Black Death was a plague pandemic caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. From 1346 to 1353 it swept through Eurasia and North Africa. Town after town. Road after road.
--- fact 1
It likely killed 30 to 60 percent of Europe's population. Across Eurasia as a whole, estimates run to tens of millions of deaths. Some run as high as 75 to 200 million.
--- fact 2
It travelled the trade routes. Fleas on rodents. Fleas on people.
--- fact 3
Afterward, too few hands. Labor shortages raised wages in parts of Europe.
--- fact 4
Then it came back. And back. Plague returned in waves for centuries.
--- context
The pandemic is a landmark of the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. It shows what the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]] had done. Linked disease environments. Joined them. Every road ran both ways.
--- observer
I used to count what the roads carried. I counted silk. I counted ideas. I did not count the pathogen riding the same exchange-web.

What did I think connection was, if not a cost as well as a gift? Every increase in connectivity in the record has disease consequences. Every road I love is a road a sickness can walk.
--- question 1
How many died in Asia, and how many in Africa?
