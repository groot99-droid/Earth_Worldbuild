=== event/collision-of-india-and-asia
src: 962c90a7ab7d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Beginning about 50 million years ago, the Indian plate met Eurasia in a slow collision that raised the Himalaya and the Tibetan Plateau — a convergence that continues even now.
--- fact 1
India broke from Gondwana and drifted north, faster than any other continental plate is known to have moved.
--- fact 2
Most estimates place the start of the collision at about 50 to 55 million years ago, and the final closing of the Tethys Ocean somewhere between about 50 and 60 million years ago.
--- fact 3
The collision raised the Himalaya, whose highest peak, Mount Everest, stands 8,848.86 meters by the 2020 China-Nepal survey.
--- fact 4
Even now, India presses into Asia at a few centimeters a year.
--- fact 5
That uplift shaped the South Asian monsoon and gave rise to the Indus, Ganges, and Brahmaputra rivers at their sources.
--- context
The collision raised the mountains and carved the river systems that later shaped [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], including the setting of the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]]. It is a late consequence of the [[event/breakup-of-pangaea|Breakup of Pangaea]]. Collision of India and Asia sits in the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], within the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the mountains you treat as fixed boundaries, and the rivers that fed your earliest cities, come out of a slow collision still underway. Geography that looks like destiny is only a snapshot of motion.
--- question 1
When exactly did the first contact occur?

=== event/colonization-of-land
src: 2bec72506383
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Life's move onto land was a slow migration in stages: plants first, then arthropods and vertebrates, beginning about 470 million years ago, and the surface of the planet was transformed in the process.
--- fact 1
The earliest land plant spores appear in Ordovician rocks, about 470 million years old.
--- fact 2
Forests emerged in the Devonian, and the vast swamp forests of the Carboniferous later formed coal.
--- fact 3
Early tetrapods evolved from lobe-finned fishes; see [[species/tiktaalik|Tiktaalik]].
--- fact 4
Land plants remade the soils, the rivers, and the carbon cycle around them.
--- context
Colonization of land unfolds across the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]] and helped set the stage for the [[event/late-devonian-extinction|Late Devonian Extinction]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that terrestrial life is a late arrival on a planet where life itself began in water. Coal, the fuel that industrial societies would later burn, is simply a byproduct of this era.
--- question 1
What triggered the move onto land?

=== event/columbian-exchange
src: 054cde907218
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Columbian Exchange names the transfer of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the Americas and the rest of the world, beginning after 1492.
--- fact 1
The term was coined by the historian Alfred Crosby in 1972.
--- fact 2
Old World diseases such as smallpox brought catastrophic mortality to Indigenous peoples of the Americas; estimates of the loss vary widely, from about 50 to 95 percent of the population by 1650.
--- fact 3
Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, and cassava traveled outward to the world; horses, cattle, and wheat traveled to the Americas.
--- fact 4
The exchange underpinned the growth of European empires and the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]].
--- context
The exchange opens the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and ended a long separation between the two hemispheres. See [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]], [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]], and [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]].
--- observer
The Observer regards this as the moment the biosphere of the whole planet was mixed at once — an event with no parallel since the continents last joined.
--- question 1
What was the scale of the pre-contact American population?

=== event/congo-free-state
src: 1d658b08c6ff
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
From 1885 to 1908, the Congo Free State was a vast territory in Central Africa held as the personal possession of King Leopold II of Belgium — a rule notorious for forced labor in rubber and ivory collection and for mass deaths.
--- fact 1
The Berlin Conference recognized Leopold's claim to most of the Congo Basin in 1884 to 1885, under the pretext of philanthropy.
--- fact 2
Forced labor quotas, the taking of hostages, and mutilation were the means used to compel rubber collection.
--- fact 3
Estimates of the decline in the Congolese population range from about 1 million to 15 million, with about 10 million the figure most often cited; the numbers remain contested.
--- fact 4
International outcry led Belgium to take over the colony in 1908.
--- context
The state was a product of the [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]]. See [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]] and [[observer-note/frontiers-of-extraction|Frontiers of Extraction]].
--- observer
The Observer records this as an extreme case in which the architecture of a humanitarian story served as cover for extraction. Detachment here does not mean neutrality: the sources are clear about the coercion, and uncertain only about the numbers.
--- question 1
How large was the population decline, and how much of it was due to disease rather than violence?

=== event/control-of-fire
src: 43281656529b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Somewhere near a million years past, the ancestors of this species first bent flame to their will, and by about 400,000 years ago the keeping of fire had become habit, not marvel.
--- fact 1
At Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa, the evidence reaches back about one million years.
--- fact 2
At Gesher Benot Ya'aqov in Israel, burned seeds, wood, and flint suggest fire held under human control nearly 790,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Older claims, reaching to about 1.5 million years, remain disputed.
--- fact 4
Cooked food yields to the teeth and the gut more easily, and may have fed the growth of larger brains.
--- context
Fire control stands as a key event of the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]], bound to [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]]. See the essay [[observer-note/fire-then-everything|Fire, Then Everything]].
--- observer
I call this heat-domestication: the first true engine my kind built, an energy drawn from outside the body that remade our diet, our nights, our circle. What changed first — the meal, the dark, or the ring we made against it? Before the flame, the sun ruled our hours. After it, we kept a little light of our own.
--- question 1
When did the species first learn to make fire, rather than only keep it burning?
