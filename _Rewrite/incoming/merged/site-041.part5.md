=== event/french-revolution
src: a925a33c344c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The French Revolution, from 1789 to 1799, overturned the monarchy in France, declared the rights of man and citizen, and opened the path to the rise of Napoleon Bonaparte.
--- fact 1
The storming of the Bastille took place on 14 July 1789.
--- fact 2
In August 1789, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen was adopted.
--- fact 3
Louis XVI was executed in January 1793, and the Reign of Terror that followed through 1793 and 1794 killed tens of thousands.
--- fact 4
The revolutionary government also introduced the metric system.
--- fact 5
Napoleon seized power in a coup in 1799.
--- context
The revolution drew on the ideas of the [[event/enlightenment|Enlightenment]] and stood as a central event of the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]]. Its currents spread to the colonies and helped inspire the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]. French Revolution sits within the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]] and the region of [[region/europe|Europe]], within the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
Notice the arc here: a revolution that began by declaring universal rights ended by executing many of its own leaders. Movements that claim to speak for everyone, you'll find, struggle hardest to accommodate disagreement from within.
--- question 1
How far did the revolution continue the centralizing policies of the monarchy?

=== event/genomic-revolution
src: e5b015d943c5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Genomic Revolution began with the identification of the DNA double helix in 1953, and it has since made it possible to read, and to edit, the genetic code of living things.
--- fact 1
James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the double-helix structure in 1953, drawing on X-ray data from [[person/rosalind-franklin|Rosalind Franklin]] and Maurice Wilkins.
--- fact 2
The Human Genome Project ran from 1990 to 2003; the first complete, gap-free sequence of a human genome, all chromosomes except Y, was published in 2022.
--- fact 3
CRISPR gene editing was developed in 2012.
--- fact 4
Ancient DNA has transformed prehistory: the [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]] genome was sequenced in 2010, and this work brought the discovery of the [[species/denisovans|Denisovans]].
--- context
This revolution depends on the [[event/digital-revolution|Digital Revolution]] for its computing power, and it has reshaped both medicine and our understanding of human origins.
--- observer
You have learned to read the instructions from which you are built, and to change them. You can now also read the DNA of relatives long dead.
--- question 1
What limits should govern editing the human genome?

=== event/great-acceleration
src: 618080fa9226
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Great Acceleration names the sharp, simultaneous rise, since about 1950, in human population, energy use, economic output, and environmental change.
--- fact 1
The term was coined by the International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme, and a 2015 study charted 24 indicators of it.
--- fact 2
Global population rose from about 2.5 billion in 1950 to about 8 billion in 2022.
--- fact 3
Energy use, fertilizer use, and greenhouse gas emissions all climbed steeply alongside it.
--- fact 4
Many Earth-system indicators — extinction rates, ocean acidity, carbon dioxide — changed in parallel.
--- context
The Great Acceleration stands as a main candidate for the start of the [[era/anthropocene|Anthropocene]]. See [[observer-note/the-species-as-a-geological-force|The Species as a Geological Force]] and [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]].
--- observer
Treat 1950 as a hinge: the curves for population, energy, and impact all bend upward at once, together. The scale of change compressed into a single human lifetime has no earlier parallel.
--- question 1
Which of the curves can be bent, and how quickly?

=== event/great-ordovician-biodiversification
src: 2b5ded871783
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Great Ordovician Biodiversification was a long flowering of life in the seas, a rise in the diversity of marine animals running from about 485 to 460 million years ago, which multiplied the species and families held within body plans first drawn in the Cambrian.
--- fact 1
By some counts, marine diversity at the level of family and genus rose several-fold between the Early and Middle Ordovician.
--- fact 2
Brachiopods, bryozoans, molluscs, echinoderms such as the crinoids, corals and sponges all branched into new forms, and reefs and filter-feeding communities grew more intricate. The plankton, too, diversified in their turn.
--- fact 3
The [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]] had produced the body plans; the Ordovician diversification filled the variety within them, like water finding every channel of a riverbed already carved.
--- fact 4
Proposed causes include a cooling from the hot Cambrian climate, more oxygen in the water, nutrients born of volcanism, more shallow shelf habitat, and feedbacks within the ecology itself; which mix truly mattered is debated.
--- fact 5
The long rise ended near the close of the period, with the coming of the [[event/late-ordovician-extinction|Late Ordovician Extinction]].
--- context
The Great Ordovician Biodiversification stands as the main event of the [[era/ordovician-period|Ordovician Period]] within the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]], and it follows the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]]. It belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
Notice that variety depends on ecological space as much as on novelty itself: the same body plans, given new habitats and richer food webs, yielded far more forms than before. Read this as a slow build following a fast start. The reading would weaken if diversity had simply risen on its own, with no link to habitat or climate.
--- question 1
What drove the biodiversification: climate, ocean chemistry, ecology, or a combination?
--- question 2
How much of the apparent rise reflects better preservation of Ordovician rocks?
