=== species/horse
title: Horse
src: fa8c443348fd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic horse descends from a lineage that a 2021 genetic study traced to the Pontic-Caspian steppe by about 2200 BCE, and once it bore riders and drew chariots it transformed warfare, transport and steppe societies across Eurasia, as a wind reshapes the grass it runs through.
--- fact 1
A 2021 study of 264 ancient genomes followed modern domestic horses back to a lineage that arose in the Volga-Don region of the Pontic-Caspian steppe by about 2200 BCE; before then horses ran wild across much of the continent, and that older history lies outside this note.
--- fact 2
The Botai culture of Kazakhstan (about 3700 to 3500 BCE) left horse teeth worn in a way consistent with bits, and traces of horse milking, but a 2018 study found modern domestic horses are not closely related to Botai horses, whose feral descendants are the Przewalski's horses, and some researchers hold that the Botai people hunted and managed wild horses without fully domesticating them.
--- fact 3
Horse-drawn chariots appear in Sintashta and Petrovka graves dated about 2100 to 1700 BCE; within perhaps 500 years chariot evidence surfaces in Greece, Egypt and Mesopotamia, and within another 500 years in China, the sound of wheels carried like fire along dry grass.
--- fact 4
Horse bones appear in Mesopotamia from about 2000 BCE, where horses began to take the place of hybrid equids in war; in the Iron Age mounted cavalry rose, and mounted archers in time displaced the chariot.
--- fact 5
Columbus brought horses to Hispaniola on his 1493 voyage, and Hernán Cortés landed in Mexico in 1519 with 16 horses, a return across a gulf of ages, for wild horses had vanished from the Americas at least 10,000 years earlier.
--- fact 6
After the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, horses left by the Spanish passed among Pueblo, Apache and Comanche communities like a spark through tinder, and by the late 1700s most peoples of the western plains were mounted.
--- context
The horse first shaped the long song of human history through the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]] and the [[technology/chariot|Chariot]], after the earlier and separate [[culture/botai-culture|Botai Culture]]; later, mounted steppe peoples such as the [[culture/scythians|Scythians]], the [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]] and the [[culture/mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] built their power upon its back. Its return to the Americas belongs to the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. The horse stands in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and its hoofbeats run through the threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the horse changed the speed at which humans could move armies, messages and goods. A claim-line that once held for weeks of travel could be crossed in days, and steppe societies that lived on horseback were able to reach agricultural states that were far away. Think of distance as a kind of wall: the horse did not tear it down, but it made it lower, and whoever stood behind it now stood closer. The Plains peoples' adoption of horses within roughly a century after 1680 shows how quickly one animal can rebuild a way of life, a whole architecture of daily practice. And the sanctioned harm carried out on horseback across many centuries is recorded here as suffering, not as glory. Speed serves whatever it carries. The reading would weaken if genetic work shows that the horse's spread owed less to riding communities than to trade in animals alone.
--- question 1
Were the Botai people true domesticators of horses, and what became of their herds and of their tradition?
--- question 2
What turned between about 2200 and 2000 BCE that made horses so valuable in war, and so swiftly?

=== species/last-universal-common-ancestor
title: Last Universal Common Ancestor
src: ca35e2e11205
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The last universal common ancestor, or LUCA, is the inferred population of cells from which all living Bacteria and Archaea, and so all life today, descend: the spring from which every living river still flows. It is reconstructed from the genes of living organisms, not from any fossil, with age estimates from about 3.5 to 4.3 billion years ago.
--- fact 1
LUCA is not a named organism or a fossil. It is the last population ancestral to every cell alive today; other lineages lived beside it, lamps that burned and then went dark, leaving no descendants.
--- fact 2
A 2024 study dated LUCA to about 4.2 billion years ago (about 4.09 to 4.33, depending on the clock model), using gene duplications that predate LUCA. Older estimates gave 3.5 to 3.8 billion years, and some researchers doubt any LUCA older than 4 billion years, a dawn they will not grant.
--- fact 3
The same study inferred an anaerobic cell, one that lived without oxygen, with about 2,650 proteins. A 2016 study with stricter criteria traced only 355 protein families to LUCA and pictured a heat-loving cell of hydrothermal vents, cradled where the deep earth breathes heat into water.
--- fact 4
The oldest widely accepted traces of life are stromatolites in Western Australia, about 3.43 to 3.48 billion years old, the first faint handwriting of the living. Older claims, such as graphite in a 4.10-billion-year-old zircon from Jack Hills, are disputed.
--- fact 5
Where the root of the tree of life lies is still unresolved, and it shifts every reconstruction, as a moved keystone shifts an arch.
--- context
LUCA stands near the threshold between the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] and the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]], a hinge between two ages of the young world. It comes after the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]] and before the great parting into Bacteria, among them the [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]], and [[species/archaea|Archaea]], the lineage behind the [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]]. Its witnesses include [[species/stromatolites|Stromatolites]] and the [[place/jack-hills-zircons|Jack Hills Zircons]]. Its story runs on through [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that every cell of the species, like every cell of every living thing, descends from a population known only by inference, whose age is redrawn with each new method. Sit with that for a moment: your whole lineage, and the oak's, and the mold's, converges on a stranger you will never see, whose birth date keeps moving. The Observer treats LUCA as a precondition, one lineage among many that happened to leave descendants, and reads no direction into that survival. That is the discipline here: to honor the ancestor without turning its luck into a destiny. Inheritance is not a plan. The reading would weaken if early gene exchange proved so extensive that no single ancestral population can be defined.
--- question 1
When did LUCA live, and was it one population, or a whole community of cells trading genes as travelers trade tales?
--- question 2
Where, in the deep dark of time, does the root of the tree of life lie?
