=== person/ashurbanipal
title: Ashurbanipal
src: 76c27ecadf78
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ashurbanipal (about 685 to about 631 BCE) held the throne of the Neo-Assyrian Empire from 669 BCE onward. His legacy rests on conquest, on the royal library he built at Nineveh, and on palace reliefs that set war and lion hunts side by side as proof of sovereignty.
--- fact 1
Born about 685 BCE as a son of Esarhaddon, Ashurbanipal came to the Assyrian throne in 669 BCE, while his brother Shamash-shum-ukin took the throne of Babylon.
--- fact 2
In 663 BCE, Assyrian forces sacked Thebes in Egypt, ending Kushite rule there.
--- fact 3
Shamash-shum-ukin rose in rebellion in 652 BCE; a long war followed, and Ashurbanipal finally took Babylon in 648 BCE. Campaigns of about 647 to 646 BCE broke Elam, and the campaign of about 646 BCE destroyed Susa.
--- fact 4
His libraries at Nineveh, uncovered in the 19th century, held tens of thousands of clay tablets and fragments, among them a version of the Epic of Gilgamesh. He boasted of his own training as a scribe.
--- fact 5
His own inscriptions and reliefs record harsh treatment of defeated enemies: mass deportation, mutilation, execution.
--- fact 6
He died about 631 BCE, though some place his death about 627. Nineveh itself fell to Babylonians and Medes in 612 BCE.
--- context
Ashurbanipal ruled the [[culture/neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]] during the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]]. His library carried forward a tradition of writing reaching back to the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]]; see [[event/invention-of-writing|Invention of Writing]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ashurbanipal built an architecture of memory for an entire civilization inside the very palace where he recorded the punishment of those who defied him. The library shows memory-outsourcing used as an instrument of rule: gathering omen and ritual texts was a way of governing knowledge alongside territory. This reading would weaken if the library proved to be chiefly the work of earlier kings and scribes, leaving his own part smaller than his boasts suggest.
--- question 1
How much of the library's collecting reflects his own initiative, and how much the labor of his scribes?

=== person/attila
title: Attila
src: 0cd2d5508567
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Attila (about 406 to 453) ruled the Huns from 434, first jointly with his brother Bleda and, from about 445, alone. He led Hunnic armies against both the Eastern and Western Roman empires, yet almost every written source about him was composed by Greeks and Romans.
--- fact 1
Attila's birth year remains uncertain, with estimates ranging from the 390s to the early 400s. He became joint ruler in 434, and sole ruler after Bleda's death, probably about 445.
--- fact 2
After the Treaty of Margus in 435, the Eastern Roman Empire doubled its yearly gold tribute to him, and after 443 that tribute is reported to have reached 2,100 Roman pounds.
--- fact 3
In 451 he invaded Gaul and met a combined Roman and Visigothic army at the Catalaunian Plains; in 452 he invaded northern Italy. Both times, he withdrew.
--- fact 4
He died in 453, after his wedding to Ildico. Priscus, as reported by Jordanes, describes a severe bleed. A later claim that his wife killed him is rejected by most historians, and his empire broke apart within a few years.
--- fact 5
Priscus, an envoy who visited his court in 449, left the only known eyewitness account of it. Attila's capital and his tomb have never been found.
--- context
Attila's power was rooted on the middle Danube in [[region/europe|Europe]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], and his campaigns added weight to the pressures behind the [[event/fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|Fall of the Western Roman Empire]]. See [[culture/romans|Romans]], [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Huns left no writing of their own, so a ruler remembered as one of history's great enemies survives only through the testimony of his opponents. The gold paid to him ran the empire's exchange-web in reverse: payment offered in return for peace rather than for goods. A specialist could argue that Roman diplomacy and a Germanic revolt, more than any single man's grip, broke the Hunnic polity, a reading that would weaken any portrait of empire built on one personality.
--- question 1
How far can the accounts of his court be trusted, given that the main eyewitness was an envoy of the empire he was pressing?
--- question 2
Where were his capital and his grave?

=== person/augustus
title: Augustus
src: f3ea721fd09a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Augustus (63 BCE to 14 CE) became Rome's first emperor. He closed out a century of civil war and raised the imperial architecture that would stand for centuries.
--- fact 1
Born Gaius Octavius, he stood as the adopted heir of Julius Caesar.
--- fact 2
Having defeated his rivals, he took the name Augustus in 27 BCE, all while preserving the republic's old forms.
--- fact 3
His reign opened the Pax Romana, a long stretch of relative stability.
--- fact 4
He reorganized the army, the provinces and taxation alike.
--- context
See [[culture/romans|Romans]] and [[place/rome|Rome]]. Augustus shaped the political architecture of the empire that would dominate the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a deft piece of story-glue at work: a monarch who insisted he was merely 'first citizen', so that the old republican stories could go on serving a new order.
--- question 1
How deliberate was this pretense of restoring the republic?

=== person/ba-trieu
title: Ba Trieu
type: person
date: c. 248 CE
themes: war, kinship
src: 101d79bb4069
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ba Trieu (Trieu Thi Trinh; her birth year unknown, she died in 248 CE) led an uprising in 248 CE against the Chinese Eastern Wu administration then governing northern Vietnam, and became a lasting symbol of Vietnamese resistance to foreign rule.
--- fact 1
She is said to have raised a rebel force in Cuu Chan, in what is now Thanh Hoa province, at about age twenty, together with her brother Trieu Quoc Dat.
--- fact 2
Later chronicles picture her riding into battle on an elephant, clad in gold-decorated armor, though these vivid details come from accounts written many centuries after her death rather than from any contemporary record.
--- fact 3
Eastern Wu sent the general Lu Yin to put down the revolt; after several months of fighting, her forces were defeated later in 248.
--- fact 4
Vietnamese tradition holds that she fled to Bo Dien commune and took her own life rather than be captured, her burial place attributed to Mount Tung in Hau Loc District, while Chinese-source summaries instead have Lu Yin putting her to death; the sources give no age at her death. A temple to her still stands near the site.
--- context
Ba Trieu's revolt unfolded in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]], against the Chinese administration that had governed the region since the Han conquest. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ba Trieu is known largely through Vietnamese chronicles written centuries after her death, in periods when a symbol of resistance to Chinese rule served a clear political purpose. The core event, a suppressed revolt in 248, most likely happened; the vivid personal details are harder to separate from later invention. The earliest and most detailed record, the Jiaozhou ji of the Western Jin period (265 to 318), dates only decades after 248, though Taylor held that Chinese records made no mention of her, and the elephant and gilded-tunic details first appear in later texts such as the Le-era Dai Viet su ky toan thu.
--- question 1
How much of her story took shape through later Vietnamese nation-building rather than contemporary memory?

=== person/ban-zhao
title: Ban Zhao
src: ed6a0972eef3
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ban Zhao (about 45 to about 117 CE) was a Chinese historian and scholar of the Eastern Han dynasty. After her brother's death she helped complete the Book of Han, and she wrote Lessons for Women; at court she also served as tutor and adviser.
--- fact 1
Ban Zhao was born about 45 CE, though some sources say about 49, into a scholarly family. Her father Ban Biao and her brother Ban Gu wrote much of the Book of Han (Hanshu), a history of the Western Han dynasty.
--- fact 2
When Ban Gu died in prison in 92 CE, the history stood unfinished, and Ban Zhao completed it in part, supplying the missing section of the Eight Tables.
--- fact 3
At the imperial court she served as tutor to the empress and her ladies, and from about 106 CE she advised the regent, Empress Deng.
--- fact 4
She is believed to have written Lessons for Women (Nüjie), in seven chapters, about 106 CE, though that date is not firmly established. It argues that girls should be educated and lays out rules of conduct for wives.
--- fact 5
Later writers made Lessons for Women a standard text for teaching girls obedience, and modern scholars still disagree on whether it reinforced or challenged the limits placed on women.
--- fact 6
She died about 117 CE, though some sources say about 120.
--- context
Ban Zhao worked under the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] in [[region/east-asia|East Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I find one woman behind two legacies: the historian who helped complete a dynasty's standard record, and the author of the text most used to teach women obedience. I cannot resolve her into a single direction. Later readers copied Lessons for Women because they valued its counsel, so what survives may carry their wanting as much as her meaning. I would trust this less if the text turned out to be heavily rewritten after she died. Until then, I hold her two legacies together, and let neither go.
--- question 1
Did she mean Lessons for Women as a defense of women's education, as a guide to conduct, or as both?
