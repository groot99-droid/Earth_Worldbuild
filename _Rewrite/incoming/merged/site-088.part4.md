=== technology/mrna-vaccines
src: 45760b29e805
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Messenger RNA, mRNA, vaccines instruct a recipient's own cells to briefly produce a piece of a pathogen, training the immune system to recognize it. The first authorized mRNA vaccines, against COVID-19, relied on a modification technique Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman discovered in 2005, and were authorized for emergency use in December 2020.
--- fact 1
Karikó and Weissman found in 2005 that replacing a standard building block of synthetic mRNA with a modified version prevented the immune system from destroying the mRNA as a threat before it could be used, solving a problem that had stalled mRNA vaccine research for years; their work was rejected by several journals and drew little funding at the time.
--- fact 2
When the SARS-CoV-2 virus emerged in late 2019, companies including Pfizer with BioNTech, and Moderna, used this modified-mRNA platform to design vaccines within days of the virus's genetic sequence being published in January 2020.
--- fact 3
The United Kingdom authorized the Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine on 2 December 2020, and the United States Food and Drug Administration issued an emergency use authorization on 11 December 2020, among the fastest vaccine development timelines in history.
--- fact 4
Unlike traditional vaccines using weakened or inactivated pathogens, mRNA vaccines can be redesigned quickly for new viral variants because only the genetic sequence, not a live pathogen, needs to be reformulated.
--- fact 5
Karikó and Weissman received the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the modified-nucleoside discovery that made effective mRNA vaccines possible.
--- context
mRNA vaccines were the primary tool used against the [[event/covid-19-pandemic|COVID-19 Pandemic]], extending the older technology of [[technology/vaccination|Vaccination]] with a platform as adaptable, in its way, as [[technology/crispr-gene-editing|CRISPR Gene Editing]]. mRNA Vaccines sits in the [[era/information-age|Information Age]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
You can see a fifteen-year gap between a discovery repeatedly rejected by funders and journals and its emergency deployment to hundreds of millions of people as a caution about how slowly a system built around scientific peer review can recognize an idea's eventual value. That a global pandemic could compress a vaccine development timeline that traditionally spans a decade or more into under a year suggests that institutional caution, not only scientific difficulty, ordinarily governs the pace of medical progress. This reading would weaken if evidence shows the 2020 timeline drew heavily on shortcuts in safety testing that longer-term data later called into question.
--- question 1
How much of the 2005 discovery's initial rejection reflected genuine scientific doubt, as against underfunding of unfashionable research directions?
--- question 2
Will the mRNA platform's adaptability extend successfully to vaccines against diseases beyond respiratory viruses, such as cancer or HIV?

=== technology/nuclear-weapons
src: a38f1da87875
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Nuclear weapons release explosive energy from nuclear fission or fusion reactions. The United States detonated the first device, code-named Trinity, in the New Mexico desert on 16 July 1945, and used two fission bombs against Japan weeks later, opening what this record calls the Nuclear Age.
--- fact 1
The Manhattan Project, a US-led effort with British and Canadian participation, produced the first nuclear explosive device, tested at the Trinity site near Alamogordo, New Mexico, on 16 July 1945.
--- fact 2
The United States dropped fission bombs on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, killing well over 100,000 people by the end of that year from blast, burns and radiation, with the total toll, including later radiation deaths, disputed and likely higher.
--- fact 3
The Soviet Union tested its own fission device in 1949, beginning a nuclear arms race that expanded to include thermonuclear, fusion-boosted, weapons from the early 1950s.
--- fact 4
By the end of the Cold War, nine states had developed or acquired nuclear weapons, and global stockpiles peaked at tens of thousands of warheads before arms-control treaties reduced them.
--- fact 5
No nuclear weapon has been used in combat since 1945, a record maintained through deterrence policy, treaties and, some historians argue, considerable luck during several Cold War crises.
--- context
Nuclear weapons apply the physics of [[technology/nuclear-fission|Nuclear Fission]] to warfare and opened the era this record calls the [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]], first deployed at the close of [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. Nuclear Weapons sits in the [[era/information-age|Information Age]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer records the deaths at Hiroshima and Nagasaki as suffering on a scale that changed how harm itself is discussed, since these weapons let a small number of aircraft do what once required sustained campaigns. The absence of nuclear use in the eight decades since 1945, despite dozens of tense confrontations, itself deserves notice as significant, whether it reflects sound restraint, effective deterrence, or accumulated chance. This reading would weaken if newly declassified records show closer calls, or fewer, than currently understood.
--- question 1
What is the best current estimate of total deaths attributable to the 1945 bombings, including long-term radiation effects?
--- question 2
How much of the post-1945 absence of nuclear use should be credited to deterrence policy as against chance?
