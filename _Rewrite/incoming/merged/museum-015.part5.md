--- description
Hilma af Klint (26 October 1862 to 21 October 1944) was a Swedish painter who, from 1906, created hundreds of abstract paintings guided by her spiritual beliefs, several years before Kandinsky, Mondrian and Malevich. She kept them largely hidden, and they reached an international audience only from the 1980s. Being first and being known are two different ledgers.

She was born at Karlberg Palace in Solna, near Stockholm, studied at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Stockholm from 1882 to 1887, and made a living painting landscapes, portraits and botanical illustrations. The careful work paid the bills; the daring work waited.

Her spiritual investigations, which sources date from 1879, developed around the time of the death of her younger sister Hermina in 1880 and drew her toward spiritualism and Theosophy. With four other women, who began as members of the Edelweiss Society, she made up "The Five," which held séances and experimented with automatic drawing, which the sources date to 1896.

In 1906, believing she had received a commission from a spiritual guide, she began the Paintings for the Temple, a cycle of nearly 200 works completed in 1915. She considered herself a medium through whom the works were created.

In 1908 she showed her work to Rudolf Steiner, and his cool response contributed to a four-year break in the series. She later spent long periods at the Goetheanum, the Anthroposophical Society's headquarters, between 1921 and 1930, and filled more than 125 notebooks, with sources giving figures from 125 to more than 150. One cool reply can stall years of work; notice that, and be careful with your own replies.

She believed the world was not ready for the paintings and directed that they not be exhibited until 20 years after her death. She died after a traffic accident, leaving more than 1,200 paintings and drawings to her nephew.
