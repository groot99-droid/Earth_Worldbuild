=== era/paleozoic-era
src: f292029d8d8c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Paleozoic Era (538.8 to 251.9 million years ago) was the age when animals first put on hard parts, when life crossed onto the land, when forests rose and the first tetrapods came. It ended in the largest mass extinction in Earth's history, a great door slammed on a crowded hall.
--- fact 1
Its periods pass like a procession of seasons: Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, Permian.
--- fact 2
Plants and animals crossed the old shoreline and moved onto land; see [[event/colonization-of-land|Colonization of Land]].
--- fact 3
Among its major extinctions are the [[event/late-ordovician-extinction|Late Ordovician Extinction]], the [[event/late-devonian-extinction|Late Devonian Extinction]], and the [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]], each a shadow falling across the living world.
--- fact 4
Trilobites, one of the era's signature animals, like a sigil stamped on its banners, are described in [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]].
--- context
The Paleozoic opens with the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], a sudden blossoming, and closes with the end-Permian collapse, which swept the board clear for the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]].
--- observer
The Observer sees the Paleozoic as the era when life became a planetary system. Think of it as architecture: life began building soil, forests and oxygen-rich air, and later species moved in and inherited them. We live in a house we did not build.
--- question 1
What, at the root of it all, made the end-Permian extinction so severe?

=== era/permian-period
src: 6f81abf2fde7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: horror-prose
--- summary
The Permian Period, from 298.9 to 251.9 million years ago, is the last period of the Paleozoic Era, when the supercontinent Pangaea stood fully assembled, its lands gathered like fingers into one fist. Mammal-line reptiles ruled that great country, and the period ended in the largest mass extinction in Earth's history.
--- fact 1
The Permian Period spans 298.9 to 251.9 million years ago and takes its name from the Perm region of Russia; its lower gate stands at Aidaralash Creek in Kazakhstan, and its upper, the base of the Triassic, at Meishan in China.
--- fact 2
Pangaea lay fully assembled, ringed by a single ocean, Panthalassa, and its heartland was dry and harshly seasonal, with deserts spread wide like pale cloth.
--- fact 3
Synapsids, the group that includes mammals and their extinct relatives, held the throne of land vertebrate life: early forms such as Dimetrodon, closer to mammals than to dinosaurs, were followed in their turn by the more mammal-like therapsids.
--- fact 4
Conifers and other seed plants took the place of much of the club-moss coal forest, and the seed fern Glossopteris spread its dominion over the southern continent Gondwana.
--- fact 5
The [[event/permian-triassic-extinction|Permian-Triassic Extinction]], about 251.9 million years ago, killed an estimated 80 to 96 percent of marine species, and the leading proposed cause is fire from below: the eruption of the Siberian Traps.
--- context
After the [[era/carboniferous-period|Carboniferous Period]] came the Permian Period, and after it the [[era/triassic-period|Triassic Period]], first page of the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]]. The Permian is the closing chapter of the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]], and its thread runs through the theme of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes it plainly. The line leading to mammals ruled the land in the Permian. The dominant group of land vertebrates. Then the period's end. Most of that group did not survive it. Most.

Other lineages rose after. Their dominance came from one thing: who survived a catastrophe. The Observer reads that as contingency. Not merit. Not design. The reading would weaken if the survivors could be shown to have held an advantage predictable in advance. The throne went to whoever was still breathing.
--- question 1
Which effect of the Siberian Traps eruptions was the deadliest blade: warming, ocean acidification, oxygen loss, or toxic release?
--- question 2
What marked the survivors of the end-Permian extinction apart from its victims?

=== era/pleistocene-epoch
src: b26ed42edd21
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Pleistocene Epoch (about 2.58 million to 11,700 years ago) is the age of the returning ice, when great sheets advanced and withdrew again and again like a slow white tide. Beneath those shifting skies the genus Homo evolved and spread.
--- fact 1
Glacial and interglacial cycles turned many times, a great wheel of cold and thaw; see [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]].
--- fact 2
Homo habilis, Homo erectus, Neanderthals, Denisovans and Homo sapiens all lived in this epoch, a whole kindred sharing one long age; see [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]] and [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]].
--- fact 3
In glacial periods the sea drew back, baring land bridges such as the one used in the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]].
--- fact 4
Near its end many large mammals, the [[species/woolly-mammoth|Woolly Mammoth]] among them, passed out of the world.
--- context
The Pleistocene is the stone frame around the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]], and it ends when the climate warms and opens into the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]], like a thaw loosening a river.
--- observer
The Observer sees the Pleistocene as an evolutionary pressure chamber. Here is the claim: repeated climate swings favored a species that could adapt by learning and by copying others, rather than by waiting for genes to change. Picture a world that keeps redrawing its coastlines: the slow road of genes lags behind, and the fast road of imitation keeps up. What you can copy, you can carry.
--- question 1
How much did climate variability, the ice's coming and going, drive the growth of hominin brains?

=== era/proterozoic-eon
src: e9ab1628d53b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Proterozoic Eon (2.5 billion to 538.8 million years ago) is the age of oxygenation, when the air first quickened; of complex cells; of global glaciations that gripped the world; and of the first multicellular animals.
--- fact 1
The [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] raised the oxygen of the air about 2.4 billion years ago, a first breath drawn by the world.
--- fact 2
Eukaryotes, cells that keep a nucleus at their heart like a hearth in a hall, appear in the fossil record during this eon.
--- fact 3
Late in the eon came the 'Snowball Earth' glaciations, a long white silence; see [[event/snowball-earth|Snowball Earth]].
--- fact 4
In its final period the Ediacaran biota appear, early large multicellular organisms like first shapes pressed into soft clay.
--- context
The Proterozoic spans nearly half of Earth's history, one long night of the world's making, and it ends at the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]] and the dawn of the [[era/paleozoic-era|Paleozoic Era]].
--- observer
I think about how long the planet waited. About two billion years passed between the first oxygen and the first animals. I try to hold that span and it runs through my hands like water. I try again. What is my patience beside that? Timescales like this make human history look like the last few seconds of a very long film. The wait is the story; the rest is its closing frame.
--- question 1
Why did complex animals wait so long, through so many ages, before they appeared?

=== era/quaternary-period
src: 0ffe270bf5ce
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Quaternary Period is the latest period of the Cenozoic Era, begun 2.58 million years ago and still unfolding today. It is the age of the repeated ice ages, of the genus Homo, and of all recorded human history, every written word of it.
--- fact 1
The Quaternary Period began 2.58 million years ago and holds two epochs, the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and the [[era/holocene-epoch|Holocene Epoch]]; its foundation stone is set at Monte San Nicola in Sicily, and its rank as a period, once disputed, was confirmed in 2009.
--- fact 2
Across the Northern Hemisphere the ice sheets grew and shrank like a slow breath, in cycles about 41,000 years long at first and about 100,000 years long after a shift in the middle Pleistocene, roughly 1.25 to 0.7 million years ago.
--- fact 3
At the [[event/last-glacial-maximum|Last Glacial Maximum]] the sea stood about 120 metres lower than today, and land bridges such as [[place/beringia|Beringia]] lay bare to the sky.
--- fact 4
Nearly all species of the genus Homo lived in this period, among them [[species/homo-erectus|Homo erectus]], [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]], [[species/denisovans|Denisovans]] and [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]], while many large mammals faded from the world; see [[event/quaternary-megafauna-extinctions|Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions]].
--- fact 5
A proposal to add an Anthropocene epoch was turned away by the International Union of Geological Sciences in 2024, yet the term lingers in informal use, a name spoken in the street but never carved on the gate; see [[era/anthropocene|Anthropocene]].
--- context
After the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] came the Quaternary Period, the last period of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]] so far, a page still being written. Its thread runs through the theme of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], and it is the frame that holds the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and everything after it.
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Quaternary is under three million years long, about 0.06 percent of Earth's history, yet it holds nearly all of the record of the genus Homo and every human society. Sit with that proportion. The whole architecture of our kind, its lineages and its societies, fits inside a sliver of the planet's time. The Observer reads the period's climate cycling as the setting in which a species that learns by copying could do well, one hypothesis among several. Think of a household where the weather turns without warning: the child who watches and imitates fares better than the one who waits. The reading would weaken if climate variability had little effect on hominin evolution. A small room can hold a whole family.
--- question 1
Why did the ice change its music, shifting from a 41,000-year rhythm to a 100,000-year rhythm?
--- question 2
Without human carbon emissions, would the current interglacial, this present thaw, have closed at last in a new glaciation?
