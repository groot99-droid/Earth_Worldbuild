=== place/machu-picchu
src: c9ecfb39c879
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Machu Picchu is a citadel of stone raised about 1450 among the Peruvian Andes, where the ridges break like frozen surf, and it was probably a royal estate for the Inca ruler Pachacuti.
--- fact 1
It keeps its seat among the clouds, about 2,430 meters above sea level.
--- fact 2
Its halls fell quiet around the time of the Spanish conquest, abandoned as a nest is left when the season turns.
--- fact 3
Local people knew of it, as a valley knows its own river; Hiram Bingham carried it to international attention in 1911.
--- fact 4
In 1983 it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, its name set down in the long roll of the world's kept places.
--- context
The citadel belongs to the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]], a stone jewel set in the crown of the [[region/andes|Andes]].
--- observer
I notice the terraces. I notice the drainage beneath them. I notice how a ruler's retreat was built at the edge of what is buildable, as if the stone were asked how far it would go. What is a retreat, if not a question put to the edge? We build our refuges where the ground almost says no.
--- question 1
What, exactly, was the citadel for, up there among the peaks?

=== place/mohenjo-daro
src: 03967ae6f6f2
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Mohenjo-daro was a great city of the Indus Valley Civilization in present-day Pakistan, a lattice of brick where people dwelt from about 2500 to 1900 BCE.
--- fact 1
Its streets were laid in a grid, true as a weaver's warp, with brick-lined drains and wells.
--- fact 2
At the heart of its citadel lies the 'Great Bath', a large water tank cupped in brick like water in a palm.
--- fact 3
Its lanes may have held tens of thousands of people, a hive of lives.
--- fact 4
In 1980 it was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and the old bricks were given a new kind of keeping.
--- context
Mohenjo-daro is a keystone of the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], and it shows that [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]] kindled in more than one region, as fire catches on separate hills.
--- observer
The Observer notes a city with sophisticated sanitation and few weapons or grand tombs. Think of what you expect to find in an ancient city: the sword, the gilded grave. Here the architecture points toward drains and water instead. It suggests other ways of organizing a city than the ones we know best from Mesopotamia and Egypt. Absence, too, is evidence.
--- question 1
How was this city of brick and water governed?

=== place/marib
src: 2a181001f595
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Marib is a city in Yemen at the edge of the Ramlat al-Sabatayn desert, east of Sanaa, where sand meets sown ground. From about the 8th century BCE it was the capital of the Kingdom of Saba, and a great dam there once drank the floods and gave them to the oasis.
--- fact 1
Marib's name is cut into Sabaean inscriptions from about the 8th century BCE; it was the capital of the Kingdom of Saba, a realm whose wealth rose on the smoke of the incense trade.
--- fact 2
The Great Dam of Marib, first built in about the 8th century BCE, turned aside the seasonal floods of Wadi Adhanah and sent them down irrigation canals like veins of silver; it was raised and repaired over many centuries, and an inscription records a repair in 542 to 543 CE.
--- fact 3
Around the 6th century CE the dam failed for the last time; the Quran speaks of a flood that ended the prosperity of Saba, and tradition binds that flood to the broken dam.
--- fact 4
Temples of Almaqah, the chief god of Saba, stand at Marib, among them the oval Awwam temple, which local tradition names Mahram Bilqis.
--- fact 5
In the 1980s a modern dam rose at Marib, built with funding from the United Arab Emirates, and it took the place of the ancient system.
--- fact 6
Since 2015 Marib has stood on the front line of the Yemeni civil war, and it has taken in large numbers of people driven from their homes by the fighting.
--- context
Marib was the capital of the [[culture/kingdom-of-saba|Kingdom of Saba]] and a knot in the long cord of the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]]; the failure of its dam lives on in traditions that flowed into the sources of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]]. It stands in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and it is woven into the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I notice that Marib was built on water as much as on incense. I notice the dam that turned flood into farmland, and I know it needed labor, tribute and coordination. I notice the inscribed repairs, and they tell me that upkeep was a political act, made again and again.

When order failed, so did the dam, and that suggests to me that infrastructure and authority rise and fall together. And the later story that gave the dam's failure a moral cause? I hold it as story-glue, the kind we make about the fall of a prosperous society.

Historians debate whether the collapse caused the decline or reflected a decline already under way, and I would read this less boldly if the dam's failures prove mainly hydrological. What holds the water holds the people.
--- question 1
When was the first dam raised, and how far did its watered fields reach?
--- question 2
Did the breaking of the dam bring Marib's decline, or only follow it?
