=== person/ambiorix
title: Ambiorix
type: person
date: c. 60 – 53 BCE
themes: war
src: 4fac8d09960e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Ambiorix (active 54 to 53 BCE) was co-ruler of the Eburones, a Belgic tribe in what is now eastern Belgium, remembered for destroying a Roman legion during a revolt against Julius Caesar's occupation of Gaul.
--- fact 1
He shared kingship of the Eburones with an older co-ruler, Cativolcus, an arrangement Caesar's own account describes but never fully explains.
--- fact 2
In the winter of 54 BCE he lured a Roman force under Quintus Titurius Sabinus and Lucius Aurunculeius Cotta out of its fortified camp with false promises of safe passage, then ambushed and destroyed it, killing both commanders.
--- fact 3
Caesar answered with a campaign of deliberate devastation against the Eburones, intended to eliminate the tribe entirely, but Ambiorix himself escaped capture and vanishes from the historical record after 53 BCE.
--- fact 4
Nearly everything known about him comes from Caesar's own Commentarii de Bello Gallico, written to justify Caesar's conduct of the war to a Roman audience.
--- context
Ambiorix led resistance against the [[culture/romans|Romans]] in [[region/europe|Europe]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Ambiorix survives in history almost entirely through the writing of the general whose legion he destroyed, an author with every incentive to cast the ambush as treacherous deceit rather than a defensible tactic of war waged by an occupied people. Ambiorix's own reasoning, and any account beyond Caesar's, are lost.
--- question 1
What became of Ambiorix after 53 BCE, and did he survive Caesar's campaign of retaliation against his people?

=== person/amir-khusrau
title: Amir Khusrau
type: person
date: 1253 – 1325
themes: art, language, religion
src: 6535da21c322
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Amir Khusrau (1253 to 1325) was the leading court poet of the Delhi Sultanate, writing in Persian while drawing on the Indian world around him. He is regarded as the founding figure of Indo-Persian literature, and is traditionally credited with founding qawwali, the devotional music of South Asian Sufi shrines.
--- fact 1
Khusrau was born in 1253 near Delhi, to a Turkic father who had migrated from Central Asia and an Indian mother, and he referred to this mixed heritage throughout his work.
--- fact 2
He served as court poet under a rapid succession of Delhi sultans over more than four decades, through palace coups and dynastic changes.
--- fact 3
He wrote in Persian, the language of the Indo-Islamic courts; his five long narrative poems, dated about 1298 to 1302, answer the Khamsa of the Persian poet Nizami Ganjavi, retelling Shirin and Khusrau, Layla and Majnun, and the legend of Alexander the Great.
--- fact 4
He was a devoted follower of the Sufi mystic Nizamuddin Auliya, and legend says Khusrau died within months of his master, in 1325; their shrines stand together in Delhi.
--- fact 5
He is traditionally credited with founding qawwali, still performed at Sufi shrines across India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, and later legend also credits him with inventing the sitar and tabla, without firm historical evidence.
--- context
Khusrau lived within the [[culture/delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I see a poet who outlived several dynasties by making himself useful to each one, carrying a mixed parentage he never hid but stated plainly, as fact. Persian was the language of the courts he served; the India around him supplied the material. What outlasted every sultan was the devotional music, which needed no palace — only a shrine, and singers to carry it. The instruments and forms credited to him rest on legend, so the picture I am given of him as founder is partly the work of admirers who came after.
--- question 1
Which of the musical forms and instruments credited to him did he actually introduce?
