=== culture/kingdom-of-himyar
src: 82442f897ca6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Kingdom of Himyar, based in what is now Yemen, became a major regional power in the southern Arabian Peninsula and, around 380 CE, saw its ruling elite convert to a form of Judaism, before falling to Christian Ethiopian invaders around 525 CE.
--- fact 1
Himyar emerged as a kingdom around the 2nd century CE and had grown into a major regional power by the 4th century, eventually conquering the older neighboring kingdom of Saba, biblical Sheba.
--- fact 2
Most historians now agree that around 380 CE the Himyarite ruling elite converted to a form of Judaism, though scholars disagree over which king, Malkikarib Yuha'min according to Byzantine and contemporary sources, or his son Abu Karib according to later Islamic sources, actually initiated the conversion.
--- fact 3
Adopting Judaism may have offered the Himyarite rulers a religious identity distinct from, and a source of political independence against, the neighboring Christian Byzantine and Ethiopian powers as well as the Zoroastrian Sasanian Empire, all of which had reasons to seek influence over southern Arabia.
--- fact 4
Religious tension between Himyar's Jewish elite and Christian communities within its territory, including a documented persecution of Christians at Najran, provided the pretext for a Christian Ethiopian invasion from the Kingdom of Aksum.
--- fact 5
Around 525 CE, Aksumite forces conquered Himyar, ending Jewish royal rule in the region and installing Christian governance, a conquest that permanently altered the religious landscape of pre-Islamic southern Arabia in the century before the rise of Islam.
--- context
Himyar's royal conversion to [[culture/judaism|Judaism]] made it a rare pre-Islamic Arabian state whose religious identity marked a claim against the neighboring Christian [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] and the Zoroastrian world represented by [[culture/zoroastrianism|Zoroastrianism]]'s state adoption elsewhere, before Aksumite conquest ended Himyar's independence. Kingdom of Himyar sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Himyar's elite appear to have chosen a faith, Judaism, less for private conviction alone than as a deliberate signal of independence from every major neighboring power's religion at once, a claim drawn through belief rather than through territory or armies. That this same religious choice, meant to secure independence, instead gave a rival power the pretext it used to justify conquest shows you how a state's chosen faith can become the very vulnerability an enemy exploits. A specialist could argue that Himyar's fall owed more to conventional strategic and economic rivalry over Red Sea trade routes than to religious tension, which would weaken a reading centered on the persecution at Najran as the conquest's true cause.
--- question 1
How widely did Judaism spread beyond the Himyarite royal court and elite into the wider population?
--- question 2
How much did control over Red Sea trade routes, as against religious conflict, actually drive the Aksumite invasion of 525 CE?

=== culture/kingdom-of-israel-and-judah
src: ea279a7d2a83
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Kingdom of Israel and Judah names the polity or polities the Hebrew Bible describes as a united monarchy under Saul, David and Solomon before it split into a northern Kingdom of Israel and a southern Kingdom of Judah, a narrative whose earliest chapters remain genuinely contested among archaeologists and historians.
--- fact 1
According to the Hebrew Bible, a united monarchy over Israel and Judah existed as early as the 11th century BCE under Saul, David and Solomon, with the kingdom dividing into separate northern and southern states following Solomon's death, traditionally dated to around 930 BCE.
--- fact 2
No archaeological evidence for the united monarchy commands scholarly consensus, and historians disagree sharply on how much of the biblical account reflects a genuinely powerful, centralized early kingdom versus a smaller chiefdom later magnified by later religious and national tradition.
--- fact 3
Some archaeologists point to monumental architecture at sites including Megiddo and Gezer as possible evidence for a strong united monarchy, while others argue these structures cannot be confidently attributed to Solomon's reign and may date from significantly later periods.
--- fact 4
Independent of the united monarchy debate, historians and archaeologists broadly agree that the separate Kingdom of Israel in the north existed by about 900 BCE and the Kingdom of Judah in the south by about 850 BCE, giving the divided kingdoms a firmer evidentiary basis than their claimed unified predecessor.
--- fact 5
The Neo-Assyrian Empire destroyed the northern Kingdom of Israel in 722 BCE, and the Neo-Babylonian Empire under Nebuchadnezzar II conquered Jerusalem and destroyed the southern Kingdom of Judah's First Temple in 586 BCE, deporting part of its population.
--- context
This kingdom's religious traditions form the historical root of [[culture/judaism|Judaism]], its capital and holiest city was [[place/jerusalem|Jerusalem]], and its southern kingdom's destruction came at the hands of [[place/babylon|Babylon]]'s Neo-Babylonian rulers. Kingdom of Israel and Judah sits in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that this kingdom's earliest chapters sit in an unusual position between evidence and belief: a richly detailed religious narrative describing a golden age of unified rule exists alongside an archaeological record too thin to confirm or clearly refute it, so this record treats the united monarchy as contested tradition rather than established history, while treating the later, better-attested divided kingdoms with somewhat greater confidence. That later religious and national tradition had strong reasons to remember a unified, powerful founding kingdom, regardless of the underlying historical reality, is itself a case of belief shaping how a society remembers its own claim to a homeland, independent of what archaeology alone could establish. A specialist could argue that continued excavation at sites like Megiddo and Gezer may yet resolve the united monarchy question more definitively in either direction, which would strengthen rather than weaken the case for treating the debate as genuinely still open.
--- question 1
Does the archaeological evidence at Megiddo, Gezer and other sites ultimately support or undermine the existence of a powerful united monarchy under David and Solomon?
--- question 2
How much of the biblical narrative about the united monarchy reflects later religious and national reconstruction rather than contemporary historical memory?
