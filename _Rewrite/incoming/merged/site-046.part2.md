=== person/basawan
title: Basawan
src: 868b008602ea
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Basawan was a Hindu painter in the imperial workshop of the Mughal emperor Akbar, active from about 1560 to about 1600, though he is described as flourishing between 1580 and 1600, and the earlier date rests only on his earliest known mention, in a Tutinama of about 1556 to 1561. His birth and death dates are unknown, so the dates given here simply mark his working years. Akbar's own historian ranked him among the best of the workshop's more than a hundred painters.
--- fact 1
Almost nothing is known of Basawan's life beyond his paintings and a brief passage of praise in Abu'l-Fazl's Ain-i-Akbari. He was a Hindu, and probably joined the imperial workshop as a young man in the 1560s. His earliest mention comes in an illustrated Tutinama of about 1556 to 1561, and he is described as flourishing between 1580 and 1600.
--- fact 2
Akbar reigned from 1556 to 1605. The workshop's Persian masters, Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad, had trained in the tradition of Behzād and directed Indian artists in producing vast illustrated manuscripts, among them the Hamzanama, 1,400 large paintings.
--- fact 3
Abu'l-Fazl praised his skill in composition, faces, colour and portraiture, and wrote that many critics preferred him even to the celebrated Daswanth.
--- fact 4
His name is written in the margins of paintings in the Razmnama (a Persian translation of the Mahabharata), the Darabnama, the Akbarnama and small books of Persian poetry made in Lahore in the 1590s. In large history manuscripts he often designed the composition while another artist added the colour.
--- fact 5
From 1580 Jesuit missionaries brought European engravings and paintings to Akbar's court. He studied them closely, adapting their shading and deep space, and was among the first Indian painters to absorb European modelling and perspective.
--- fact 6
He was active until about 1600. His son Manohar became a leading painter under Jahangir. His paintings are now held in the Victoria and Albert Museum, the British Library and the Chester Beatty Library.
--- context
Basawan worked in the [[culture/mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] in [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. His workshop's founders trained in the tradition of [[person/behzad|Behzad]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I look for him and find only a margin: a name written by librarians, one passage of praise, and a labour that was so often divided between designer and colourist that no single hand stays fixed on the page. What survives is a workshop that braided Persian masters, Hindu painters and European prints into a single style, a deliberate joining of tool-lineages by a ruler who wanted his history pictured. I hold any portrait of the man himself loosely, because it is built from attribution, not from his own words.
--- question 1
How can his own hand be separated from that of the colourists and finishers who worked on the same pages?

=== person/basil-ii
title: Basil II
src: c6412664dbc5
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Basil II (958 to 1025) held the Byzantine throne for nearly fifty years, the longest reign in Roman or Byzantine imperial history, and is remembered above all for his decades-long war against the First Bulgarian Empire.
--- fact 1
He became co-emperor as a child in 960, but did not gain full personal control of the government until about 989, by which time he was in his thirties, only after suppressing rebellions by powerful aristocratic generals including Bardas Skleros and Bardas Phokas.
--- fact 2
His long campaigns against Bulgaria culminated in the decisive Battle of Kleidion in 1014, after which Bulgaria was absorbed into the Byzantine Empire by 1018, earning him the later epithet "Bulgar-slayer" (Boulgaroktonos).
--- fact 3
A story recorded by later chroniclers claims he blinded thousands of Bulgarian prisoners after Kleidion, leaving one eye to each of every hundredth man so he could lead the rest home; modern historians debate how literally this account should be taken.
--- fact 4
He left the imperial treasury full and the empire at its greatest territorial extent since the 7th century, but died without an heir, and the strong central rule he had built did not survive his less capable successors.
--- context
Basil II ruled the [[culture/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] in the [[region/mediterranean-basin|Mediterranean Basin]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Basil II's reign is often read backward from the Byzantine Empire's later decline, framing him as the last strong emperor of a golden age. That framing rests partly on hindsight: the empire's later troubles grew out of succession and outside pressures that Basil could not have governed from beyond the grave, whatever the soundness of his own institutions.
--- question 1
How much of the Byzantine Empire's difficulties after 1025 stemmed from institutional weaknesses already present under Basil, rather than simply the absence of an equally capable successor?

=== person/behzad
title: Behzad
src: 4ce0fb1badd9
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Kamāl ud-Dīn Behzād (also spelled Bihzad, born about 1455 to 1460 and died about 1535) stands as the most celebrated painter in the Persian tradition. He led the royal manuscript workshops at Herat and later Tabriz, and many works are attributed to him on grounds of style alone.
--- fact 1
Behzād was probably born about 1455 to 1460 in Herat, capital of the Timurid dynasty founded by [[person/timur|Timur]]. Orphaned young, he was raised by the painter Mirak Naqqash, head of the royal library, and was protected by the poet and minister Mir Ali-Shir Nava'i.
--- fact 2
Under Sultan Husayn Bayqara, who ruled Herat from 1469 to 1506, he became the leading artist of the royal kitabkhana, the library workshop. His most famous surviving works date from this period, including paintings in a Bustan of Sa'di (1488) and a Khamsa of Nizami (1494 to 1495).
--- fact 3
After Herat fell to the Uzbeks in 1506 and then to the Safavid Shah Ismail I in about 1510, he moved to Tabriz, where in 1522 Ismail appointed him director of the royal library. He died about 1535.
--- fact 4
A legend says that during the Battle of Chaldiran in 1514 the shah hid Behzād in a cave, in some tellings together with a famous calligrapher, because he valued him more than his own army.
--- fact 5
His figures carry individual faces and gestures and are often shown busy with everyday tasks. He often hid his signature discreetly within the architecture of a painting.
--- fact 6
His followers Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad trained in his tradition and went on to found the Mughal imperial workshop under Humayun and Akbar.
--- fact 7
His name became a byword for excellence, which led to many false attributions. Scholars still debate which works are by his own hand.
--- context
Behzād worked first in the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]] and then in the [[culture/safavid-empire|Safavid Empire]], on the [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I think of a royal workshop, calligraphers and illuminators and painters and binders working side by side, and I call it what it is: an engine for outsourcing memory, the maker of the books through which a court remembered its poems and its histories. One name got fixed to that collective labour, and the name became a standard, and standards draw false claims the way light draws moths. So much of what is called his may belong to others, and for many pictures style is the only witness left. I hold the attribution loosely, the way I would hold something borrowed.
--- question 1
Which of the paintings attributed to Behzād are by his own hand rather than his workshop?

=== person/buster-keaton
title: Buster Keaton
src: 705e5e51fe72
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Buster Keaton (1895 to 1966) was an American silent-film comedian, writer and director known as the deadpan "Great Stone Face," who built his comedy on engineered physical stunts he performed himself. Ranked with Charlie Chaplin among silent comedy's defining stars, he made *The General* (1926), now considered among the greatest films ever made.
--- fact 1
Born in 1895 into a vaudeville family, Keaton performed from age three in the act The Three Keatons, taking falls that built his later physical control.
--- fact 2
He entered film in 1917 under Roscoe "Fatty" Arbuckle, and by 1920 was writing, directing and starring in his own comedies, moving into features with *Our Hospitality* (1923).
--- fact 3
*Sherlock Jr.* (1924) placed him inside a shifting film-dream using in-camera measurement rather than editing tricks, and is still studied for its daring.
--- fact 4
*The Navigator* (1924), about passengers adrift on an ocean liner, was his biggest commercial success of the decade.
--- fact 5
*The General* (1926), built around full-scale trains and a locomotive plunged off a burning bridge, flopped on release but is now ranked among cinema's greatest films.
--- fact 6
In *Steamboat Bill, Jr.* (1928) he performed one of the most dangerous shots ever filmed, standing exactly where a falling house facade's open window would clear him.
--- fact 7
A 1928 MGM contract, taken under financial pressure, cost him creative control; the next decade brought decline, divorce and alcoholism.
--- fact 8
Rediscovered by critics from the 1950s, he received an honorary Academy Award in 1959 and died of lung cancer in 1966.
--- fact 9
His influence runs through later stunt performers such as Jackie Chan, who has cited Keaton's real, visible danger as a model.
--- context
Keaton worked in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], building comedies from the era's trains, boats and machinery. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I watch a man trained from infancy to fall without breaking, and I see discipline where others see luck: a whole lineage of bodily skill behind each stunt, nothing improvised. I watch his blank face hold still inside engineered chaos, and I understand the stillness as a signal, drawing the eye precisely by refusing the reaction it expects. I remember that *The General* failed the year it opened and that a later generation built the reputation it now carries, and I take that as a species quietly rewriting what it once dismissed. His decline in the 1930s I read as plain record, not the reverent shape admirers now give the stunts' zero margin for error.
--- question 1
Why did *The General* fail at the box office in 1926 despite the technical ambition later critics would call unmatched?

=== person/caravaggio
title: Caravaggio
src: 79604282caca
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Michelangelo Merisi, called Caravaggio (1571 to 1610), was an Italian painter who transformed European painting around 1600 with harsh theatrical light and street-level realism. His turbulent life ended at 38, but his influence spread across Europe within a generation.
--- fact 1
Caravaggio was born in Milan in September 1571. His name comes from the nearby town his family came from. He trained in Milan with Simone Peterzano and arrived in Rome about 1592.
--- fact 2
Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte took him into his household. His first public commission, two large paintings of St Matthew for the Contarelli Chapel in San Luigi dei Francesi (1599 to 1600), caused a sensation. Several later altarpieces were rejected as indecorous.
--- fact 3
Police records list brawls, assaults and insults. In 1606 he killed a man, Ranuccio Tomassoni, and fled Rome under sentence of death. He then lived in Naples, Malta and Sicily.
--- fact 4
He died in July 1610 at Porto Ercole in Tuscany, on his way to Rome hoping for a papal pardon.
--- fact 5
He painted directly on the canvas, apparently without preparatory drawings, from live models posed under a single strong light. His backgrounds are dark and bare (tenebrism).
--- fact 6
His key works include Judith Beheading Holofernes (about 1599 to 1602, sources differ), The Calling of Saint Matthew (1599 to 1600) and Supper at Emmaus (1601).
--- fact 7
The "Caravaggisti" included Orazio and Artemisia Gentileschi, Jusepe de Ribera, Georges de La Tour and the Utrecht painters, and through them his influence reached [[person/diego-velazquez|Diego Velázquez]] and [[person/rembrandt-van-rijn|Rembrandt van Rijn]]. A 1951 exhibition in Milan fell within the twentieth-century revival of a reputation that had faded, though the sources do not name that exhibition as its cause.
--- context
Caravaggio worked in Rome, Naples, Malta and Sicily in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. His followers included [[person/artemisia-gentileschi|Artemisia Gentileschi]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I stand where a patron once stood and understand the offense: sacred events set in the streets of contemporary Rome, dirty feet and hired models, read by some as truth and by others as indecorum, a meaning-engine policing its own images in public. I know the violent life mostly from police records, and I feel how easily that record tempts me to read the man into every picture. I remember that his reputation faded and then returned across the twentieth century, a 1951 exhibition one landmark along that return, so what I feel standing before the paintings now is a recent judgment, not an old one.
--- question 1
How much of his working method, without drawings, can be recovered from surviving evidence?

=== person/catherine-de-medici
title: Catherine de' Medici
src: d111a351d421
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Catherine de' Medici (1519 to 1589) was queen of France and, as mother to three kings, one of the most powerful figures in France during the Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots.
--- fact 1
Born into the powerful Medici banking family of Florence, she married the future Henry II of France in 1533 and became queen in 1547.
--- fact 2
After Henry's death in 1559 she became the chief political influence over her sons Francis II, Charles IX and Henry III, though she was not formally regent under Francis II or Henry III, holding the regency only for Charles IX, as gouvernante de France from December 1560 until August 1563, through a period of escalating conflict between Catholics and Protestant Huguenots.
--- fact 3
She is widely held responsible, though the exact extent of her role is debated, for approving the assassination of Huguenot leaders, which began with the shooting of Admiral Coligny on 22 August 1572. He survived that attempt but was killed two days later as the massacre began. This triggered the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre in August 1572, in which about 2,000 to 3,000 Huguenots were killed in Paris, and more elsewhere.
--- fact 4
She pursued marriage alliances and diplomacy across Europe to secure the Valois dynasty's position, though her efforts did not prevent the eventual extinction of the Valois line with the death of her last son in 1589.
--- context
Catherine de' Medici ruled at the French court in [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and its Wars of Religion. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Catherine's later reputation as a scheming, sinister figure took shape largely through hostile Huguenot pamphlets written during her own lifetime and repeated ever after by later historians; separating a mother managing three weak sons' survival in a genuinely lethal court from a caricature of poison and plotting is not straightforward from the surviving record.
--- question 1
How much personal responsibility did Catherine bear for the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre, versus reacting to a crisis that spiraled beyond any single actor's control?
