=== people--basawan--1
src: 75722793d78a
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Basawan was a Hindu painter in the imperial workshop of the Mughal emperor Akbar, active from about 1560 to about 1600, though he is described as flourishing between 1580 and 1600, and the earlier date rests on his earliest mention, in a Tutinama of about 1556 to 1561. His birth and death dates are unknown, so the dates here mark his years of work. Akbar's historian ranked him among the best of the workshop's more than a hundred painters.

Almost nothing is known of his life beyond his paintings and a brief praise in Abu'l-Fazl's Ain-i-Akbari. He was a Hindu and probably entered the imperial workshop as a young man in the 1560s. His earliest mention is in an illustrated Tutinama of about 1556 to 1561, and he is described as flourishing between 1580 and 1600.

Akbar reigned from 1556 to 1605. The workshop's Persian masters, Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad, had trained in the tradition of Behzād and guided Indian artists in making vast illustrated manuscripts, the Hamzanama among them, 1,400 large paintings, a river of images.

Abu'l-Fazl praised his skill in composition, faces, colour and portraiture, and wrote that many critics preferred him even to the celebrated Daswanth.

His name stands in the margins of paintings in the Razmnama (a Persian translation of the Mahabharata), the Darabnama, the Akbarnama, and small books of Persian poetry made in Lahore in the 1590s. In large history manuscripts he often designed the composition while another artist laid on the colour.

From 1580 Jesuit missionaries brought European engravings and paintings to Akbar's court. He studied them closely, taking up their shading and deep space, and was among the first Indian painters to absorb European modelling and perspective, as one river takes in the waters of another.

=== people--queen-suriyothai--1
src: 1e5f68ef83fb
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Queen Suriyothai was a consort of King Maha Chakkraphat of the Ayutthaya kingdom, in present-day Thailand, traditionally said to have died in 1548 defending her husband from elephant-back during a Burmese invasion. Her birth year is traditionally given as 1511, and her story rests on Siamese chronicles alone.

She was a consort of Maha Chakkraphat, who became king of Ayutthaya in 1546 or 1548, as sources differ.

In 1548 the Burmese king Tabinshwehti invaded Ayutthaya. Siamese chronicles tell that Suriyothai, disguised and riding an elephant, set herself between her husband and an enemy commander and was killed by his blow.

No Burmese chronicle records this episode, and her historicity is questioned. Thai accounts add that her daughter, Princess Boromdilok, was also mortally wounded.

Maha Chakkraphat raised a memorial stupa, the Chedi Si Suriyothai, at Wat Suan Luang Sopsawan on the bank of the Chao Phraya near Ayutthaya, and it is traditionally associated with the place of her death, its spire standing over the river like a held breath.

Thai nationalist commemoration of her grew in the twentieth century, including a widely seen 2001 film.
