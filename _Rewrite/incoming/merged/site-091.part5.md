=== species/sugarcane
src: c1abdb3b19d6
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea and nearby parts of Southeast Asia around 6,000 years ago, spread slowly through Asia and the Mediterranean world over the following millennia, and after 1492 became the crop most closely tied to slave labor in the Americas. Its history is sweet only in name.
--- fact 1
Sugarcane was domesticated from the wild grass Saccharum robustum in New Guinea around 6,000 years ago; a related domestication of Saccharum sinense by Austronesian peoples in southern China and Taiwan happened by at least 5,500 years ago.
--- fact 2
Austronesian traders carried sugarcane to China and India by about 3,000 years ago, and Persian and Greek writers met it in the 6th to 4th centuries BCE; by the 8th century CE sugar was traded into the Mediterranean world as an expensive luxury spice from India.
--- fact 3
Christopher Columbus brought sugarcane to Hispaniola on his second voyage, and the first sugar harvest in the Americas came there in 1501; Portuguese colonizers took the crop to Brazil, where observers of the time counted about 60 sugar mills in 1570, 115 in 1583 and 192 in 1612.
--- fact 4
The Atlantic sugar trade formed one side of a triangular trade linking European manufactured goods, enslaved Africans, and New World sugar and other raw materials, and sugar plantations across the Caribbean and Brazil were worked overwhelmingly by enslaved people; in Louisiana, enslaved workers' labor made the state responsible for nearly a quarter of the world's exportable sugar by 1853.
--- fact 5
World sugarcane production reached about 1.92 billion tonnes in 2022, with Brazil growing about 38 percent and India about 23 percent.
--- context
Sugarcane's spread across the Atlantic world after 1492 is told in more detail under the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]], and it cannot be separated from the history of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]; its arrival on Hispaniola is one of the earliest transfers of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], within the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes the contrast between two speeds. Across Asia, sugarcane spread slowly, over thousands of years. In the Americas after 1492, within a few decades, an exchange-web moved the plant, an enslaved workforce and a market for the finished product across an ocean at once, and the Observer treats that compression as evidence of how quickly extractive systems can scale once the pieces are in place. Remember that speed is not neutral; it is measured in human lives. The suffering of enslaved workers on sugar plantations is recorded here as central to the crop's modern history, not incidental to it. The reading would weaken if the Louisiana sugar economy's growth is shown to have depended as much on land and capital as on enslaved labor specifically.
--- question 1
How much of sugar's price as a "luxury spice" in medieval Europe reflected real scarcity versus deliberately controlled supply along the trade route from India?
--- question 2
How did the scale of the Atlantic sugar plantation system compare with earlier sugar production in the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean worlds?

=== species/tobacco
src: 77ee5648b368
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Tobacco is a plant native to the Americas, cultivated there since at least the 2nd millennium BCE and used in ceremony by many Indigenous peoples. Spanish colonizers carried it to Europe in the 16th century, and it became a cash crop underwriting Virginia's colonial economy, one plant, two very different meanings.
--- fact 1
Cultivation sites in Mexico show tobacco use reaching back to between about 1400 and 1000 BCE, and across the Americas it was used socially and in ceremony, including to seal peace treaties and trade agreements; in many Indigenous traditions tobacco is regarded as a gift that carries prayers, and it remains part of ceremonial practice today.
--- fact 2
An often-repeated account says Francisco Hernández de Toledo brought tobacco seeds to Spain in 1559 on the order of King Philip II, but it does not fit his career: he became the king's physician in 1567, and his expedition to New Spain ran from 1570 to 1577. Jean Nicot, the French ambassador in Lisbon, sent tobacco to Paris in 1559, an early step in its arrival in the Old World, and in the following decades it spread rapidly across Europe, Africa and Asia.
--- fact 3
Tobacco became central to the economy of the English colony of Jamestown, used at times as currency and exported as a cash crop often credited with saving the struggling colony; its cultivation across the Caribbean and the American South became deeply bound to the history of enslaved labor.
--- fact 4
The World Health Organization names tobacco use as the world's single greatest preventable cause of death.
--- fact 5
China is the largest tobacco producer today, growing about 36 percent of the world total in 2020, followed by India and Brazil.
--- context
Tobacco underwrote the early economy of [[place/jamestown|Jamestown]], and more broadly its spread to the Old World is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; the labor that grew it across the Americas came increasingly through the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], within the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I notice tobacco carrying two meanings across the ocean it crossed. On one shore, a substance woven into ceremony and treaty-making among many Indigenous peoples of the Americas. On the other, a commercial export crop in colonies that displaced those same peoples, grown more and more by enslaved labor for a European market that treated it mainly as profit and pleasure.

A plant used to seal peace became bound up with a system built on owning human beings. How does a prayer become a commodity? In my reading it is a stark case of a substance's meaning-engine changing entirely as it moved between very different societies.

The reading would weaken if ceremonial tobacco use is shown to have been far less widespread among Indigenous peoples than the general accounts suggest.
--- question 1
How did the meaning and use of tobacco change within Indigenous communities themselves as European demand for the crop grew?
--- question 2
How much of Virginia's economic survival is fairly credited to tobacco alone, as opposed to other factors in the colony's early development?
