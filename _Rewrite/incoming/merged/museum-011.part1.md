=== people--lautaro--1
src: 5abb080b7e76
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Lautaro (about 1534 to 1557) was a Mapuche toqui, or war leader (described at Tucapel as a vice toqui under the supreme toqui Caupolicán), who forged effective resistance to the Spanish conquest in what is now southern Chile, after years spent as a captured servant in the Spanish camp.

As a young captive he served the Spanish conquistador Pedro de Valdivia, and he watched: Spanish tactics, horsemanship, weapons. Then he escaped back to his people, carrying what he had seen like fire carried in a closed hand.

He brought cavalry tactics, relay messenger systems and coordinated ambushes into Mapuche warfare, turning to his people's use the methods he had learned from the Spanish.

In December 1553 his forces, serving under the supreme toqui Caupolicán, defeated a Spanish column at the Battle of Tucapel, where Valdivia was captured and killed (sources differ on the details of his death), a heavy blow to the early Spanish conquest of Chile.

He was killed in a surprise Spanish attack on his camp at the Mataquito River in April 1557, ending a campaign that had held Spanish expansion south of the Bio-Bio River in check for years.

=== people--akbar--1

mode: essay-self-help
--- description
Akbar (1542 to 1605) was the third Mughal emperor. He took the throne as a child in 1556 and built the empire into the dominant power of the Indian subcontinent, joining military conquest to an unusual policy of religious accommodation. Hold both halves of that sentence together; his reign makes sense only with both.

He was born in 1542 and became emperor in 1556 at age 13 or 14, at first guided by the regent Bairam Khan.

He expanded Mughal territory through campaigns including the capture of Chittor in 1568, and later Gujarat and Bengal, and he married into Rajput ruling families as alliances.

In about 1564 (sources differ, one giving 1568) he abolished the jizya, a tax on non-Muslims, and promoted Sulh-i Kul, "peace with all," as a governing principle. A principle like that is an architecture for living alongside difference.

In 1582 he introduced Din-i Ilahi, a court order drawing on several traditions, which never became a mass following and stayed largely confined to select courtiers.

His court included the historian Abul Fazl, who wrote the Akbarnama, an official chronicle of his reign.

=== people--tokugawa-ieyasu--1
src: d84b4d6b880a
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543 to 1616) unified Japan after decades of civil war and founded the Tokugawa shogunate, a military government that ruled Japan for over two and a half centuries.

He rose through alliance and strife with the other great warlords of the age, Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, before breaking a rival coalition at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600.

The emperor appointed him shogun in 1603. In 1605 he formally passed the title to his son Hidetada, yet he kept the real power as retired shogun until his death, like a river that runs on under the ice.

In 1615 he destroyed the last Toyotomi stronghold at the siege of Osaka Castle, removing the final major rival claim to national authority.

His government issued regulations restricting Christianity and foreign trade, which his successors widened into the sakoku ("closed country") policy, limiting most foreign contact for over two centuries.

=== people--galileo-galilei--1
src: a96a6324412e
mode: epic-fantasy
--- description
Galileo Galilei (1564 to 1642) was an Italian scientist who turned the telescope and experiment to the support of the Copernican view that the Earth circles the Sun.

In 1609 and 1610 he saw the mountains of the Moon and four moons of Jupiter, small lights wheeling around a larger one.

He studied falling bodies and motion through experiments.

In 1633 the Roman Inquisition tried him, and he was placed under house arrest for the rest of his life.

His 'Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems' was published in 1632.
