=== technology/oral-rehydration-therapy
src: 6bd8823cfec4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Oral rehydration therapy treats severe diarrheal dehydration with a simple drink of water, salts and sugar, developed through clinical trials at the Cholera Research Laboratory in Dhaka in the late 1960s and proven at large scale during Bangladesh's 1971 refugee crisis.
--- fact 1
The Pakistan-SEATO Cholera Research Laboratory, established in Dhaka in December 1960, hosted the research, with physicians including David Nalin and Norbert Hirschhorn running key trials from about 1967 to 1970.
--- fact 2
Trials in 1968 to 1970 showed that a solution of water, salt and glucose, drunk by patients who could still swallow, cut cholera deaths from around 30 percent under intravenous-only treatment to about 3 percent.
--- fact 3
Oral rehydration's glucose component works because glucose actively helps the intestine absorb sodium and water together, allowing rehydration without needles or hospital equipment.
--- fact 4
The method proved decisive in 1971, when roughly ten million refugees fled violence around Bangladesh's war of independence and overwhelmed the capacity for intravenous treatment in crowded camps.
--- fact 5
The World Health Organization and UNICEF later promoted oral rehydration therapy worldwide as one of the most cost-effective public health interventions ever developed, credited with saving tens of millions of lives from diarrheal disease, particularly in children.
--- context
Oral rehydration therapy directly counters the dehydration caused by [[species/vibrio-cholerae|Vibrio cholerae]] and other diarrheal pathogens, and it stands alongside [[technology/vaccination|Vaccination]] among low-cost interventions with outsized effects on global child mortality. Oral Rehydration Therapy sits in the [[era/information-age|Information Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
You can see a technology stripped to its essentials: no machine, no factory and no patent stood between the discovery that sugar helps the gut absorb salt water and its use to save a life, which let a wartime refugee crisis become the proving ground for a treatment cheap enough for any household. That a discovery this consequential emerged from research aimed narrowly at cholera, rather than from a program targeting child mortality broadly, is another case of careful science yielding benefits its investigators had not fully anticipated. This reading would weaken if evidence shows the 1971 refugee-camp results were exaggerated relative to controlled clinical outcomes.
--- question 1
How much of the estimated lives saved by oral rehydration therapy can be attributed to the original Dhaka formula, as against later refinements to the solution?
--- question 2
What barriers have limited oral rehydration therapy's use in regions where diarrheal disease remains a leading cause of child death?

=== technology/photovoltaic-cell
src: 1bec60d0af1f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The modern photovoltaic cell, which converts sunlight directly into electricity using semiconductor materials, was demonstrated by Bell Labs researchers Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller and Gerald Pearson on 25 April 1954, drawing on the same silicon semiconductor research that had recently produced the transistor.
--- fact 1
Chapin was seeking a reliable power source for remote telephone equipment when Fuller, a chemist, supplied silicon treated with impurities, and Pearson, a physicist, found that dipping it further created a functioning solar cell.
--- fact 2
The 1954 "solar battery," as Bell Labs called it, converted about 6 percent of sunlight into electricity, a dramatic improvement over earlier, far less efficient selenium-based light-sensitive cells developed in the 19th century.
--- fact 3
Early photovoltaic cells were too expensive for general electricity generation and found their first major practical use powering satellites, beginning with Vanguard 1 in 1958.
--- fact 4
Efficiency and manufacturing improvements over subsequent decades, driven partly by the same semiconductor industry that grew around the transistor and integrated circuit, gradually lowered costs.
--- fact 5
By the early 21st century, falling costs made photovoltaic solar power competitive with fossil-fuel generation in many markets, making it one of the fastest-growing sources of new electricity generation worldwide.
--- context
The photovoltaic cell emerged from the same Bell Labs semiconductor research that produced the [[technology/transistor|Transistor]], and its eventual affordability reshaped how the [[technology/electrical-power-grid|Electrical Power Grid]] sources power. Photovoltaic Cell sits in the [[era/information-age|Information Age]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
You can see a decades-long gap between invention and consequence: a laboratory curiosity that converted only a small fraction of sunlight to electricity in 1954 became, within one lifetime, a serious rival to the fossil-fuel power that had run the industrial world for two centuries. That the breakthrough came from researchers solving a narrow, unglamorous problem, powering remote telephone lines, rather than pursuing energy transformation as a goal, suggests that a technology's largest eventual effect is not always visible to its inventors. This reading would weaken if evidence shows Bell Labs researchers explicitly anticipated large-scale power generation as the cell's destination.
--- question 1
How much of the multi-decade delay between 1954 and mass adoption was due to cost, and how much to competing energy-policy priorities?
--- question 2
Could a different early application, besides satellites, have accelerated cost reduction sooner?
