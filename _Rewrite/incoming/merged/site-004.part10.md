=== place/cahokia
src: 6505fd4f24a9
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Cahokia was the largest city of the Mississippian culture, near present-day St. Louis, a city of raised earth that flourished from about 1050 to 1350 CE.
--- fact 1
At its height, about 1050 to 1150, it may have held 10,000 to 20,000 people, a crowd of hearths, though some estimates are much higher.
--- fact 2
Monks Mound, about 30 meters high, is the largest earthen mound in the Americas, a hill raised by human hands.
--- fact 3
A circle of posts, known as Woodhenge, likely kept watch on the sun.
--- fact 4
Its population lived on maize agriculture and declined after about 1250, like a fire burning low, for reasons that are debated. It was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1982.
--- context
Cahokia is the largest known urban center in pre-Columbian [[region/north-america|North America]] north of Mexico, a crown upon that wide land. See [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]].
--- observer
I look for their writing and find none. I look for their record and find it in the earth. I find monumental architecture shaped from soil, a city that kept its memory in mounds. Who taught me that a city needs a book? Its size reminds me that urban life was not limited to the regions usually associated with cities. The map of cities is wider than the map in my head.
--- question 1
Why did Cahokia decline, its fires burning low?

=== place/caral
src: 20674c43e204
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Caral is an ancient city in Peru's Supe Valley, occupied from about 2600 BCE, and one of the oldest known urban centers in the Americas, an early flame of city life in that land.
--- fact 1
Its monumental platform mounds and sunken plazas date to the same era as the Egyptian pyramids, two dawns breaking in one age.
--- fact 2
The society had no pottery, and there is no evidence of large-scale warfare, no scar of great battles.
--- fact 3
Coastal fishing provided anchovies and sardines, and cotton for nets, while inland farming provided crops: sea and field feeding one city.
--- fact 4
Knotted cords found at the site may be early relatives of the quipu, memory tied in string; see [[technology/quipu|Quipu]]. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009.
--- context
Caral shows [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]] kindling independently in the [[region/andes|Andes]], long before the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] rose.
--- observer
I was taught that cities are built on grain and conquest. I look at Caral and find a city fed by sea and farm together. I find monuments. I find, it seems, little violence. What else did I accept because no one showed me otherwise? It challenges the picture I carried. A city can be held together by something other than the sword.
--- question 1
What bound Caral's people together, without weapons or pottery?

=== place/carthage
src: 40ef53036a52
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Carthage was a Phoenician colony on the coast of present-day Tunisia, traditionally founded in 814 BCE, which grew into a major Mediterranean power, a queen of the western sea, until Rome destroyed it in 146 BCE.
--- fact 1
It grew into a naval and commercial empire across the western Mediterranean, its sails spread from shore to shore.
--- fact 2
Three Punic Wars against Rome were fought between 264 and 146 BCE, a long contest of two powers.
--- fact 3
Hannibal crossed the Alps with an army in 218 BCE, leading his soldiers over the mountain wall.
--- fact 4
Rome destroyed the city in 146 BCE and later founded it anew as a Roman colony. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979.
--- context
Carthage descended from the [[culture/phoenicians|Phoenicians]] and lost its long rivalry with the [[culture/romans|Romans]], two powers facing each other across the water. See [[place/rome|Rome]].
--- observer
Ask who is telling the story. The Observer notes that most of what is known about Carthage comes from its enemies, since its own records were largely lost. Think of learning about a family only from the neighbors who took its house. The winners of a war often write the losers' histories. A silence in the archive is not the same as an empty life.
--- question 1
What did Carthaginian civilization look like in its own words, spoken by its own people?
