=== species/soybean
title: Soybean
src: dbb27ccb0936
mode: essay-self-help

--- summary
The soybean is a legume domesticated between the Yellow and Huai rivers in China between roughly 9,000 and 6,000 years ago. For a long time it was food, central to East Asian diets through tofu and soy sauce; now it is grown worldwide chiefly as animal feed and oil. If you want to see how one plant can change its place in the human ledger, this is the lineage to study.
--- fact 1
Its origin is a place you can find on a map: north-central China, between the Yellow River and the Huai River, where the earliest evidence comes from the site of Jiahu in Henan province, dated to between about 9,000 and 7,800 years ago.
--- fact 2
Chinese tradition credits the legendary Emperor Shennong, dated in myth to 2853 BCE, with naming the soybean one of five sacred grains alongside rice, wheat, barley and millet: a lineage of honor held in legend.
--- fact 3
Consider the patience in a fermented food: soy sauce, fermented bean paste, natto and tempeh, along with tofu made from soy milk, developed over many centuries in Asia; the earliest Japanese textual reference to soybeans appears in the Kojiki, compiled in 712 CE.
--- fact 4
The soybean crossed into the Americas and Europe in the 18th century: its first recorded North American cultivation came in 1765 on Skidaway Island, Georgia, and its first French cultivation in 1779, small thresholds for so large a future.
--- fact 5
Genetically modified, herbicide-tolerant soybean varieties were introduced commercially in 1995 and made up about 8 percent of United States soybean acreage by 1997, rising to about 93 percent by 2010; that is what a technological threshold looks like once it is crossed.
--- fact 6
In 2019 Brazil, the United States and Argentina were the largest producers, together accounting for roughly four-fifths of world output, and about 80 percent of the global soybean crop goes to feed livestock.
--- context
Think of the soybean's domestication as one of East Asia's founding contributions to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]], developed alongside rice and other regional crops. Its adoption of genetically modified varieties from the 1990s parallels the yield-focused breeding of the 20th-century [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]] in other crops: the same doctrine of yield, carried into a new era. It sits in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the soybean has passed through two very different uses over its long relationship with humans. For millennia it was mainly a food, transformed by fermentation and pressing into tofu, sauce and other dishes across East Asia. In the last century it has been mainly an input, grown at enormous scale chiefly to feed livestock and produce oil, in a system reliant on genetically modified varieties barely three decades old. Picture the same bean in two hands: one presses it into curd for a family meal, the other pours it into a feed silo by the ton. The bean changed far less than the architecture around it. This shift shows how a crop's tool-lineage of use can change faster than the plant itself. The reading would weaken if the rapid adoption of genetically modified soy proves to have depended more on regulatory approval sequencing than on farmer preference.
--- question 1
