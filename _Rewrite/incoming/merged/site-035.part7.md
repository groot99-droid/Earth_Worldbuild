=== species/anomalocaris
src: c74bfb426ccc
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anomalocaris was a large swimming animal of the Cambrian seas, armed with spiny grasping appendages, a round mouth ringed by plates, and large compound eyes. It lived about 515 to 505 million years ago, is known from Canada and Australia, and is a stem-group relative of arthropods, a cousin from before the family took its final shape.
--- fact 1
Anomalocaris is securely known from the Burgess Shale of British Columbia (about 505 million years old) and the Emu Bay Shale of South Australia (about 515); Chinese fossils once assigned to it have mostly been moved to other genera, like names rubbed from an old stone.
--- fact 2
One creature was scattered into three ghosts: its appendages were described in 1892 as a crustacean tail, its mouth as a jellyfish, and its body as a sea cucumber, until Harry Whittington and Derek Briggs showed in 1985 that they belonged to one animal.
--- fact 3
Complete Burgess specimens are about 20 to 25 cm long; estimates of 35 to 50 cm include appendages and tail, and a claim of about 1 m rests on fragments, a giant built from shards.
--- fact 4
Compound eyes from the Emu Bay Shale, up to 3 cm long, preserve at least 16,000 lenses, a mosaic of light comparable to the sharpest-sighted living arthropods.
--- fact 5
The mouth looks too weak to crush trilobite shells, and studies propose suction feeding or soft prey instead. Whether it was the top predator of its seas is debated.
--- context
Anomalocaris lived in the [[era/cambrian-period|Cambrian Period]], a signature animal of the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]], sharing its waters with [[species/trilobites|Trilobites]], [[species/hallucigenia|Hallucigenia]], and [[species/pikaia|Pikaia]]. Its sites include the Burgess Shale, the Emu Bay Shale, and Chengjiang Fossil Site, stone pages where the old seas left their writing. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Anomalocaris was first assembled from three fossils believed to be three animals, and there is a lesson for you in that. What you see depends on the pieces you hold and the frame you bring to them: the apex predator of popular pictures depends on how its mouth is read. Consider, too, that it is one of many body plans that arose and vanished in the Cambrian, when nothing marked in advance which would leave descendants. The reading would weaken if radiodonts were shown to be ancestral to a living animal group. Survival is a verdict delivered late, never a promise made early.

=== species/archaea
src: 9ca892147305
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Archaea are single-celled organisms without a nucleus, one of the three domains of life beside Bacteria and eukaryotes, a hidden branch of the great tree. Recognized as separate only in 1977, they may date to about 3.5 billion years ago, though no fossil archaeon has been identified with confidence.
--- fact 1
In 1977 Carl Woese and George Fox read ribosomal RNA sequences and showed that methane-producing microbes stood as far from bacteria as from eukaryotes; in 1990 the three domains were named Bacteria, Archaea, and Eucarya.
--- fact 2
Archaea were first known from hot springs, salt lakes, and methane-rich mud, but they also dwell in soils, deep sediments, and the open ocean, where in one Pacific study they made up about 39 percent of the smallest plankton at depth, a silent multitude in the dark water.
--- fact 3
The earliest evidence is indirect, a shadow rather than a body. Methane in Australian rocks about 3.5 billion years old was read in 2006 as microbial, but nonliving processes can give similar isotope values. Molecular clocks range from 3.5 to 4.1 billion years, and they too are disputed.
--- fact 4
Asgard archaea, first found in 2015, carry genes for proteins otherwise known only in eukaryotes. Some researchers therefore place eukaryotes inside Archaea, which would leave two primary domains, not three, the great tree redrawn around a single fork.
--- fact 5
No archaeon is established as a human pathogen; for all their multitudes, none is known to bring sickness to us.
--- context
Archaea trace back to the [[species/last-universal-common-ancestor|Last Universal Common Ancestor]] and the [[event/origin-of-life|Origin of Life]], and they belong to the [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]]. From their relatives rose eukaryotic cells; see [[event/origin-of-eukaryotes|Origin of Eukaryotes]]. Compare the bacterial [[species/cyanobacteria|Cyanobacteria]]. See [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a third branch of life sat unrecognized in hot springs and salt lakes until sequence comparison revealed it in 1977. Sit with that: the thing was always there, and we lacked the instrument to see it. Then comes the stranger turn. The cells you are made of possibly descend from a merger involving an archaeon, so the stranger in the hot spring may belong to your own lineage. The Observer takes it that categories are set by the tools available. The reading would weaken if eukaryotes proved not to have archaeal ancestry. We name only what we have learned to see.
