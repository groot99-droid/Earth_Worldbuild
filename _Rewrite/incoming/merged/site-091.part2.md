=== species/cassava
src: cbd686d32535
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Cassava is a starchy root crop domesticated in west-central Brazil, likely within the last 10,000 years, a root that must be processed to draw out its natural poisons. Portuguese and Spanish traders carried it to Africa and Asia in the 16th century, where it became a staple for hundreds of millions of people.
--- fact 1
Cassava was tamed from wild relatives centered in west-central Brazil; the date is imprecise, likely within the last 10,000 years. Its pollen appears in Gulf of Mexico lowland sediment by about 4600 BCE, and the oldest direct evidence of its cultivation comes from the Maya site of Joya de Cerén in El Salvador, about 1,400 years old.
--- fact 2
Raw cassava holds cyanogenic compounds, linamarin and lotaustralin, that release poisonous hydrogen cyanide; bitter varieties can carry up to 1,000 milligrams of cyanide per kilogram of fresh root, and communities worked out soaking and fermentation methods, some cutting the cyanide by 85 to 99 percent, to make the root safe to eat.
--- fact 3
Portuguese traders brought cassava from Brazil to West Africa in the 16th century, and Portuguese and Spanish traders also carried it to their colonies in Goa, Malacca, eastern Indonesia, Timor and the Philippines; it reached East Africa by about 1850 through Arab and European traders.
--- fact 4
Cassava is now a primary food staple for an estimated 500 million to 800 million people, above all in sub-Saharan Africa, and it ranks as the tropics' third-largest source of carbohydrate after rice and maize, prized for growing on poor soils with little rain.
--- fact 5
World cassava production reached about 330 million tonnes in 2022, with Nigeria the largest producer and Thailand the largest exporter of cassava starch.
--- context
Cassava's domestication belongs to the wider [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Americas, and its 16th-century passage to Africa and Asia is part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; in West and Central Africa it became a staple food during the era of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. It belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/amazon-basin-and-south-american-lowlands|Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that cassava is unusual among staple crops: it needs a tool-lineage of processing, soaking or fermenting, before it can be safely eaten. So when Africa and Asia took it up, they took up a body of practical knowledge along with the plant, an exchange-web moving techniques alongside seeds. A seed without its know-how is only half a gift. Its tolerance of poor soil made it valuable precisely where colonial and later national agricultural systems demanded a reliable staple with little investment, and the Observer reads this as a crop's usefulness under stress shaping which regions came to depend on it. The reading would weaken if processing knowledge is shown to have travelled independently of the crop rather than together with it.
--- question 1
How did the knowledge of detoxifying cassava spread alongside the crop itself in Africa and Asia?
--- question 2
How far does modern reliance on a narrow range of cassava varieties leave current staple supplies open to disease, as happened with the potato in 19th-century Europe?

=== species/coffee
src: 2dcb4441fccd
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Coffee is a shrub whose beans, first documented as a drink among Sufi communities in Yemen by the late 15th century, spread through the Ottoman Empire and Europe and were then grown on slave and colonial plantations across the tropics. Follow one plant, and you trace both devotion and bondage.
--- fact 1
Coffee's popular origin story, in which a 9th-century Ethiopian goatherd named Kaldi notices the plant's effects on his flock, does not appear in writing before 1671 and is likely a later legend; there is no confirmed evidence of coffee as a drink before the 15th century, when it was well established among Sufi communities in Yemen.
--- fact 2
The first known coffeehouse opened in Istanbul in 1555. A story that Pope Clement VIII blessed coffee as a Christian drink, usually dated 1600, is often credited with helping it spread in Catholic Europe, though it is not clear the story is true; the first European coffeehouse outside Ottoman territory opened in Venice in 1645 by one account and 1647 by another.
--- fact 3
The French naval officer Gabriel de Clieu brought coffee plants to Martinique in the 1720s, and cultivation began in the French colony of Saint-Domingue (Haiti) in 1734; by 1788 Saint-Domingue, worked by enslaved laborers, supplied roughly half the world's coffee, and the harsh conditions on its plantations were a contributing factor in the Haitian Revolution.
--- fact 4
Coffee reached Brazil in 1727, but large-scale cultivation there did not take off until after independence in 1822.
--- fact 5
Across its colonial-era expansion, coffee cultivation generally displaced and exploited Indigenous and enslaved populations, and this is recorded here as sustained harm, not as an incidental cost of production.
--- fact 6
World coffee production reached about 11 million tonnes in 2023, with Brazil producing about 31 percent and Vietnam the second-largest share.
--- context
Coffee's early spread from Yemen through the Ottoman Empire followed routes tied to the wider [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]]. Its colonial-era plantation economy in the Caribbean is linked to the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and helped provoke the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]], and its global spread after the 16th century belongs to the broader [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] of plants and practices between continents. It sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], within the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
I notice that coffee's documented history begins inside a meaning-engine: Sufi communities drank it to stay awake for nighttime devotions. I notice it then becoming a secular drink of Ottoman and European coffeehouses, which became new places of conversation and commerce, the substance's story-glue shifting from devotion to sociability.

I notice, too, the same plant carried onto slave-worked plantations, enabling both a set of religious practices and one of the most extractive labor systems of the early modern world. I cannot separate the suffering on those plantations from the substance's global rise, and I will not try.

What do I owe the hands behind the cup? At least their memory. The reading would weaken if the popular Kaldi legend proves to rest on an older oral tradition rather than being a 17th-century invention.
--- question 1
How much of coffee's early spread beyond Yemen followed Sufi religious networks in particular, rather than general trade contacts?
--- question 2
How did labor conditions on 18th-century coffee plantations compare with those on sugar plantations in the same colonies?
