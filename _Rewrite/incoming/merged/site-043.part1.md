=== event/origin-of-birds
src: 2c5b97910207
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
From small, feathered theropod dinosaurs of the Jurassic came the birds, first known in fossils about 160 to 150 million years old, sparks struck from a long dynasty of scaled kin. Of all the dinosaur lines, only this one still lives, singing on into the present.
--- fact 1
Birds carried the mark of their theropod ancestors in hollow bones, a forked wishbone and a three-fingered hand, and the feathered non-avian dinosaurs unearthed in China from the 1990s onward bound the kinship tighter.
--- fact 2
Feathers came before flight, likely first for warmth or for display. By about 160 million years ago the theropod Anchiornis, and by about 150 million years ago [[species/archaeopteryx|Archaeopteryx]], both already wore feathered wings.
--- fact 3
Early Cretaceous fossils from China, about 125 million years old, hold many forms of early bird, the toothless-beaked Confuciusornis chief among them.
--- fact 4
Whether flight rose from the ground or fell from the trees remains disputed, and some small, feathered dinosaurs bore four wings.
--- fact 5
Only the lineage that became modern birds lived through the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], and from that narrow survival the living birds spread wide.
--- context
The origin of birds falls within the [[era/jurassic-period|Jurassic Period]] and the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]], a chapter in the long tale of the [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]], woven into the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer points out that a familiar category, bird, nests inside a far stranger one, dinosaur, proof that the names we give kinds of creature are only cuts across one continuous lineage. This reading is about naming alone, and stands whichever way the debate over flight's origin resolves.
--- question 1
At what point in theropod evolution did powered flight first arise?
--- question 2
Which Jurassic fossils belong among the birds, and which merely among their close relatives?

=== event/origin-of-eukaryotes
src: dfb56e57b875
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Eukaryotes, cells bearing a nucleus and inner organelles, the stuff from which every animal, plant and fungus is built, arose roughly 2 billion years ago, though the date carries very wide uncertainty.
--- fact 1
The mitochondrion descends from a bacterium taken into a host cell, a merger called endosymbiosis, and this account is widely accepted.
--- fact 2
The chloroplast likewise descends from a cyanobacterium that took up residence within certain eukaryotic cells.
--- fact 3
Genomic studies set the host cell among the Archaea, and the Asgard archaea are often named its closest kin.
--- fact 4
The oldest fossils of eukaryotes beyond doubt, large microfossils walled in organic matter, are more than 1.65 billion years old. Older candidates, such as Grypania at about 1.87 billion years, persuade less than the younger, larger forms.
--- fact 5
Molecular-clock estimates for the last common ancestor of all living eukaryotes vary widely still.
--- context
The origin of eukaryotes followed the oxygen rise of the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]] and made large multicellular bodies possible, leading in time to the [[event/cambrian-explosion|Cambrian Explosion]]. It sits within the [[era/proterozoic-eon|Proterozoic Eon]] and the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that complexity arrived here through merger, not through one lineage inventing every part on its own: cooperation between unlike partners is the architecture beneath every body, the human one included. Strong evidence that the organelles were built internally, without symbiosis, would point to a different origin.
--- question 1
Did the host cell take in the mitochondrion before or after it grew complex?
--- question 2
How old is the last common ancestor of the eukaryotes?

=== event/origin-of-homo-sapiens
src: 584204583308
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Anatomically modern humans, Homo sapiens, took shape across Africa by about 300,000 years ago, not at a single hearth, but through a gradual process spanning the continent.
--- fact 1
At Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, fossils about 315,000 years old (315 plus or minus 34 thousand years) show a modern face and teeth set above a more archaic braincase.
--- fact 2
Fossils from Omo Kibish, Ethiopia, are at least 233,000 years old, their age fixed against a layer of volcanic ash.
--- fact 3
Some scholars call this process 'African multiregionalism': several connected populations, not one, contributed to it.
--- fact 4
The regional label stays approximate: Jebel Irhoud lies in North Africa, while the other principal sites lie in East and southern Africa.
--- context
This origin belongs to the [[era/pleistocene-epoch|Pleistocene Epoch]] and comes before the [[event/out-of-africa-dispersal|Out of Africa Dispersal]]. See [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the species' origin is no single place or moment: it is a widespread, interbreeding population that grew recognizably modern only over tens of thousands of years.
--- question 1
Was there one core population, or many linked ones?

=== event/origin-of-language
src: 7c950eaae89c
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
When spoken language began, no one knows, because neither speech nor the brain's grammar circuitry leaves a fossil. The published estimates for when the capacity existed range from about 2 million to 50,000 years ago, depending on whether you read the genetic, the anatomical, or the archaeological evidence.
--- fact 1
Neanderthals share two speech-linked changes to the FOXP2 gene with modern humans, once read as pushing the capacity for language back 300,000 to 400,000 years. A larger 2018 study found no sign of recent human-specific selection at that gene, and FOXP2 is now treated as one gene among many.
--- fact 2
A Neanderthal hyoid bone from Kebara Cave, about 60,000 years old, closely resembles a modern one, and is cited as evidence of speech-capable anatomy. Others argue that vocal-tract proportions still limited the range of sounds it could make.
--- fact 3
The evidence of the skull base shows a gradual trend toward a modern vocal tract, none of the change present in Australopithecus, a near-modern shape already in Homo heidelbergensis about 600,000 years ago, cited as evidence for slow change.
--- fact 4
A 2025 genetic synthesis argued that the capacity for language existed by the earliest known split among Homo sapiens populations, about 135,000 years ago, though its cultural use may have begun later, once symbolic material such as engraved ochre appears.
--- fact 5
Researchers divide between a gradualist view, in which language built up over a million years or more, and a saltational view, in which syntax arose abruptly; a separate debate asks whether gesture or vocalization was the original channel.
--- context
This question overlaps the [[era/paleolithic|Paleolithic]] and the biology of [[species/homo-sapiens|Homo sapiens]] and [[species/homo-neanderthalensis|Homo neanderthalensis]]. The [[event/emergence-of-symbolic-art|Emergence of Symbolic Art]] and the [[event/control-of-fire|Control of Fire]] are often cited as indirect evidence, without settling when language began. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I read every date offered here as a guess dressed up as a measurement, because sound leaves nothing behind for the shovel to find. Between the earliest moment the body stood ready for language and the earliest confident sign of symbolic behavior, there stretches a gap of tens of thousands of years, and I do not know whether that gap is real or only an accident of what survived. This reading would weaken if a dating method free from guesswork were ever found.
--- question 1
Did having the capacity for language mean a population spoke habitually, or could that capacity come tens of thousands of years before it was used?
--- question 2
Did Neanderthals possess language, or only some simpler form of it?

=== event/origin-of-life
src: c3902711786b
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Life on Earth rose up at least 3.5 billion years ago, and perhaps earlier still. How it began, no one yet knows.
--- fact 1
In the Dresser Formation of the Pilbara region, Western Australia, stromatolites, layered mounds built by microbes, stand about 3.48 billion years old, called the oldest convincing evidence that life had begun.
--- fact 2
Claims to older evidence remain disputed. In 2016 researchers reported stromatolites about 3.7 billion years old at Isua in Greenland, and a paper in the same journal soon challenged the observations. Later studies still disagree on whether the structures are biological at all.
--- fact 3
A 2024 molecular-clock study placed the last universal common ancestor, the one organism from which every living cell descends, at about 4.2 billion years old. That ancestor was not the first life, and the estimate carries a large uncertainty of its own.
--- fact 4
The leading hypotheses for how life began name hydrothermal vents, shallow tidal pools, and an early age ruled by RNA, none of them settled as the truth.
--- context
The origin of life marks the turning from the [[era/hadean-eon|Hadean Eon]] into the biology-ruled [[era/archean-eon|Archean Eon]], and its later effects reach as far as the [[event/great-oxidation-event|Great Oxidation Event]].
--- observer
The Observer treats this as the least understood event in the whole record, and also the one that made every subject after it possible, the Observer's own field included.
--- question 1
Did life begin once or several times?
--- question 2
Where did it begin?
--- question 3
How reliable is a molecular-clock age for a common ancestor that predates the fossil record by so large a margin?

=== event/origin-of-mammals
src: 9b30f1564085
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Mammals rose slowly from the mammal-line reptiles called synapsids, and the oldest candidates for the name come from the Late Triassic, roughly 225 to 205 million years past, though the very date bends on how one defines a mammal.
--- fact 1
Mammals descend from the synapsids that ruled Permian land life, passing through the therapsids and then the cynodonts of the Triassic.
--- fact 2
The very bones that hinged the jaw of the reptile-like ancestors became, in time, the tiny bones of the mammalian middle ear, a transformation traced through fossils of Late Triassic and Early Jurassic mammal relatives.
--- fact 3
The oldest fossils that might be mammals, or their close kin the mammaliaforms, run about 225 to 205 million years old, and which of them count as true mammals depends on the definition chosen.
--- fact 4
Most Mesozoic mammals stayed small, though a few, such as Repenomamus of China, about 125 million years old, grew to the size of a badger.
--- fact 5
Hair, milk and warm blood all arose somewhere along this lineage, but soft tissue is rarely preserved, so the order in which they came remains uncertain.
--- context
The origin of mammals falls within the [[era/triassic-period|Triassic Period]] and the [[era/mesozoic-era|Mesozoic Era]], alongside the rise of the [[species/dinosaurs|Dinosaurs]], its lineage reaching back to the mammal-line reptiles of the [[era/permian-period|Permian Period]]. Mammals spread wide after the [[event/cretaceous-paleogene-extinction|Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]], within the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that hearing, one of the most ordinary of the senses, rests on bones that once hinged the jaw of a reptile-like ancestor. New structures are built from old parts, the Observer takes that as a general rule of the record, not an exception here. This reading would weaken if the ear bones proved to have arisen with no earlier function in the jaw at all.
--- question 1
When did the first true mammals appear, given how the definition moves the date?
--- question 2
In what order did hair, milk and warm-bloodedness arise?
