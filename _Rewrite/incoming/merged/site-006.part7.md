=== place/kyoto
title: Kyoto
src: c033fcbe568d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Kyoto is a city on the Japanese island of Honshu, founded in 794 CE as the imperial capital Heian-kyō, and it remained the seat of the emperor, a hearth the throne did not leave, until 1868.
--- fact 1
In 794 Emperor Kanmu founded Kyoto as Heian-kyō, laying it out on a grid plan modeled on Chinese capitals such as Chang'an, its streets ruled straight as the threads of a loom.
--- fact 2
Under the Heian court (794 to 1185) Kyoto was the heart of court culture, and from it came the novel The Tale of Genji, written in the early 11th century by Murasaki Shikibu.
--- fact 3
The Ashikaga shogunate ruled from Kyoto from 1336 to 1573, and the Onin War (1467 to 1477) laid much of the city in ruin.
--- fact 4
Under the Tokugawa shogunate (1603 to 1868) political power lay in Edo, while Kyoto remained the imperial seat and a center of crafts and Buddhist institutions, a moon still shining while the sun stood elsewhere.
--- fact 5
In 1868 to 1869, after the Meiji Restoration, the emperor moved to Tokyo (formerly Edo), and Kyoto's long reign as capital came to its end.
--- fact 6
In 1945 Kyoto was taken off the list of possible atomic-bomb targets at the urging of US Secretary of War Henry Stimson, who cited its cultural significance.
--- fact 7
In 1994 UNESCO inscribed 17 "Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto", lanterns kept lit along an old road.
--- context
Kyoto's plan followed [[place/chang-an|Chang'an]] as a reflection follows its image, and the city succeeded [[place/nara|Nara]] as the imperial capital. It stayed the imperial seat under the [[culture/tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] and ceased to be the capital with the [[event/meiji-restoration|Meiji Restoration]]. It belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Kyoto held an imperial house for more than a thousand years while, for much of that time, armies and administrations answered to shoguns elsewhere. The story-glue of legitimacy and the practical power to govern sat in different places, and you should notice how long such a split can hold. A throne can keep the meaning while another hand keeps the sword.

A city laid out from a foreign template was maintained through fires and wars as a store of court culture, and its survival in 1945 shows that a city can be spared because of what its buildings hold, in a war that destroyed others. This reading would weaken if the decision not to bomb Kyoto rested mainly on operational reasons and not on cultural ones. What a city keeps can, at times, keep the city.
--- question 1
How much of the Heian grid still runs beneath Kyoto's modern street plan, like old riverbeds under new fields?
--- question 2
How far did cultural reasons, weighed against military ones, decide Kyoto's removal from the 1945 target list?

=== place/l-anse-aux-meadows
title: L'Anse aux Meadows
src: 3e691fc2e0fe
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
L'Anse aux Meadows is an archaeological site on the northern tip of Newfoundland, Canada, where Norse people from Greenland raised a small settlement of sod buildings around 1000 CE, a spark struck on a far shore: the only widely accepted Norse site in North America.
--- fact 1
L'Anse aux Meadows lies at the northernmost tip of Newfoundland; in 1960 Helge and Anne Stine Ingstad found it with the help of a local resident, George Decker, and in the 1960s they excavated it, drawing it back out of the turf.
--- fact 2
The site holds eight buildings of sod over wooden frames, among them an iron smithy, a carpentry workshop and a boat-repair area, and more than 800 Norse objects came up from the ground.
--- fact 3
Radiocarbon dates place activity between about 990 and 1050 CE; a 2021 study of tree rings, reading a solar-storm signal written into the wood in the year 993, dated felled timber cut with metal tools to 1021 CE.
--- fact 4
The occupation was short, perhaps about 20 years, though this is debated; no burials or signs of farming were found, so the site appears to have been left in an orderly way, a hall swept before the door is shut; the date_end of 1030 is a rough placeholder.
--- fact 5
Butternuts found at the site grow farther south, so Norse travelers reached or traded with regions beyond Newfoundland, their wake running past the edge of the known map.
--- fact 6
The Icelandic sagas, written down in the 13th century, tell of voyages to a land called Vinland, and whether L'Anse aux Meadows is that place or a base for voyages farther south is debated.
--- fact 7
In 1978 UNESCO inscribed L'Anse aux Meadows.
--- context
L'Anse aux Meadows keeps the memory of a voyage by the [[culture/norse|Norse]] that reached North America about five centuries before the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], and long after the [[event/peopling-of-the-americas|Peopling of the Americas]]. It belongs to the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that here a story-glue, the sagas, and a physical record, the sod buildings and iron working, were long treated as separate until the Ingstads matched them. Take the lesson: a story and a site can wait a long time for each other.

The place shows a link between two hemispheres that did not become an exchange-web, because no lasting settlement, herds or diseases were left to change either side. A threshold crossed is not yet a door opened. The sagas describe meetings with the region's Indigenous people, and how those meetings went is known only from the Norse side; keep that in view, since a ledger kept by one party is only half a ledger. The reading would weaken if evidence shows that contact between the Norse and northeastern peoples was more frequent and lasting than a short-lived camp suggests.
--- question 1
Was L'Anse aux Meadows the Vinland of which the sagas tell, or only a base for voyages farther south?
--- question 2
How long did people live at the site, and why was it left behind?
