=== place/great-wall-of-china
src: adeb14c23c2a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Great Wall of China is a series of walls, ditches, towers and forts built across northern China by successive states and dynasties from about the 7th century BCE to the 17th century CE, mainly to control movement between farming societies and the steppe, a seam of stone stitched between the sown land and the wind.
--- fact 1
The Great Wall began as separate walls, scattered like broken threads, built by Warring States kingdoms such as Qi, Yan, Zhao and Qin from about the 7th century BCE (the dating of the earliest walls is uncertain); after unifying China in 221 BCE, Qin Shi Huang linked and extended walls in the north.
--- fact 2
The Han dynasty drew walls and signal towers far to the northwest along the Gansu corridor, like a spear laid along the road, to protect the routes westward and to counter the Xiongnu.
--- fact 3
The best-known standing sections, in brick and stone, belong to the Ming dynasty (1368 to 1644), and building quickened like a drumbeat after Mongol forces captured the Ming emperor Zhengtong at Tumu in 1449.
--- fact 4
A Chinese government survey published in 2012 reported about 21,000 kilometers of walls, trenches and natural barriers raised across all dynasties, of which about 8,850 kilometers are Ming.
--- fact 5
Soldiers, conscripted laborers and convicts built the wall with their own hands; how many died is unknown, and the large figures in later stories are not documented.
--- fact 6
UNESCO inscribed the Great Wall in 1987. The claim that it is visible to the naked eye from the Moon is false, a story taller than the wall itself.
--- context
The Great Wall of China threads together the [[event/unification-of-china-under-qin|Unification of China under Qin]], the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]], the [[culture/xiongnu|Xiongnu]] and the [[culture/ming-dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] like beads on one long cord, and it protected routes used in the [[event/opening-of-the-silk-roads|Opening of the Silk Roads]]. It belongs to the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] (its earliest walls), to the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Great Wall of China is a claim-line built at very large scale, yet it worked less as a sealed barrier than as a filter. Think of a sieve rather than a door: towers, garrisons and gates let a state watch and tax movement between two ways of living. That is the wall's real architecture. The image of one unbroken wall is partly a later story-glue, and some historians argue it grew mainly in the 19th and 20th centuries as a national symbol. Specialists could weaken this reading by showing that the wall's military effect was larger than a filtering account allows. A border is less a line than a practice of watching.
--- question 1
Weighed against diplomacy and regulated trade, how much did the walls change frontier outcomes?
--- question 2
For each major section of the Great Wall, how many people worked on it, and how many died building it?

=== place/great-zimbabwe
src: 6de92dbe624e
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Great Zimbabwe was a large stone-walled capital city in southeastern Africa, occupied from about the 11th to 15th century CE, a crown of stone set upon the land and ancestral to the Shona-speaking peoples.
--- fact 1
Its Great Enclosure rises in dry-stone walls up to about 11 meters high, stone laid on stone with no mortar to bind it.
--- fact 2
Older estimates gave 10,000 to 20,000 people; more recent modeling suggests the population did not rise beyond about 10,000.
--- fact 3
It traded gold and ivory with the Swahili coast and beyond, its wealth running outward like rivers to the sea.
--- fact 4
The modern country Zimbabwe carries its name, as a child carries an ancestor's.
--- context
The city belongs to the long story of the [[event/bantu-expansion|Bantu Expansion]], and to the Indian Ocean trade whose tides reached into [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]].
--- observer
I remember that colonial-era writers sometimes denied its African origin. I remember that archaeology corrected them. I stand before walls whose builders were once written out of their own work. Who gets to tell a stone what it means? A monument's meaning can be bent by the hand that describes it.
--- question 1
Who held power within these walls, and what was the political structure of the city?

=== place/harappa
src: eb7f22d34670
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Harappa is an archaeological site in Punjab, Pakistan, on a dry bed of the Ravi River, a riverbed the water has forgotten; occupied from about 3300 BCE, it was a major city of the Indus Valley Civilization until its urban phase declined after about 1900 BCE.
--- fact 1
People dwelt at Harappa from about 3300 BCE, and its urban "Mature Harappan" phase ran from about 2600 to 1900 BCE, when estimates place its population at about 20,000 to 40,000, a city at its full tide.
--- fact 2
Harappa had planned streets, brick drains and standardized weights, and its bricks kept a common size ratio used at other Indus sites, like verses held to a single meter.
--- fact 3
Harappa's seals, carved with animals and with signs in a script that has not been deciphered, a voice still locked away, are found in many Indus cities, and Indus objects such as seals and carnelian beads have been found in Mesopotamian cities.
--- fact 4
Daya Ram Sahni began excavations at Harappa in 1921, after British engineers had carried off bricks from the site in the 1850s for railway ballast, the old city laid beneath the rails.
--- fact 5
After about 1900 BCE the city's urban phase declined like an ebbing tide, and occupation went on at a smaller scale; proposed causes include changes in monsoon rainfall and in river courses, and the causes are debated.
--- context
Harappa lent its name to the [[culture/indus-valley-civilization|Indus Valley Civilization]], whose other great city was [[place/mohenjo-daro|Mohenjo-daro]], and its planned streets belong to the story of [[event/the-urban-revolution|The Urban Revolution]]. It belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/south-asia|South Asia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Harappa left thousands of inscribed seals that cannot yet be read, so its people's own account of themselves is missing. Imagine a house where every letter is written in a hand you cannot read; you come to know its owners from the furniture. So it is here: what is known of Harappa's order comes from bricks, weights and drains that suggest a shared administrative logic, a grammar of daily life. The lack of obvious palaces or royal tombs has led some scholars to propose that its rule differed from that of contemporary Mesopotamia and Egypt, though the proposal is debated. The reading would weaken if the script is deciphered and shows a more conventional royal order. Silence is not the same as absence.
--- question 1
What do the signs on the Harappan seals say, if only we could hear them?
--- question 2
Why did Harappa's urban phase ebb away after about 1900 BCE?
