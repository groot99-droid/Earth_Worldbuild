=== species/cattle
src: b2cd201aeb97
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Cattle are large grazing animals domesticated from the wild aurochs in southwestern Asia about 10,500 years ago, and with over 940 million alive today they are among the most numerous and heaviest livestock on Earth, a herd spread over the world like a moving plain.
--- fact 1
Domestic cattle descend from the aurochs (Bos primigenius), a wild ox that once ranged like thunder across Europe, North Africa and much of Asia; humans hunted aurochs long before domestication, and that older history lies outside this note.
--- fact 2
Taurine cattle were domesticated about 10,500 years ago in a zone spanning central Anatolia, the Levant and western Iran, and mitochondrial DNA suggests the taurine line may descend from as few as about 80 aurochs tamed in upper Mesopotamia, a great river of herds rising from a small spring.
--- fact 3
On the Indian subcontinent a separate domestication gave rise to the humped zebu cattle, a second lineage from a second hearth.
--- fact 4
Cattle are kept for meat, milk and leather, and as draft animals that pull carts and farm implements; taurine cattle spread across Europe and temperate Asia and were later carried by Europeans to the Americas and Australia.
--- fact 5
More than 940 million cattle lived in the world in 2022, led by India (about 307 million), Brazil (about 194 million) and China (about 102 million); by one estimate they hold the largest biomass of any animal species, about 650 million tonnes of living weight.
--- fact 6
Cattle farming accounts for about 7 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions, mostly methane rising from digestion, and overgrazing has thinned the biodiversity of some rangelands.
--- fact 7
Cattle are sacred in Hinduism, and the slaughter of cows is forbidden by law in several Indian states and in Nepal.
--- context
Cattle were brought under [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]] beside [[species/sheep|Sheep]] and [[species/goat|Goat]] as part of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in southwestern Asia, and their carrying to the Americas after 1492 belongs to the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. Cattle sit in the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and their threads run through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
This is a reading, not a fact, and I offer it as one. I watch grass that humans cannot digest become milk, meat and pulling power. I watch a herd become wealth that walks. I watch its size, in many pastoral societies, become a status-signal and a record of kin-lattice obligations.

How can one animal be property in one society and sacred in another, and not change at all? It carries opposite meaning-engines on the same back. And now its methane ties a Neolithic partnership to the planet's climate.

If herd size proves to have signaled status in far fewer societies than I assume, this reading weakens.

What we make of a creature says more about us than about the creature.
--- question 1
How many separate taming events lie behind taurine cattle, and how did the zebu and taurine lines mingle as herds moved across Asia and Africa?
--- question 2
How did ploughing with cattle reshape the size of fields and settlements after domestication?

=== species/chicken
src: 2a9705e7c1b5
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The chicken descends from the red junglefowl of Southeast Asia; carried across the Pacific and the Old World like sparks on the wind, it is now the most numerous bird on Earth, with more than 26 billion alive in 2023, though the timing of its domestication is uncertain.
--- fact 1
Chickens descend from the red junglefowl (Gallus gallus), native to Southeast Asia, and still interbreed with it, the forest never wholly left behind.
--- fact 2
Genomic studies estimate that chickens were domesticated about 8,000 years ago in Southeast Asia, but the earliest bones judged probably chicken come from Ban Non Wat in central Thailand, about 3,250 years old, and older claims from China are debated; the date_start of -1250 marks that earliest secure evidence, not the beginning of domestication.
--- fact 3
One proposal holds that wild junglefowl were drawn to rice seeds and nested nearby, another that chickens were first kept for cockfighting and only later for food; researchers dispute both, and the first dawn of the partnership stays in shadow.
--- fact 4
Chickens spread in the wake of Austronesian-speaking voyagers across island Southeast Asia, Micronesia, Melanesia, Polynesia and Madagascar, and reached Egypt from the Middle East about 1400 BCE, becoming widely bred there by about 300 BCE.
--- fact 5
Whether chickens reached the Americas before European contact is debated: bones from Chile's Arauco Peninsula were dated as pre-Columbian and linked by DNA to Polynesian birds, but further study cast doubt on the result, like fog closing over a found shore.
--- fact 6
More than 26.5 billion chickens were alive in 2023 and more than 50 billion were produced each year for food; about 74 percent of the world's poultry meat and 68 percent of its eggs come from factory farming.
--- fact 7
A modern broiler may weigh about 1.8 kilograms at 35 days against about 1.05 kilograms for a heritage bird, and the life expectancy of commercial laying hens falls from around seven years to under two.
--- context
The chicken's early history may be tied to the fields of the [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]]; it traveled with the [[event/austronesian-expansion|Austronesian Expansion]] and the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]], and it was bred widely by the [[culture/ancient-egyptians|Ancient Egyptians]]. It belongs to the wider record of [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]]. The chicken sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and its threads run through [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes something you might pass over at breakfast: a bird first kept for reasons we cannot yet fix, whether food, ritual or sport, has become the largest vertebrate population humans have ever raised. Consider its industrial form. There you see a tool-lineage of breeding and housing that reshapes an animal within a few decades, the architecture of the shed written into the architecture of the body. Many people regard the conditions of industrial flocks as suffering, others weigh them against low food prices, and the Observer records that this disagreement is now part of the chicken's history. The reading would weaken if earlier secure chicken remains were found that changed the sequence of its domestication. What we build around an animal, we build into it.
--- question 1
When and where were chickens first domesticated, and were they kept first for food, for sport or for ritual?
--- question 2
Did chickens reach the shores of the Americas before 1492?
