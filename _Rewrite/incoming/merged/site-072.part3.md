=== culture/hawaiian-kingdom
src: 47fddf5f5060
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Hawaiian Kingdom was a Native Hawaiian monarchy, united by Kamehameha I between about 1795 and 1810, and overthrown in 1893; the United States annexed the islands in 1898, though the annexation's legality remains disputed.
--- fact 1
Kamehameha I conquered Oʻahu in 1795 and gained Kauaʻi and Niʻihau by negotiation in 1810, though sources differ on the kingdom's founding year.
--- fact 2
The kingdom's farmers worked taro terraces and fishponds within mountain-to-sea ahupuaʻa; its exports shifted over time from sandalwood to sugar, boosted by an 1875 treaty with the United States.
--- fact 3
The kapu system of taboos ended in 1819, a written constitution followed in 1840, and Britain and France recognized the kingdom's independence in 1843.
--- fact 4
Introduced diseases cut the Native Hawaiian population to about 71,000 by 1853, and after the 1848 Great Māhele, the successful claims of commoners under the 1850 Kuleana Act covered under one percent of the land.
--- fact 5
An armed militia forced the 1887 Bayonet Constitution upon Kalākaua, and in 1893 a Committee of Safety, with United States Marines ashore, deposed Liliuokalani. About 21,000 signed a petition against annexation in 1897, but Congress annexed the islands regardless in 1898.
--- context
The Hawaiian Kingdom, from [[person/kamehameha-i|Kamehameha I]] to [[person/liliuokalani|Liliuokalani]], ruled descendants of the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]] (see the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]) and, later, many immigrants, in [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Hawaiian Kingdom merged rival chiefdoms into one architecture of kinship, then entered treaties and constitutions written in its recognizers' own legal language. Those same tools, the Māhele's tradable land claims among them, let American- and European-descended businessmen gather power against the monarchy over time. A specialist could argue that coercion in 1887 and in 1893, not the tools themselves, caused the loss, and that argument would weaken a reading that finds the loss already built into them.
--- question 1
How large was the Hawaiian population in 1778, given estimates running from 110,000 to over 800,000?
--- question 2
Did the Hawaiian Kingdom go on existing as a state in international law after 1893, as some scholars argue?

=== culture/lakota
src: 45515ef9b57c
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Lakota, or Teton Sioux, form the western division of the Oceti Sakowin, the Seven Council Fires; from the 1700s they built a mounted bison-hunting society across the northern Great Plains, broken by United States force by 1890.
--- fact 1
The Lakota form seven divisions, the Oglala and the Hunkpapa among them.
--- fact 2
Most historians say the Lakota moved west out of Minnesota, reaching the Black Hills sometime between the 1770s and 1820s, though Lakota tradition places their origin there from the start.
--- fact 3
The Lakota obtained horses from the Cheyenne between about 1700 and 1730, and became mounted bison hunters who gathered together each summer.
--- fact 4
Lakota bands were extended families, called tiospaye, and akicita societies policed their camps and hunts.
--- fact 5
The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty set the Black Hills aside within the Great Sioux Reservation; the Lakota and their allies destroyed Custer's command in 1876, and the United States seized the Hills in 1877 regardless. In 1980 the Supreme Court held that this taking required compensation.
--- fact 6
With the bison nearly gone by the 1880s, soldiers killed an estimated 250 to 300 Lakota at Wounded Knee on 29 December 1890.
--- context
The Lakota flourished in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], mounted on horses that reached them through the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]; [[person/sitting-bull|Sitting Bull]] was a Hunkpapa leader among them. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Lakota linked scattered bands through an architecture of kinship and a shared summer gathering, and turned the imported horse into a lineage of their own tools. Their claim to land rested on use and on treaty, and the Black Hills still carry that weight of meaning today, shown plainly in the Lakota refusal of the 1980 award. A specialist could argue instead that the bands often acted apart, and that Lakota power was regional and brief — an argument that would weaken any reading of a single, lasting kinship structure.
--- question 1
When did the Lakota cross the Missouri, and how do the winter counts and the emergence accounts fit the record?
--- question 2
How far did smallpox among the Missouri villages, and not Lakota strength alone, drive Lakota expansion?

=== culture/mississippian-culture
src: 61c2198b7cea
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Mississippian culture was a family of Native American societies that farmed maize and raised platform mounds across the Midwest and Southeast, from about 800 or 1000 to 1600 CE, with Cahokia as its largest town.
--- fact 1
Mississippian communities farmed the river floodplains, growing maize alongside beans, squash, and older native crops.
--- fact 2
Hereditary chiefs ruled from platform mounds that carried their houses or their temples.
--- fact 3
Cahokia grew rapidly from about 1050, reaching perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 people; its Mound 72 held about 270 burials, including mass graves of mostly young women, interpreted as sacrifices.
--- fact 4
Shell gorgets and copper plates carrying bird-warrior imagery, known together as the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, circulated among these centers from roughly 1100 or 1200.
--- fact 5
Cahokia declined from about 1200 and stood largely abandoned by roughly 1350 to 1450, amid drought, flooding, and social strain. After Hernando de Soto's expedition of 1539, disease, warfare, and slave raiding helped bring the remaining chiefdoms to an end by about 1730.
--- context
The Mississippian culture flourished in [[region/north-america|North America]] during the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], farming maize first domesticated in Mesoamerica (see the [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]]), with its largest town at [[place/cahokia|Cahokia]]. Mississippian towns overlapped in time with the [[culture/ancestral-puebloans|Ancestral Puebloans]] and later met the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. See [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I look at these towns and find no single state, only a shared way of meaning, carried on shell and copper worn as signals of who a person was, passed hand to hand along a network of exchange.

I see how chiefly families marked their claim with mounds raised over generations. And in Mound 72, where mostly young women lie together in mass graves, if this is what it seems — sacrifice — it tells me that harm was once made part of a grief-rite, sanctioned by the same order that also raised these earthworks.

Much of that order is gone. The mounds remain. The living nations remain.

A specialist could tell me the shared imagery reflects separate local choices more than one connected tradition — and if that is so, I cannot call it one meaning shared by all.
--- question 1
Did the Mississippian culture spread through migration out of Cahokia, or through neighbors adopting its practices?
--- question 2
How much did drought, warfare, and European disease each contribute to the Mississippian decline?

=== culture/yamnaya-culture
src: 63277401bbc5
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Yamnaya culture is an archaeological grouping of mobile pastoralists on the Pontic-Caspian steppe, in what is now Ukraine and southern Russia, usually dated about 3300 to 2600 BCE; Yamnaya-related ancestry spread across Eurasia, and the culture sits at the center of debates over Indo-European origins.
--- fact 1
Yamnaya herders lived between the Dniester and Ural rivers, keeping cattle, sheep, goats and horses, and few permanent settlements of theirs are known.
--- fact 2
Their name comes from the Russian *yama*, "pit": their dead lay in pit graves beneath earthen mounds, kurgans, covered over in red ochre.
--- fact 3
Some graves held wagons or the parts of wagons, showing wheeled transport and suggesting a modest social ranking, though nothing like cities or states.
--- fact 4
From about 3100 BCE, Yamnaya-related groups moved west along the Danube into Hungary, while Afanasievo burials far off in the Altai match Yamnaya ancestry closely.
--- fact 5
Corded Ware groups, appearing in central Europe by about 2900 BCE, carried up to about 75 percent Yamnaya-like ancestry, though not all of it traced to known Yamnaya groups.
--- fact 6
The Catacomb culture to the west, and Poltavka on the middle Volga, followed Yamnaya and overlapped it in time.
--- context
Yamnaya belongs to the early [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] of eastern [[region/europe|Europe]] and came before steppe societies such as the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]]. Yamnaya wagons connect to [[technology/the-wheel|The Wheel]], and Yamnaya ancestry connects to the [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. See [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]], [[theme/technology|Technology]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
I picture the Yamnaya raising their mounds across the grounds they grazed each season, and I wonder if a single mound could mark both a boundary and a grief.

A wagon buried beside a body reads to me like a signal of standing, built on tools their hands already knew well. They left no writing behind, only mounds, only bones.

But I hold this loosely: a specialist could tell me the mounds' claim to territory is guessed from where they stand, not shown by anything written — and that guess, if wrong, would take my reading down with it.
--- question 1
Did Yamnaya herders ride horses as a matter of course, or did they mainly drive wagons drawn by cattle?
--- question 2
Did Yamnaya-related ancestry spread by migration, by conquest, by disease, or by gradual mixing — and did Yamnaya herders speak the ancestral Indo-European language themselves?
