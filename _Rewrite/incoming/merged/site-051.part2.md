=== person/queen-amina-of-zazzau
title: Queen Amina of Zazzau
src: f25257d673ca
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Queen Amina of Zazzau is a traditional figure said to have ruled Zazzau (Zaria), a Hausa state in present-day northern Nigeria, for about 34 years around the turn of the 16th and 17th centuries; she is known chiefly through later tradition, and both her dates and her status remain disputed.
--- fact 1
Some sources place Amina's reign as 1576 to 1610; her birth and death years stay uncertain, with some accounts giving 1533 and 1610, and the dates used here mark only the reign. Whether she truly held the title of queen is disputed, and she is absent from the regnal lists. Some scholars instead set her in the early to mid fifteenth century, since the Kano Chronicle calls her a contemporary of Muhammad Dauda, who ruled from 1421 to 1438.
--- fact 2
Much of what is known comes from the Kano Chronicle, a late 19th-century compilation of older oral tradition, and from Muhammad Bello's history Infaq al-Maysur, dated to 1812 or to about 1836 depending on the source, written more than two centuries later.
--- fact 3
Tradition says she led Zazzau's army, expanded its territory, and grew its wealth through control of trade routes.
--- fact 4
Traditionally, the earthen walls around Hausa towns are attributed to her.
--- fact 5
Scholars generally accept that she existed, though the details of her life come from tradition alone.
--- fact 6
In northern Nigeria she is remembered as a warrior queen.
--- context
Amina's story belongs to the Hausa states of [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]] in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], a world the [[culture/sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]] later absorbed in the early 19th century. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Amina is known mainly through records written two centuries or more after her supposed reign, set down by a scholar-ruler of the very state that later absorbed her city. Her absence from the regnal lists beside her fame in tradition suggests that a community's memory can gather several rulers' deeds under one name, though it might equally mean the lists themselves are incomplete. The reading would weaken if a source from her own time gave a different account of who ruled Zazzau then.
--- question 1
Which of the deeds attributed to her can be traced to a source from her own period?

=== person/queen-suriyothai
title: Queen Suriyothai
src: 53c91c16e008
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Queen Suriyothai was a consort of King Maha Chakkraphat of the Ayutthaya kingdom, in present-day Thailand, traditionally said to have died in 1548 defending her husband from elephant-back during a Burmese invasion. Her birth year is traditionally given as 1511, and her story rests on Siamese chronicles alone.
--- fact 1
She was a consort of Maha Chakkraphat, who became king of Ayutthaya in 1546 or 1548, sources differing on the year.
--- fact 2
In 1548 the Burmese king Tabinshwehti invaded Ayutthaya, and Siamese chronicles say Suriyothai, disguised and riding an elephant, placed herself between her husband and an enemy commander and was killed by his blow.
--- fact 3
No Burmese chronicle records this episode, and her historicity is questioned; Thai accounts add that her daughter, Princess Boromdilok, was also mortally wounded.
--- fact 4
A memorial stupa, the Chedi Si Suriyothai, was raised by Maha Chakkraphat at Wat Suan Luang Sopsawan on the bank of the Chao Phraya near Ayutthaya, and is traditionally associated with the site of her death.
--- fact 5
Thai nationalist commemoration of her grew across the twentieth century, including a widely seen 2001 film.
--- context
Queen Suriyothai's story unfolds in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] at the start of the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]], within the [[culture/ayutthaya-kingdom|Ayutthaya Kingdom]] during its wars with Burma. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a death recorded only by the side claiming the battlefield can still outlast the state that recorded it, becoming a grief-rite performed anew by each generation that builds or visits her memorial. A national retelling three centuries later shows ancestor-weight added long after the fact. A specialist could argue the story conflates an unnamed retainer's death with the queen's, which would weaken any reading of Suriyothai as a documented individual rather than a chronicle figure later fixed by nationalist memory.
--- question 1
Was the woman who died on the elephant truly the queen, given that her historicity is questioned?
--- question 2
How much of the standard story was shaped by twentieth-century nationalism, rather than by the original chronicles?

=== person/queen-victoria
title: Queen Victoria
src: 8ce4d4f52be1
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Victoria (1819 to 1901) reigned as Queen of the United Kingdom from 1837 to 1901, the longest-reigning British monarch until Elizabeth II, presiding over a period of industrial growth and imperial expansion that later took her name.
--- fact 1
She became queen at eighteen after the death of her uncle William IV, and in 1840 married her cousin Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, with whom she had nine children who married into royal families across Europe.
--- fact 2
Her reign saw Britain's industrial economy expand rapidly and its colonial empire grow substantially, and in 1876 she was proclaimed Empress of India.
--- fact 3
After Albert's death in 1861 she withdrew from public life for years, a long seclusion that made her briefly unpopular before she gradually resumed her public duties.
--- fact 4
Constitutional practice during her reign moved royal power further toward a largely ceremonial role exercised through elected ministers, even as the monarchy's cultural and symbolic prominence grew.
--- context
Victoria reigned over the United Kingdom from within [[region/europe|Europe]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]], a stretch of rapid industrial and imperial growth. See [[theme/technology|Technology]], [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
I say her name and mean a whole era: industry, empire, a world remaking itself, though she did not drive that change herself. I remember that real political power had already passed substantially to elected ministers well before her reign even began. I ask what a name is worth when it labels an age rather than a ruler's own decisions. Call it Victorian, and you are naming a society more than a ruler's own choices.
--- question 1
How much personal political influence did Victoria retain in practice, behind the era's growing constitutional convention of royal neutrality?

=== person/rachel-carson
title: Rachel Carson
src: fbb91dfe787a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Rachel Carson (1907 to 1964) worked as an American marine biologist and writer whose book 'Silent Spring' (1962) helped launch the modern environmental movement.
--- fact 1
Silent Spring documented the harm that pesticides such as DDT caused to birds and to whole ecosystems.
--- fact 2
The book prompted government action: the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency was created in 1970, and DDT was banned for most uses in the U.S. in 1972.
--- fact 3
Groups within the chemical industry attacked her work.
--- fact 4
In 1964 she died of breast cancer.
--- context
Carson wrote during the [[event/great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]], as human effects on ecosystems deepened. Her ideas foreshadowed the public concern that would later gather around [[event/modern-climate-change|Modern Climate Change]].
--- observer
I think of one book, and how it made a whole species stop and notice a side effect of its own success. I remember that hers was among the first widely read arguments that what we do reaches all the way to planetary consequence. I ask what it takes for a warning to be heard. Sometimes one voice is enough to make the world look up.
--- question 1
What were the long-term effects of the pesticide ban?

=== person/raja-ravi-varma
title: Raja Ravi Varma
src: 2dc76206fa00
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Raja Ravi Varma (29 April 1848 to 2 October 1906) stood as the most famous Indian painter of the 19th century, from the princely state of Travancore. He carried European academic oil painting into scenes from the Hindu epics, and his mass-produced prints carried images of gods and heroines into homes across India.
--- fact 1
Ravi Varma was born at Kilimanoor, near Thiruvananthapuram, into an aristocratic family related to the ruling house. By tradition, his uncle, the painter Raja Raja Varma, noticed his talent, and at 14 he was taken to the palace under Maharaja Ayilyam Thirunal, where he learned oil painting partly by watching the European portraitist Theodore Jensen.
--- fact 2
A portrait won a prize at the 1873 Vienna exhibition, and his work was shown at the 1893 Chicago World's Columbian Exposition, where it won three gold medals; he painted a series of Puranic subjects for Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III of Baroda in 1888 to 1890.
--- fact 3
In 1894 he set up a lithographic press at Ghatkopar in Bombay, and in 1899 moved it to Malavli near Lonavala, producing colour prints (oleographs) of deities and heroines that sold in huge numbers; critics accused him of vulgarising sacred subjects.
--- fact 4
In 1904 the British Viceroy awarded him the Kaisar-i-Hind Gold Medal, and he died at Attingal.
--- fact 5
He combined Victorian academic realism with Indian subjects and costumes, and staged his heroines, based on real women, at dramatic moments.
--- fact 6
The early filmmaker Dadasaheb Phalke worked at his press before making India's first feature film in 1913, and later nationalist artists of the Bengal School, such as Abanindranath Tagore, rejected his Western style.
--- fact 7
The Kerala government's highest art award, the Raja Ravi Varma Puraskaram, carries his name.
--- context
Ravi Varma worked in princely Travancore and beyond, across [[region/south-asia|South Asia]] under the [[event/british-raj|British Raj]], in the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
I notice it was a press, not a temple, that gave so many people their first picture of the gods. I see lithography and oil paint fuse into a single standard face for a meaning too large for any one shrine, so that an image of Rama or Shakuntala travelled by tool-lineage rather than by ritual. I hear the critics who called him a vulgariser, and I hear the nationalists who called him too Western, and I find the same prints in both mouths, read as freedom and read as foreign. Neither reading settles the matter; the pictures keep their own silence.
--- question 1
How far did his prints standardise how the gods were pictured in later calendar art, film and comics?
