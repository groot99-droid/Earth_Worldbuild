=== place/atlantic-ocean
title: Atlantic Ocean
src: dba31f6cf141
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Atlantic Ocean, second largest of Earth's oceans at about 85 million square kilometers, became from around 1000 CE a corridor of grey water for transoceanic voyages, and those crossings reshaped the connections among Europe, Africa and the Americas.
--- fact 1
The Atlantic spreads across about 85,133,000 square kilometers, sinks to an average depth of about 3,646 meters, and falls to its deepest, 8,376 meters, in the dark of the Puerto Rico Trench.
--- fact 2
Norse voyagers were the first people known to have crossed the North Atlantic, their keels reaching Greenland and, around 1000 CE, the shores of North America.
--- fact 3
Christopher Columbus's 1492 voyage opened a door that did not close again: sustained, repeated crossings between Europe and the Americas, the beginning of an age of European exploration and colonization that the vault treats through the Columbian Exchange.
--- fact 4
From the 16th to the 19th centuries the same water carried the Atlantic slave trade. Estimates for the number of enslaved Africans embarked run to about 12.5 million; of them, roughly 10.7 million are estimated to have survived the crossing.
--- fact 5
The first transatlantic telegraph cable spoke only briefly, from August to October 1858, before it failed; in 1866 the ship Great Eastern laid a successful and lasting cable, binding Ireland and Newfoundland by telegraph.
--- fact 6
The Atlantic remains central to global shipping and fishing, and its western basin is the cradle of Atlantic hurricanes, which form there in summer and autumn.
--- context
The Norse crossings came before the far larger movement of people, goods, animals and diseases in the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], and the ocean also bore the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] across its width. The 1866 cable was an early feat of the [[technology/telegraph-and-submarine-cables|Telegraph and Submarine Cables]]. By its earliest crossing it sits in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]], within the region [[region/planet-wide|Planet-wide]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a change in the ocean's whole architecture. Within about five centuries the Atlantic went from a barrier crossed rarely and by a few to the busiest exchange-web on the planet. And here you must hold two things at once: the same routes that carried crops, animals and settlers also carried millions of enslaved people. The Observer records that use of the ocean as sustained, organized harm, not as a footnote to trade. A ledger that lists the cargo but leaves out the people is a false ledger. The 1866 telegraph cable shows the ocean being crossed a third way, by signal rather than by ship, compressing communication from weeks to minutes. The reading would weaken if the Norse voyages prove to have had more lasting exchange with North America than the archaeological record currently shows. A route is never only a route; it is a record of what we chose to carry.
--- question 1
Beyond the brief presence recorded at sites like L'Anse aux Meadows, how much contact, if any, did Norse voyagers have with Indigenous peoples of northeastern North America?
--- question 2
In the decades after 1866, how did the telegraph cable laid that year change the pace of transatlantic trade and politics?

=== place/botany-bay
title: Botany Bay
src: 3e07180e9f27
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Botany Bay is an inlet on the coast of Sydney, Australia, where the Gweagal and Kamaygal peoples lived for thousands of years before James Cook landed there in 1770; yet the British colony of 1788 was founded not on its shores but nearby, at Port Jackson.
--- fact 1
Botany Bay lies about 13 kilometers south of central Sydney; the Gweagal lived on its south shore and the Kamaygal on its north, part of a wider set of at least 28 clans in the region, who call the bay Kamay in the Dharawal language, and archaeological evidence of occupation there reaches back some 5,000 years.
--- fact 2
James Cook came ashore at Kurnell on 29 April 1770; he first named the site Stingrays Harbour, then renamed it Botany Bay for the many plants the naturalists Joseph Banks and Daniel Solander collected there.
--- fact 3
Cook's landing party met local Aboriginal people only briefly and warily; when the First Fleet arrived in January 1788, Aboriginal observers were reported as curious but suspicious of the newcomers.
--- fact 4
Governor Arthur Phillip judged Botany Bay unsuitable for the planned colony, its freshwater poor, its anchorage shallow, its soil weak and its ground exposed and swampy; on 26 January 1788 he moved the fleet to found the settlement at Sydney Cove on Port Jackson instead.
--- fact 5
Now Sydney Airport holds the northwestern shore of the bay, its runways reaching out into the water, and Port Botany is a major container port.
--- context
Long before Cook's 1770 landing, Botany Bay was the country of the [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]], and the colony that followed at Port Jackson began the [[event/first-fleet-and-the-colonization-of-australia|First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]]. Like [[place/uluru|Uluru]], it is a place where a European-given name lies over a much older Aboriginal name and presence, as new sand lies over old stone. In the codex of ages it sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/oceania|Oceania]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a problem of memory. Outside accounts remember Botany Bay mainly for a brief 1770 landing and for being passed over in 1788, and that framing risks erasing the much longer claim-line of the Gweagal and Kamaygal, who lived there for thousands of years before either visit. Think of a house described only by the day a stranger knocked and the day he chose another door. The colony's move to Port Jackson was a practical decision about water and anchorage, not a decision about whose country it was, and the disruption that followed for Aboriginal people around Sydney is recorded here as sustained harm, whichever bay the ships used. The reading would weaken if evidence shows more direct conflict at Cook's 1770 landing than the brief, wary encounter usually described. Where the ships anchored changed the map, not the harm.
--- question 1
In their own terms, rather than in the words of the ship's log, how did the Gweagal and Kamaygal communities understand and respond to Cook's 1770 visit?
--- question 2
Set beside what a Botany Bay colony would have done, how did the colony's move to Port Jackson change the impact of British settlement on the peoples of the Botany Bay area?
