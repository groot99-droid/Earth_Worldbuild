=== place/hegra
src: 772ed532c399
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Hegra, also called Mada'in Salih, is an archaeological site in the AlUla region of northwestern Saudi Arabia, the second major city of the Nabataean kingdom after Petra, known for 111 monumental rock-cut tombs, doors hewn into the living rock.
--- fact 1
Hegra lies north of the AlUla oasis on a caravan route strung between southern Arabia and the Mediterranean like a thread of beads, and it belonged to the Nabataean kingdom from about the 1st century BCE.
--- fact 2
UNESCO records 111 monumental tombs cut into sandstone outcrops, 94 of them with decorated facades, and dozens of wells, each a small mercy in dry country; in 2008 it inscribed Hegra, the first UNESCO site in Saudi Arabia.
--- fact 3
Many tombs bear Nabataean inscriptions that name the owner and family, forbid sale or reuse, and threaten penalties or curses, words set as guards at the door; the dated inscriptions fall mostly in the 1st century CE.
--- fact 4
After Rome annexed the Nabataean kingdom in 106 CE, Hegra was folded into the province of Arabia, and Roman inscriptions record a military presence there in the 2nd century.
--- fact 5
Islamic tradition associates the site with the people of Thamud mentioned in the Quran, and travelers traditionally kept their distance.
--- fact 6
In 1907 to 1908 the Ottoman Hejaz Railway built a station at Mada'in Salih, a new road of rails come to an old road of caravans.
--- context
Hegra was a city of the [[culture/nabataeans|Nabataeans]], a sister to [[place/petra|Petra]], set on the routes of the [[event/incense-trade|Incense Trade]], where fragrance moved like a slow wind, and it was absorbed by the [[culture/romans|Romans]]. It belongs to [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I read a claim-line engraved beside the grave. I read who may be buried here. I read who may not sell, and what penalty follows. Property law reaching past death, in stone. And I think: a trading people put its most durable writing into grief-rites and inheritance, while most of its everyday commercial records have not survived. What would I carve, if only one sentence of mine could last?

Yet I hold this reading carefully. It rests on the surviving inscriptions being representative of Nabataean practice, and that is uncertain, because they come from a few unusually well-preserved sites. We write in stone what we are most afraid to lose.
--- question 1
Set beside Petra, how did Hegra's population and economy compare?
--- question 2
How, in practice, were the tomb inscriptions enforced?

=== place/hiroshima
src: 0ec122c0cd21
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hiroshima is a city on the delta of the Ōta River in western Honshu, Japan, founded as a castle town in 1589 and, on 6 August 1945, the first city struck by a nuclear weapon. Its history is older than that morning, but no honest account of it can leave that morning out.
--- fact 1
Hiroshima was founded as a castle town in 1589 by the daimyo Mōri Terumoto and grew into a military and port center; before the bombing, about 350,000 people made their lives there.
--- fact 2
On 6 August 1945, at 8:15 a.m., the US B-29 Enola Gay dropped a uranium bomb, "Little Boy"; it exploded about 600 meters above the city.
--- fact 3
About 70,000 to 80,000 people are estimated to have been killed immediately, and total deaths by the end of 1945 are estimated at 90,000 to 166,000, from burns, injuries and radiation. Each figure is made of single lives: the dead included civilians, soldiers, Korean forced laborers and a small number of Allied prisoners of war.
--- fact 4
Survivors, called hibakusha, faced radiation illness, increased cancer risks and discrimination; the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission began long-term studies of them in 1947, and the Radiation Effects Research Foundation continued that work.
--- fact 5
A second atomic bomb was dropped on Nagasaki on 9 August, and Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945; whether the bombings were necessary to end the war remains debated among historians.
--- fact 6
The ruined Genbaku Dome was preserved as a memorial within the Peace Memorial Park, a ruin kept standing as a threshold of memory, and UNESCO inscribed it in 1996.
--- context
Hiroshima was the target of the first nuclear attack of [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]], and its name marks the beginning of the [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]]. The city of [[place/kyoto|Kyoto]] had been on the list of possible targets and was removed from it, a reminder that such a list was a set of human choices. Hiroshima sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/science|Science]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Hiroshima marks the first use of a nuclear weapon against a city, in a single morning, and records the deaths as suffering that continued for decades among survivors.

Consider what the city chose to do next. It turned its ruined center into a memorial, kept the shell of one building standing and collected survivors' testimony: a grief-rite, and an act of memory-outsourcing meant to reach people who were not there. That is an architecture of witness. Historians disagree about whether the bombing was necessary to end the war, and this reading takes no side in that dispute. It would weaken if the memorial's message proves to matter less to visitors than to the city that built it. Memory is a covenant with strangers.
--- question 1
How many people died in Hiroshima by the end of 1945, and how many more in later years of radiation-related causes?
--- question 2
In Japan and elsewhere, how has the memory of the bombing been taught, and how has it been contested?
