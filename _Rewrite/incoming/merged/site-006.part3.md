=== place/jamestown
title: Jamestown
src: 97af00030c9e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jamestown, on the James River in Virginia, USA, was founded in 1607 as the first permanent English settlement in North America, and it remained the capital of the Virginia colony until 1699. Read it as a threshold, and read it whole.
--- fact 1
Notice whose land it was: Jamestown was founded in May 1607 by about 105 English men and boys sent by the Virginia Company of London, within a paramount chiefdom of many Algonquian-speaking communities led by Wahunsenacawh, whom the English called Powhatan.
--- fact 2
In the winter of 1609 to 1610, remembered as the Starving Time, hunger and disease reduced the colony from a few hundred people (commonly given as about 500) to about 60, and both written accounts and archaeological evidence record cannibalism; the ledger states it plainly, and so should we.
--- fact 3
John Rolfe's tobacco, first harvested successfully in 1614, gave the colony an export crop, and with it a new architecture of demand: the colonists wanted more land and more labor.
--- fact 4
Hold both halves of 1619 together: in July the first representative assembly in English America met at Jamestown, and in late August an English privateer, the White Lion, landed about 20 Africans at Point Comfort, downriver, who had been taken from a Portuguese slave ship; they were traded for food, some were brought to Jamestown, and their legal status is debated.
--- fact 5
A Powhatan attack on 22 March 1622 killed more than 300 colonists, about a third of the English population, and years of war followed; Powhatan deaths from war and introduced disease are poorly recorded, a silence in the ledger that is itself part of the record.
--- fact 6
Jamestown burned in 1676 during Bacon's Rebellion; after the statehouse burned in 1698, the capital moved in 1699 to Middle Plantation, renamed Williamsburg.
--- fact 7
In 1996 archaeologists found the remains of the 1607 fort, long thought lost to river erosion: what we assume is gone may only be buried.
--- context
Think of Jamestown as an outpost of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]] in North America, a two-way ledger: tobacco went out to Europe, and people, animals and diseases came in across the Atlantic. The arrival of 1619 belongs to the history of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. The settlement sits in the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/north-america|North America]], within the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that two claims to the same land met here: an English company's charter and a Powhatan chiefdom's territory. The settlement survived only after it found a crop that turned land and labor into exported wealth, and you should notice how survival became an architecture of extraction.

The year 1619 is remembered for a representative assembly and for the arrival of enslaved Africans. The Observer holds that both belong in one account, the covenant and the cost on the same page, with the deaths of colonists, Powhatan people and Africans recorded as suffering. This reading would weaken if new records show that Virginia's system of hereditary slavery grew from later laws with little connection to the 1619 arrivals. A history told in halves is a history told wrong.
--- question 1
What legal status did the Africans landed in 1619 hold, and how did Virginia's law of slavery take shape afterward?
--- question 2
How many Powhatan people died from war and introduced disease in the colony's first decades, deaths the record barely kept?

=== place/jerusalem
title: Jerusalem
src: 3a4a9a5e0ee9
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Jerusalem is a city in the Judean hills of the southern Levant, settled since about the 4th millennium BCE and holy to Jews, Christians and Muslims, a hill toward which many roads of faith run like water to a well. Its political status is disputed today.
--- fact 1
Settlement on the spur above the Gihon spring reaches back to about the 4th millennium BCE, and the city's name surfaces like a stone in a streambed in Egyptian texts of the early 2nd millennium BCE and in the Amarna letters of the 14th century BCE.
--- fact 2
According to the Hebrew Bible, King David captured the city about 1000 BCE and his son Solomon built the First Temple; archaeologists debate how large Jerusalem was in the 10th century BCE, and by the 8th century BCE the city had grown markedly, as a spring swells into a pool.
--- fact 3
In 586 BCE Babylonian forces destroyed Jerusalem and the First Temple and deported part of its population; in 516 BCE the Second Temple was dedicated.
--- fact 4
In 70 CE Roman forces destroyed the city and the Second Temple; after the Bar Kokhba revolt (132 to 135 CE) the Romans refounded it as Aelia Capitolina, a new name laid over the old, and barred Jews from entering.
--- fact 5
In the 4th century, under Constantine, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre rose; Muslim forces took the city in 637 to 638, and the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik completed the Dome of the Rock in 691 to 692.
--- fact 6
Crusaders captured Jerusalem in 1099 and killed many of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, and estimates of the dead vary widely; Saladin retook the city in 1187, and from 1517 to 1917 it lay under Ottoman rule.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed the Old City of Jerusalem and its walls in 1981. In 1948 the city was divided between Israel and Jordan; since 1967 it has been under Israeli control, and its status remains disputed between Israelis and Palestinians.
--- context
Jerusalem's story is bound to [[place/babylon|Babylon]] by the destruction of 586 BCE, and to the [[culture/romans|Romans]], the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], the [[event/crusades|Crusades]] and the [[culture/ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] by the conquests that came after, wave upon wave against one hill. Its earliest settlement sets it in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], its region is [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that three meaning-engines, Jewish, Christian and Muslim, locate some of their most significant sites here, within a few hundred meters of one another. Think about what that does to a place: one spot can hold the claim-lines of several communities, and the builders of each period have reused or replaced the structures of the last. The architecture of the city is a ledger written over itself.

Its long record of sieges, destructions and massacres is recorded here as suffering, whichever community was its victim at the time; we do not rank the grief. This reading would weaken if the sanctity of particular sites proves to have been less continuous over time than each tradition holds. Where many hold a place sacred, every stone carries more than one memory.
--- question 1
How large a city was Jerusalem in the 10th century BCE?
--- question 2
How did Jerusalem's people rise and fall in number as ruler followed ruler?
