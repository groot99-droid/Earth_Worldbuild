=== place/aksum
src: ea8c5064f198
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Aksum is a town in the Tigray region of northern Ethiopia that was once the capital of the Kingdom of Aksum, a trading state active from about the 1st century CE to the 7th century and later. It is known for its stone stelae standing like spears against the sky, for its coinage, and for its early adoption of Christianity.
--- fact 1
Aksum lies in the highlands of northern Ethiopia, and the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek trade guide of about the 1st century CE, names Aksum and its port Adulis on the Red Sea, two lights on a long shore.
--- fact 2
Aksumite rulers minted gold, silver and bronze coins from about the late 3rd century CE, and King Ezana adopted Christianity around 330 CE; the coins struck afterward bear the cross, small suns carrying a new sign.
--- fact 3
Ezana's inscriptions were written in three tongues: Ge'ez, Sabaean and Greek.
--- fact 4
Aksum's monolithic stelae mark burial sites; the largest, about 33 meters high, fell and broke like a felled giant, and a 24-meter stele taken to Rome by Italian forces in 1937 was returned in 2005 and raised again in 2008.
--- fact 5
Ethiopian Christian tradition holds that the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion at Aksum houses the Ark of the Covenant; the claim cannot be examined, a door that stays shut.
--- fact 6
Aksum declined from the 7th century, and the proposed causes include changes in Red Sea trade after the rise of Islam, environmental stress and internal conflict; the causes are debated, tracks crossing on windblown ground.
--- fact 7
UNESCO inscribed Aksum in 1980, setting its name in a long roll of remembered places.
--- context
Aksum was the capital of the [[culture/kingdom-of-aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]], which had contacts with the [[culture/kingdom-of-saba|Kingdom of Saba]] across the Red Sea; its stele was carried off during the Italian occupation, in the time of the [[event/second-italo-ethiopian-war|Second Italo-Ethiopian War]]. Later Ethiopian rulers built the [[place/rock-hewn-churches-of-lalibela|Rock-Hewn Churches of Lalibela]]. Aksum sits in [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and the region [[region/sub-saharan-africa|Sub-Saharan Africa]], and its threads run through the themes of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]], [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]] and [[theme/art-and-ritual|Art and Ritual]].
--- observer
I see a state announcing its meaning-engine in stone and in metal. I see giant stelae standing over graves. I see coins that carried a cross after Ezana's conversion, small portable things that spread the ruler's story-glue across the trading network, hand to hand. And I see a stele taken and a stele returned, and I learn that a monument, once moved, becomes a claim-line of another kind. What does a stone mean when it travels?

The Ark tradition is a belief held by a community, and it cannot be tested. I record it as belief. I neither endorse it nor dismiss it. If the coin evidence proves too sparse to date Ezana's change of religion, this reading weakens. What a people holds sacred, I hold carefully.
--- question 1
Why did Aksum's star begin to fall from the 7th century?
--- question 2
How many people filled Aksum at its height?

=== place/arkaim
src: cc4691cfcf24
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Arkaim is a fortified Bronze Age settlement of the Sintashta culture in Chelyabinsk Oblast, in the southern Urals of Russia, a ring of walls on the open land, occupied from about 2000 to 1700 BCE.
--- fact 1
Archaeologists identified Arkaim in 1987 during surveys ahead of a planned reservoir; the waters never came, for the reservoir was not built, and the area became a protected reserve.
--- fact 2
Arkaim was laid out on a roughly circular plan, on the order of 150 meters across, with concentric walls of earth, timber and clay wrapped around houses set in a ring, like ripples frozen around a dropped stone.
--- fact 3
Arkaim shows evidence of copper and bronze metalworking, the forge's glow on the steppe, and sites of the same culture in the region are associated with graves holding horses and the earliest known spoke-wheeled chariots.
--- fact 4
Arkaim appears to have been burned and abandoned around 1700 BCE, its ring left to ash and wind; whether by attack, by accident or by deliberate burning on departure is debated.
--- fact 5
Since the 1990s Arkaim has become a tourist and pilgrimage destination, and stories gather around it like moths around a lamp: popular claims that it was an "Aryan" homeland or proto-city, claims that professional archaeologists do not accept as established.
--- context
Arkaim is one of the fortified sites of the [[culture/sintashta-culture|Sintashta Culture]], whose chariot-using elites are often linked, though not conclusively, to early Indo-Iranian speakers, as discussed under [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]]. It sits in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/central-asian-steppe|Central Asian Steppe]], and its threads run through the themes of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
Imagine trying to know a people who left you no writing. The Observer notes that Arkaim, its bronze workshops and the horse-and-chariot graves of its region come from a people with no known mark-code, so everything about them is inferred from soil, metal and bone. Their concentric walls are an early durable claim-line on the steppe, and their pairing with elite chariot burials suggests that sanctioned harm was entangled with status-signals. Then look at what came after: the site is now used to support identity stories about ancestry. A silent place invites story-glue; where the dead cannot speak, we speak for them. The reading would weaken if the settlement proves to have been mainly ceremonial or economic rather than defensive.
--- question 1
Was Arkaim's ring raised mainly for defense, for ceremony, or for the forge?
--- question 2
Why was Arkaim burned and abandoned around 1700 BCE, its ring left empty?
