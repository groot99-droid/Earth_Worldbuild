=== place/hattusa
src: d1a65722d4a1
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Hattusa was the capital of the Hittite Empire, at Boğazkale in central Turkey, made the royal seat in about 1650 BCE and abandoned around 1200 BCE, and it preserved an archive of about 30,000 clay tablets, a memory of clay that outlasted its kings.
--- fact 1
Hattusa lies at Boğazkale in Çorum Province, Turkey; a Hattian settlement stood there before it, and in about 1650 BCE King Hattusili I raised it to be the Hittite capital.
--- fact 2
Hattusa was ringed by walls pierced by monumental gates, the Lion Gate and the Sphinx Gate among them, and it held a Great Temple, with a rock sanctuary nearby at Yazılıkaya where the stone itself was made holy.
--- fact 3
Under Muwatalli II the capital flew briefly to Tarhuntassa, like a bird leaving its nest, and under Mursili III it came home to Hattusa.
--- fact 4
Excavators, beginning with Hugo Winckler in 1906, drew about 30,000 clay tablets from the royal archives, written in Hittite, Akkadian, Luwian, Hurrian and other tongues and holding treaties, rituals and letters, among them a version of the treaty with Egypt made after the Battle of Kadesh.
--- fact 5
In 1915 Bedřich Hrozný identified Hittite as an Indo-European language, a lost cousin recognized at last among the family.
--- fact 6
Around 1200 BCE, during the Bronze Age Collapse, Hattusa was abandoned; its central buildings burned, though whether the city was emptied before or after the fire is debated.
--- fact 7
In 1986 UNESCO inscribed Hattusha.
--- context
Hattusa was the capital of the [[culture/hittites|Hittites]], whose army fought at the [[event/battle-of-kadesh|Battle of Kadesh]] and whose empire went dark in the [[event/bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]]. The Hittite language kept in its archive is central to the study of the [[event/indo-european-language-spread|Indo-European Language Spread]], a keystone in that arch. Hattusa belongs to the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]] and the region [[region/levant-and-anatolia|Levant and Anatolia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]] and [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Hattusa is a case of memory-outsourcing at imperial scale: a state kept its treaties, rituals and letters on clay in several languages. Here is the paradox worth sitting with. The archive survived in part because fire hardens clay, so the destruction of the city helped preserve its records; the ruin became the librarian. Treaties written in the diplomatic language of the day, Akkadian, show rulers of rival powers binding one another with story-glue in the form of oaths sworn before gods, a covenant made of words. Remember, too, that the archive records what the palace chose to keep. The reading would weaken if the tablets prove to reflect chance survival more than the state's actual priorities. What we keep is a portrait of what we valued, until chance paints over it.
--- question 1
Why was Hattusa abandoned, and did the fire come before the city was left, or after?
--- question 2
At its height, how large was the population of Hattusa?

=== place/havana
src: 2bc5c3a8d48a
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Havana is the capital of Cuba, on the island's northwest coast, established on its present harbor site in 1519 and for a long age the assembly point where Spanish treasure fleets gathered, like birds before a migration, to cross the Atlantic.
--- fact 1
Havana was founded as San Cristóbal de la Habana; the settlement moved twice, like a bird choosing its branch, before it was established on its present harbor site in 1519.
--- fact 2
From the 1560s Spanish fleets heavy with silver from Mexico and Peru assembled at Havana for the crossing to Spain, and the city armored itself with La Real Fuerza (built 1558 to 1577) and El Morro (built 1589 to 1630).
--- fact 3
Britain captured Havana after a siege in 1762, during the Seven Years' War, and returned it to Spain in 1763 in exchange for Florida; Spain then built the La Cabaña fortress, a new shield over the harbor.
--- fact 4
From the late 18th century sugar and coffee plantations, worked by enslaved people, expanded Cuba's economy; hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans were brought to Cuba, many through Havana, and slavery there was abolished in 1886.
--- fact 5
On 15 February 1898 the US battleship Maine exploded in Havana harbor, killing about 260 crew, an event that helped lead to the Spanish-American War.
--- fact 6
Since 1959 Havana has been the capital of a socialist state, and in 1962 the Cuban Missile Crisis set Cuba at the center of the Cold War, the eye of a gathering storm.
--- fact 7
In 1982 UNESCO inscribed Old Havana and its fortifications.
--- context
Havana was a key port for the silver that flowed from [[place/potosi|Potosi]] and the mainland, for the [[event/sugar-plantation-complex|Sugar Plantation Complex]] and for the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]], and it changed hands during the [[event/seven-years-war|Seven Years' War]]. Later it stood as a focal point of the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. Havana belongs to the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Havana grew because a state needed a fixed place to gather and guard a moving stream of wealth. Think of a river that must pause in a basin before it can go on: silver had to be assembled somewhere before the crossing, and the fortifications followed the cargo. Its later wealth from sugar rested on enslaved labor, which the Observer records as suffering, borne by people, and not only as an economic input. The waterfront was claimed in turn by the leading naval power of each era, and a historian could challenge this reading by showing that local Creole elites and enslaved people shaped the city as much as outside powers did. Walls tell you who guarded a place; they do not tell you who made it.
