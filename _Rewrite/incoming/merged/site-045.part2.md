=== person/al-khwarizmi
title: Al-Khwarizmi
type: person
date: c. 780 – 850 CE
themes: science, language
src: 604b980a7d5a
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (about 780 to 850 CE) was a Persian scholar at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, and his work on algebra and Hindu-Arabic numerals shaped mathematics.
--- fact 1
His book on 'al-jabr' (restoration) gave algebra its name.
--- fact 2
The word 'algorithm' derives from the Latinized form of his name.
--- fact 3
His work helped carry Hindu-Arabic numerals and place-value arithmetic to the wider world.
--- fact 4
He also compiled astronomical tables and geographical data.
--- context
He worked within the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] in [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]]. His texts later influenced the [[event/scientific-revolution|Scientific Revolution]].
--- observer
I trace a chain: Indian numerals, then Persian and Arabic scholarship, then Latin translation. I see how knowledge moved — not on its own, but carried by translators, carried by empire. A number is never just a number; it is a hand that passed it on.
--- question 1
How original was his work relative to earlier traditions?

=== person/alan-turing
title: Alan Turing
type: person
date: 1912 – 1954
themes: science, technology, war
src: 6f331da5d1ee
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Alan Turing (1912 to 1954), a British mathematician, formalized the concept of computation, helped break German ciphers during World War II, and proposed a test for machine intelligence.
--- fact 1
In 1936 he described the abstract 'Turing machine,' a foundation of computer science.
--- fact 2
At Bletchley Park he worked to break the Enigma cipher.
--- fact 3
In 1950 he published a paper proposing what became known as the Turing test.
--- fact 4
He was prosecuted in 1952 for homosexuality, then a crime in Britain, and received a posthumous royal pardon in 2013.
--- context
Turing's work underlies the [[technology/digital-computer|Digital Computer]] and the [[event/digital-revolution|Digital Revolution]], and his codebreaking bore on [[event/world-war-ii|World War II]]. Compare [[person/ada-lovelace|Ada Lovelace]].
--- observer
The Observer notes the irony at the center of this ledger: a society relied on one man's mind to survive a war, then punished him for who he was. Our laws about intimacy have too often stood at odds with our own interest.
--- question 1
How significant was his codebreaking to the war's outcome?

=== person/albert-einstein
title: Albert Einstein
type: person
date: 1879 – 1955
themes: science
src: e5073be0224f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Albert Einstein (1879 to 1955), a German-born physicist, developed the theories of special and general relativity and made key contributions to quantum theory.
--- fact 1
In 1905 he published papers on special relativity, the photoelectric effect, and Brownian motion.
--- fact 2
General relativity, published in 1915, describes gravity as the curvature of space and time.
--- fact 3
He won the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics for the photoelectric effect.
--- fact 4
In 1939 he signed a letter to President Roosevelt about the possibility of atomic weapons.
--- context
Einstein's work extended that of [[person/isaac-newton|Isaac Newton]] and reaches into the [[event/nuclear-age|Nuclear Age]] through the equivalence of mass and energy.
--- observer
I think of ideas about the smallest and largest scales of the universe, both born from the same mind's thought experiments, both carried first in imagination before they were carried in equations. I think, too, of how a name outgrows a body of work — how he became a symbol of genius standing far beyond what he actually did. What do we love: the man, or the shape we have made of him?
--- question 1
How much did he influence the decision to build nuclear weapons?

=== person/alexander-the-great
title: Alexander the Great
type: person
date: 356 – 323 BCE
themes: war, trade, language
src: e98aeb21b38f
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Alexander III of Macedon (356 to 323 BCE) was a military commander who conquered the Persian Empire and carried Greek influence from Egypt to India.
--- fact 1
He became king at about twenty, after the assassination of his father, Philip II.
--- fact 2
He won major battles at Issus and Gaugamela against the Persian king Darius III.
--- fact 3
His army turned back at the Hyphasis (Beas) river in India.
--- fact 4
He died in Babylon in 323 BCE, and his empire was divided among his generals.
--- context
See [[event/alexander-s-conquests|Alexander's Conquests]]. His campaigns shaped [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]] and joined the world of the [[culture/ancient-greeks|Ancient Greeks]] to that of the [[culture/achaemenid-persians|Achaemenid Persians]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that one man's ambition moved armies across thousands of kilometers, and that his lasting legacy was not conquest itself but the exchange of ideas and languages that followed in its wake.
--- question 1
What were his actual plans for the empire?
