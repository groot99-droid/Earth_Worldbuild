=== person/truganini
title: Truganini
type: person
date: c. 1812 – 1876
themes: war, kinship
src: f43ed8dca844
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Truganini, born about 1812 and dead in 1876, was a Nuenonne woman who grew up on Bruny Island and lived through the British colonization of Tasmania, dying in Hobart. History later widely fixed her with the label "the last Tasmanian Aboriginal" — a closing line that Tasmanian Aboriginal people themselves reject.
--- fact 1
She was born about 1812, reportedly at Recherche Bay in southern Tasmania, as a member of the Nuenonne people, and grew up on Bruny Island.
--- fact 2
Accounts say colonists killed or abducted members of her family during the Black War of the 1820s and early 1830s.
--- fact 3
In the 1830s she worked with George Augustus Robinson, a government-appointed conciliator, who persuaded surviving Aboriginal people to move to Flinders Island, where disease and hardship were severe.
--- fact 4
She later lived at Oyster Cove, south of Hobart, and died on 8 May 1876.
--- fact 5
Her skeleton was exhumed in 1878 and put on display in the Tasmanian Museum from 1904 until 1947, despite her reported fear of mutilation.
--- fact 6
In 1976 her remains were cremated and her ashes were scattered in the D'Entrecasteaux Channel.
--- fact 7
Tasmanian Aboriginal people today descend from other women, among them Fanny Cochrane Smith, and their community continues.
--- context
Truganini's life sits inside the wider British colonization that followed the [[event/first-fleet-and-the-colonization-of-australia|First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]], which reshaped the lives of the [[culture/aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal Australians]] across [[region/oceania|Oceania]] during the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that colonial officials wrote almost the whole record of Truganini's life, and that the account of her final decades centers on what happened to her body after death, once her own wishes about burial went unhonored. The label "the last Tasmanian" turned a living people into a full stop, a closing device that suited the settlers' version of events. A specialist could argue that this label rests on a narrow test of descent, and that such a reading undercounts how much of Truganini's own purpose can still be recovered.
--- question 1
What might Aboriginal oral histories add to the colonial record of her life?

=== person/trung-sisters
title: Trưng Sisters
type: person
date: 40 – 43 CE
themes: war, kinship
src: 675c36b7d465
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Trưng Sisters, Trưng Trắc and Trưng Nhị (known in Chinese as Zheng Ce and Zheng Er), led a revolt against Han Chinese rule in northern Vietnam from 40 to 43 CE. Their birth years are unknown; the dates recorded here belong to the revolt itself.
--- fact 1
The sisters came from Mê Linh in the Red River region, then governed as part of the Han commandery of Jiaozhi.
--- fact 2
They launched the revolt in 40 CE. Chinese sources tie its cause to the harsh rule of the Han governor Su Ding, and Vietnamese tradition adds that he had executed Trưng Trắc's husband, Thi Sách.
--- fact 3
They seized towns across several southern commanderies and are recorded as having ruled as queens for about three years.
--- fact 4
The Han general Ma Yuan marched an army south and defeated them by 43 CE.
--- fact 5
The Chinese history *Hou Hanshu* records that they were captured and beheaded, while Vietnamese tradition holds that they drowned themselves in a river; this second account remains traditional rather than documented.
--- fact 6
Temples across Vietnam honor the sisters today, and streets carry their joint name, Hai Bà Trưng.
--- context
The Trưng Sisters rose against the [[culture/han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]] in [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] during [[era/classical-antiquity|Classical Antiquity]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Vietnam remembers the sisters as founders of national resistance, a unifying story for a people whose own state would not exist for centuries. The two chief records, Chinese court histories and later Vietnamese chronicles, tell different versions of their end, so each side's writers shaped what survived. This reading would weaken if evidence showed that the local lords who backed the revolt acted chiefly for their own interests rather than for any shared cause.
--- question 1
Who were the local leaders who backed the revolt, and what did they actually want?

=== person/tupaia
title: Tupaia
type: person
date: c. 1725 – 1770
themes: science, language
src: 52e2c7e7f8b4
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Tupaia, born about 1725 and dead in 1770, was a priest and navigator from Ra'iatea in the Society Islands who sailed with James Cook aboard the Endeavour in 1769 and drew a chart of the Pacific islands. He died at Batavia in 1770.
--- fact 1
Tupaia was born about 1725 on Ra'iatea and rose as a priest and navigator. When warriors from Borabora took Ra'iatea, he found refuge on Tahiti.
--- fact 2
In July 1769 he joined Cook's Endeavour as guide and interpreter, usually said to have boarded at Tahiti, though sources differ on the place, bringing with him a young servant, Taiata.
--- fact 3
In August 1769 he began charting the Pacific together with Cook, Joseph Banks and other officers. Sources differ: the chart is said to show about 130 islands, of which he could name about 74, and it is often described as centered on Ra'iatea.
--- fact 4
The chart did not follow European map conventions, and modern scholars have argued that it instead follows a Polynesian way of ordering islands by voyages and directions.
--- fact 5
On reaching New Zealand in October 1769 he found he could speak with Māori, whose language is closely related to Tahitian, and so interpreted for the crew.
--- fact 6
He died at Batavia on 20 December 1770, of illness, and Taiata died there that same month.
--- context
Tupaia belonged to the [[culture/polynesians|Polynesians]], whose ancestors carried out the [[event/polynesian-settlement-of-the-pacific|Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]. That he could speak with [[culture/maori|Maori]] in New Zealand reflects the shared root of their languages. See [[theme/science|Science]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
I picture Tupaia holding an ocean's routes only in memory, then setting them down on paper for men who could not read them, because the paper did not match their grid. I picture two ways of knowing the sea meeting on one small ship. I picture that meeting surviving only because one side wrote it down.

What would be left of Tupaia's chart, I ask, if the officers who helped him draw it shaped its layout more than he did? Knowledge kept only in memory is no less knowledge for going unwritten.
--- question 1
How much of the chart's layout is Tupaia's own work, and how much belongs to the officers who drew it with him?

=== person/tupac-amaru-ii
title: Túpac Amaru II
type: person
date: 1738 – 1781
themes: war, trade, kinship
src: 7fb7744891ce
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Túpac Amaru II — José Gabriel Condorcanqui, born in 1738 or about 1742, and dead in 1781 — was a hereditary Indigenous chief and trader who led a large rebellion against Spanish colonial rule in the Andes across 1780 and 1781. The Spanish executed him in Cusco.
--- fact 1
José Gabriel Condorcanqui was born in 1738, though some sources say about 1742, in the highlands near Cusco. He rose as a kuraka, a hereditary local chief, and became a successful muleteer and trader.
--- fact 2
He adopted the name Túpac Amaru, claiming descent from the Inca ruler Túpac Amaru, whom the Spanish had executed in 1572.
--- fact 3
Grievances behind the rebellion included higher Spanish taxes, forced sales of goods to Indigenous communities, and the mita, forced labor in the mines.
--- fact 4
On 4 November 1780 he seized the local Spanish official Antonio de Arriaga, who was executed shortly after — 10 November 1780, according to two sources — and the rebellion began.
--- fact 5
He issued proclamations abolishing the mita and other abuses, and the rebellion spread across the southern highlands.
--- fact 6
Spanish forces captured him in April 1781, and he was executed in Cusco on 18 May 1781 alongside his wife, Micaela Bastidas, who had helped lead the rebellion.
--- context
Túpac Amaru II claimed the legacy of the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]], which the Spanish had ended in the [[event/spanish-conquest-of-the-inca-empire|Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]], in the [[region/andes|Andes]] during the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]]. His rebellion is often grouped with the [[event/age-of-revolutions|Age of Revolutions]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Túpac Amaru II cast a dispute over taxes and labor in the language of descent, drawing on the ancestral weight of the Inca name to call a wide and mixed population into common cause. His trade dealings and colonial education gave him a foothold in both worlds, and the rebellion shows how the architecture of the past can serve a grievance in the present. This reading would weaken if sources showed that most support came from local grievances his Inca claim did little to mobilize.
--- question 1
How much of the rebellion's support rested on the Inca claim, and how much on local grievances alone?

=== person/ulugh-beg
title: Ulugh Beg
type: person
date: 1394 – 1449
themes: science, art
src: 16965471c167
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
Ulugh Beg, who lived from 1394 to 1449, was a Timurid ruler and astronomer who built an observatory at Samarkand and produced one of the most accurate star catalogues made before the telescope.
--- fact 1
He became governor of Samarkand in 1409, at about sixteen years old.
--- fact 2
He built a madrasa between 1417 and 1420, then, in 1420, an observatory.
--- fact 3
Completed by about 1437, his star catalogue lists at least 1,018 stars.
--- fact 4
He died in 1449 and was laid to rest in the Gur-e-Amir mausoleum in Samarkand, beside his grandfather, Timur.
--- context
Ulugh Beg turned [[place/samarkand|Samarkand]] into a center of learning within the [[culture/timurid-empire|Timurid Empire]], carrying forward the traditions of [[person/al-khwarizmi|Al-Khwarizmi]] and the [[event/translation-movement-and-the-house-of-wisdom|Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]].
--- observer
I find a ruler who chose the sky over the battlefield, and I find his star catalogue still standing after his dynasty fell. I did not expect a pattern-tamer to flourish inside a palace built for war, but here he is. Wonder can grow in unlikely soil.
--- question 1
How accurate were Ulugh Beg's stellar measurements, really?

=== person/usman-dan-fodio
title: Usman dan Fodio
type: person
date: 1754 – 1817
themes: religion, war, language
src: 3e06b2ad148e
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Usman dan Fodio, who lived from 1754 to 1817, was a Fulani scholar and religious leader whose jihad created the Sokoto Caliphate.
--- fact 1
He taught and preached within the Hausa state of Gobir, writing widely in both Arabic and Fulfulde.
--- fact 2
In 1804 he and his followers left Gobir, and the jihad opened that February.
--- fact 3
He is regarded as the founder of the Sokoto Caliphate and its first sultan, or caliph.
--- fact 4
His brother and his son both helped administer the new state.
--- context
See [[culture/sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]]. His movement stood among several reformist jihads across West Africa.
--- observer
The Observer notes that this state grew out of a program of teaching and reform. The founder's writings, not his armies alone, gave the caliphate its lasting identity.
--- question 1
How far did his writings go on to shape the caliphate's law?
