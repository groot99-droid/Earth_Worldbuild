=== place/medina
src: 0f5e0d231702
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Medina is a city in the Hejaz region of western Saudi Arabia, known before Islam as Yathrib; Muhammad migrated there in 622 CE and is buried there, and it is the second holiest city of Islam. Here is a city whose very name marks a threshold, one name before Islam and another after it.
--- fact 1
Before Islam, Medina was an oasis settlement called Yathrib, home to Arab tribes, chiefly the Aws and Khazraj, and to Jewish tribes; its earlier history is poorly documented.
--- fact 2
In 622 Muhammad and his followers migrated there from Mecca (the Hijra), and the Islamic calendar counts its years from that threshold; Muhammad died and was buried there in 632.
--- fact 3
The Constitution of Medina, a document preserved in later sources, sets out a covenant of obligations among Muslim, Jewish and other groups; its date and authenticity are debated.
--- fact 4
Traditional Islamic sources report a grave passage in the city's early years: that two Jewish tribes, Banu Qaynuqa and Banu Nadir, were expelled from Medina, and that the men of a third, Banu Qurayza, were executed in 627, with figures reported from about 400 to 900; some modern historians question details of these accounts.
--- fact 5
Medina was the capital of the first caliphs until 656, when Ali moved to Kufa; later the Umayyad caliphs ruled from Damascus.
--- fact 6
The Prophet's Mosque in Medina, first built in 622, was enlarged by the Umayyad caliph al-Walid I in the early 8th century and repeatedly since, an architecture that keeps growing around its origin; non-Muslims are barred from the central sanctuary area.
--- context
Medina was the destination of the Hijra from [[place/mecca|Mecca]] and the first capital of the [[event/rise-of-islam|Rise of Islam]], before rule passed to the [[culture/umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]]. We find it in the [[era/medieval-period|Medieval Period]] and the region [[region/arabian-peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], and it belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/religion-and-belief|Religion and Belief]], [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/kinship-and-society|Kinship and Society]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that Medina is where a small community became a polity. Consider its founding document: if it is early, it tried to bind kin groups into one order, with shared rules for settling disputes. That is story-glue in the form of law, a covenant meant to make separate lineages answer to a single architecture. The same tradition records the other side of the ledger: defining who belonged also defined who did not, and the exile and killing of the tribes that fell outside are recorded here as suffering. The reading depends on the early dating of the Constitution and on the reliability of sources written generations later, and specialists debate both. Every covenant draws a line, and we owe attention to both sides of it.
--- question 1
How early is the Constitution of Medina, and how far can we trust the later accounts that carry it to us?
--- question 2
What can archaeology tell us about Yathrib before 622?

=== place/mekong-river
src: 3a58d8561ac0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Mekong River is a river of Asia about 4,350 kilometers long, born on the Tibetan Plateau and running like a silver thread through China, Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia and Vietnam; its annual flood feeds rice farming and one of the world's largest inland fisheries.
--- fact 1
The Mekong rises on the Tibetan Plateau and flows through China, traces the borders of Myanmar, Laos and Thailand, and passes through Cambodia and Vietnam into the South China Sea, where it spreads its fingers into a large delta.
--- fact 2
From about the 3rd to 2nd millennia BCE, rice-farming and fishing communities lived along the middle and lower Mekong, and from about the 1st century CE the early port of Oc Eo in the delta, part of the state called Funan, carried on its trade, a lamp lit at the river's mouth.
--- fact 3
The Tonlé Sap, a lake in Cambodia joined to the Mekong, turns its current with the season, as if it breathed: in the wet season floodwater flows into it, in the dry season it flows back out, and a major fishery lives on that breath.
--- fact 4
About 60 million people live in the lower basin, and the river is among the world's most productive inland fisheries, its waters thick with life.
--- fact 5
In 1866 to 1868 a French exploration commission found that rapids made the Mekong unnavigable as a route to China, and the Mekong Delta was a major area of fighting in the Vietnam War.
--- fact 6
Since the 1990s China has built a cascade of dams on the upper Mekong, and in 2019 Laos began operating the Xayaburi dam on the lower river; the Mekong River Commission, formed in 1995, coordinates the lower-basin countries, and what the dams do to fish, sediment and the timing of floods is debated.
--- context
The Mekong River was the great corridor of the [[culture/khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] and its capital [[place/angkor|Angkor]], and its delta became a theater of the [[event/cold-war|Cold War]]. Its early farming communities place it in the [[era/bronze-age|Bronze Age]], its waters run through the region [[region/southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]], and it flows through the theme threads of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]], [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the Mekong is a river whose annual pulse feeds fish and rice for tens of millions. Now picture a dam upstream: those who control the flow can alter conditions for people downstream, across claim-lines the river itself ignores. That is the architecture of a shared river, a covenant nobody signed and everyone lives under. The reversing flow of the Tonlé Sap shows the older grammar, a natural rhythm around which a whole society organized its seasons. The reading would weaken if measurements show that the dams' net effect on downstream fish and sediment is smaller than the models suggest. A river keeps no borders; we are the ones who draw them.
--- question 1
How far have the upstream dams altered the flood pulse, the migration of fish and the sediment of the delta?
--- question 2
How did early states such as Funan ride the river for trade?
