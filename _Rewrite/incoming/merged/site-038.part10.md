=== culture/neo-assyrian-empire
title: Neo-Assyrian Empire
src: 1dbce1c2e8f2
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Neo-Assyrian Empire, from about 911 to 609 BCE, was a large empire based in northern Mesopotamia, known for its army, its palace reliefs, and the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. Hold those three together and you see its whole architecture: force, image and archive.
--- fact 1
Its main capitals were Ashur, Kalhu (Nimrud), Dur-Sharrukin, and Nineveh; power, here, kept more than a single address.
--- fact 2
Consider its reach: at its height in the 7th century BCE it controlled Mesopotamia, the Levant, and, briefly, Egypt.
--- fact 3
It used mass deportation as an instrument of rule, moving conquered populations across the empire.
--- fact 4
The library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh held tens of thousands of clay tablets and fragments, including a standard version of the Epic of Gilgamesh, a codex of inherited literature.
--- fact 5
A coalition of Babylonians and Medes captured Nineveh in 612 BCE, and Assyrian resistance ended by about 609 BCE.
--- context
The Neo-Assyrian Empire carried on the cuneiform tradition begun by the [[culture/sumerians|Sumerians]] and preserved much of it in royal libraries. It is an early example of a state organized around [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]], as its own propaganda describes. The Neo-Assyrian Empire stands in the [[era/iron-age|Iron Age]] and the region of [[region/mesopotamia|Mesopotamia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[theme/language-and-writing|Language and Writing]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the empire boasted of its violence in stone reliefs, and also collected and copied the literature of its predecessors. We like our categories clean, the destroyer here and the keeper of memory there; this state refuses them. A state can be both a destroyer and an archivist, and the archive is why we know about the destruction. Hold that paradox without resolving it.
--- question 1
How far can we trust what Assyrian royal inscriptions say about their own campaigns?

=== culture/norse
title: Norse
src: e6d2a3b8fdd7
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Norse (Vikings) were seafaring peoples from Scandinavia who raided, traded, and settled across Europe and the North Atlantic, riding the whale-road from about 793 to 1066 CE.
--- fact 1
The raid on Lindisfarne monastery in 793 is often taken as the start of the Viking Age, the opening note of a long saga.
--- fact 2
Norse settlers colonized Iceland (from about 870) and Greenland (from about 985), carrying their hearths over cold water.
--- fact 3
They reached North America: the L'Anse aux Meadows site in Newfoundland has been dated by tree rings to 1021 CE, the wood itself keeping count.
--- fact 4
Norse traders also travelled the rivers of eastern Europe, exchanging goods for silver from the Islamic world, silver flowing north like moonlight on a current.
--- context
The Norse bound [[region/europe|Europe]] to the North Atlantic and to the Eurasian exchange-web, as knots bind a net.
--- observer
The Observer notes that raiders and traders were often the same people, with the balance of violence and commerce depending on the opportunity. Think of a hand that can open to trade or close into a fist: it is the same hand. Opportunity, not identity, set the balance.
--- question 1
Why did Norse Greenland fade away?

=== culture/olmecs
title: Olmecs
src: 6596f1ec6052
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: confessional-poetry
--- summary
The Olmecs were an early Mesoamerican culture on the Gulf coast of Mexico, best known for colossal carved stone heads that gaze out of deep time, from about 1200 to 400 BCE.
--- fact 1
Major centers included San Lorenzo and La Venta, hearths of an early light.
--- fact 2
Colossal heads, some over 3 meters tall, were carved from basalt hauled long distances, mountains carried across the land.
--- fact 3
They worked rubber. The name 'Olmec' is the Nahuatl word for 'rubber people', which the Aztecs applied to later Gulf-lowland neighbors and which archaeologists later applied to this ancient culture; its own name is unknown, lost like a voice under water.
--- fact 4
Whether they were a 'mother culture' or one of several interacting societies is debated, a question still turning like a slow current.
--- context
The Olmecs are among the earliest societies in [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and their influence flowed on into later cultures such as the [[culture/maya|Maya]].
--- observer
I see a face carved larger than any living face. I see monumental art made as a status-signal for rulers. I see it made in a region with no metal tools and no wheeled transport. What would I carry that far, without a wheel, to be remembered? Power has long wanted a face.
--- question 1
Did the Olmecs ever set their words down in writing?

=== culture/ottoman-empire
title: Ottoman Empire
src: 1a779f54f19d
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Ottoman Empire was a state founded in Anatolia about 1299 that grew, like a great tree spreading its branches, into a multiethnic empire across Southeast Europe, West Asia, and North Africa, lasting until 1922.
--- fact 1
Mehmed II conquered Constantinople in 1453, an old city bowing to a new banner.
--- fact 2
The empire reached its zenith under Suleiman the Magnificent (reigned 1520 to 1566).
--- fact 3
The millet system gave religious communities a degree of self-government, many lamps beneath a shared roof.
--- fact 4
The sultanate was abolished in 1922, and in 1923 the Republic of Turkey was proclaimed, a new day rising where an empire had set.
--- context
See [[place/constantinople|Constantinople]]. The empire inherited from earlier states such as the [[culture/abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] and endured into the [[era/industrial-age|Industrial Age]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a long-lived empire that governed diverse peoples by layering communities. Think of a building where each floor keeps its own rules but all of them share a foundation. Pluralism was a practical answer to the problem of ruling strangers: not a sermon, but a floor plan.
--- question 1
How did the millet system actually work, day to day?
