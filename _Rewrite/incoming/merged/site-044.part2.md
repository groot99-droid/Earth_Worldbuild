=== event/spanish-conquest-of-the-aztec-empire
title: Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire
src: 17934de00662
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Between 1519 and 1521, a small Spanish force under Hernan Cortes — allied with many Indigenous city-states and aided by epidemic disease — overthrew the Aztec Empire and captured Tenochtitlan.
--- fact 1
In 1519, Cortes landed on the Mexican coast with several hundred men.
--- fact 2
Tlaxcala and other city-states, resentful of Aztec tribute, provided large numbers of allied fighters.
--- fact 3
A smallpox epidemic in 1520, introduced by the Spanish, killed a large share of the region's population.
--- fact 4
Malintzin (Dona Marina) served Cortes as interpreter and negotiator.
--- fact 5
Tenochtitlan fell on 13 August 1521, after a siege of about three months.
--- context
The conquest ended the [[culture/aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] and destroyed its capital, [[place/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]. It stands as an early episode of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], in which disease played a central part. The Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the conquest is sometimes told as a victory of a few hundred men, but the record shows Indigenous allies and disease doing much of the work. Simple stories of small forces conquering empires tend to leave out the local politics that made them possible.
--- question 1
What share of the population died from disease?

=== event/spanish-conquest-of-the-inca-empire
title: Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire
src: 3ae362617f25
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
In 1532, Francisco Pizarro's small Spanish force captured the Inca emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca, opening a conquest of the Inca Empire that ended with the fall of the last Inca stronghold in 1572.
--- fact 1
At Cajamarca, Pizarro commanded around 168 men.
--- fact 2
The empire had only just come through a civil war between Atahualpa and his half-brother Huascar, and epidemic disease had already reached the Andes.
--- fact 3
Held for a room-filling ransom of gold and silver, Atahualpa was then executed by strangulation on 29 August 1533.
--- fact 4
In 1533, the Spanish occupied Cusco.
--- fact 5
In 1572, the neo-Inca state at Vilcabamba was destroyed.
--- context
The conquest ended the [[culture/inca-empire|Inca Empire]] and opened the silver mines of [[place/potosi|Potosi]] to Spanish exploitation, following the pattern of disease and political division seen across the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]]. The Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/andes|Andes]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that an empire with roads, storehouses, and a knotted-cord recording system was taken over by a group that could not read its records. What was lost included the very means of understanding what had been lost.
--- question 1
How decisive was the civil war, compared with the epidemic?

=== event/spread-of-grasslands
title: Spread of Grasslands
src: 1bc890fce7dd
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Across the Miocene, from about 25 to 5 million years ago, open grasslands spread like a green tide across the continents. Animal life was remade in its wake, and the plant family that advanced with it now supplies most of the world's staple crops.
--- fact 1
Grass pollen and microscopic plant silica reach back to the latest Cretaceous or early Paleogene, yet for tens of millions of years grass remained a minor thread in the world's vegetation.
--- fact 2
Open grasslands widened from the early to middle Miocene, about 20 to 15 million years ago. Grasses of the C4 pathway, suited to hot, dry conditions and low carbon dioxide, spread widely about 8 to 6 million years ago, as carbon isotopes in fossil soils and teeth attest.
--- fact 3
Proposed drivers include cooling and drying, seasonal rainfall, falling carbon dioxide, and fire — their relative roles still debated.
--- fact 4
Grazing mammals answered with high-crowned teeth and long limbs built for running; horses turned from browsing to grazing, and grazing antelope, cattle, and their kin diversified.
--- fact 5
The cereals — wheat, rice, and maize among them — are grasses themselves; see [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]].
--- context
The Spread of Grasslands belongs mostly to the [[era/neogene-period|Neogene Period]] of the [[era/cenozoic-era|Cenozoic Era]], a long backdrop to the story of the Miocene apes and early hominins. Its crop lineage is the wellspring of [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]] and [[event/domestication-of-maize|Domestication of Maize]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/earth-systems-and-life|Earth Systems and Life]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that the staple foods of nearly every large human society trace to a plant family that became widespread only in the last few tens of millions of years. Open landscapes are also the setting of a long-running hypothesis about human origins, one the Observer treats as a hypothesis, not a finding. It would weaken if early hominins proved to have lived mainly in closed woodland.
--- question 1
What mix of climate, carbon dioxide, and fire drove the spread of C4 grasses?
--- question 2
How much did open grassland actually shape hominin evolution?

=== event/suez-canal
title: Suez Canal
src: 873035caac21
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Suez Canal, opened on 17 November 1869, is a sea-level waterway across Egypt linking the Mediterranean and the Red Sea, shortening the sea route between Europe and Asia.
--- fact 1
It was built by a French company under Ferdinand de Lesseps, using a great deal of Egyptian forced and paid labor.
--- fact 2
It is about 193 kilometers long.
--- fact 3
In 1875 Britain bought Egypt's shares in the canal company, and in 1882 it occupied Egypt.
--- fact 4
The canal shortened the voyage between Europe and India by thousands of kilometers.
--- context
The canal reshaped the [[event/indian-ocean-trade-network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] for the industrial age and sits within the [[region/nile-valley|Nile Valley]] region. See [[event/scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]] and [[technology/steam-engine|Steam Engine]].
--- observer
The Observer notes a strip of water dug by hand that rearranged the geography of world trade. A single chokepoint can become a lasting source of power for whoever holds it.
--- question 1
How many workers died building the canal?

=== event/sugar-plantation-complex
title: Sugar Plantation Complex
src: 2d6d64b19bfd
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
From the 1500s, European colonizers built large sugar plantations across Brazil and the Caribbean, worked mainly by enslaved Africans — one of the most profitable and brutal systems in early modern trade.
--- fact 1
Sugarcane originated in New Guinea and spread across Asia before reaching the Mediterranean and the Atlantic islands of Madeira and Sao Tome.
--- fact 2
The plantation model — large-scale production for export by enslaved labor — developed on the Atlantic islands and spread to Brazil in the 16th century.
--- fact 3
Beginning in Barbados in the 1640s, the Caribbean sugar boom expanded the use of enslaved labor.
--- fact 4
Saint-Domingue, the French colony that became Haiti, stood as the largest sugar producer in the world in the late 18th century.
--- fact 5
Sugar plantations drove much of the demand behind the Atlantic slave trade.
--- context
The sugar economy was the engine of the [[event/atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and a leading part of the [[event/columbian-exchange|Columbian Exchange]], following the collapse of the [[culture/taino|Taino]] population. In Saint-Domingue it was overthrown by the [[event/haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]. The Sugar Plantation Complex sits within the [[era/early-modern-period|Early Modern Period]] and the region [[region/caribbean-and-atlantic-world|Caribbean and Atlantic World]], and belongs to the theme thread of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that a sweet crop, bound for European tea tables, required the forced movement of millions of people across an ocean. Distance between consumer and producer made the harm easy to overlook.
--- question 1
How large a contribution did the sugar sector make to European industrialization?

=== event/taiping-rebellion
title: Taiping Rebellion
src: 6e57f9dab184
mode: essay-self-help
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The Taiping Rebellion (1850 to 1864) was a civil war in Qing China led by Hong Xiuquan, who claimed to be the younger brother of Jesus Christ; it stands among the deadliest conflicts in history.
--- fact 1
In 1853, the rebels captured Nanjing and made it their 'Heavenly Capital'.
--- fact 2
The war devastated about 17 provinces.
--- fact 3
Most estimates put deaths at 20 to 30 million people, about 5 to 10 percent of China's population, though some estimates run far higher.
--- fact 4
Hong Xiuquan died on 1 June 1864, and Nanjing fell shortly afterward.
--- context
The rebellion unfolded as the [[culture/qing-dynasty|Qing Dynasty]] reeled from the [[event/opium-wars|Opium Wars]]. See [[theme/war-and-conflict|War and Conflict]] and [[observer-note/sanctioned-harm|Sanctioned Harm]].
--- observer
The Observer notes how a new meaning-engine, blending Christian ideas with local grievances, mobilized millions within a decade — one of the largest wars in history, and among the least remembered outside China.
--- question 1
What were the true mortality figures?
