=== species/goat
src: cdde9ad070e0
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
The domestic goat was bred from the wild bezoar ibex in southwestern Asia around 10,000 years ago, one of the first animals kept by farming communities, and it is still prized for milk, meat and fiber, above all on thin, hard land where other livestock cannot thrive.
--- fact 1
Domestic goats descend from the bezoar ibex, a wild goat still roaming the Zagros Mountains of Iran and once widespread in Anatolia, and their domestication is dated to about 10,000 years ago in Iran.
--- fact 2
The earliest clear archaeological evidence of goat domestication comes from Ganj Dareh in Iran, about 10,000 years ago, and goats from other early sites at Jericho, Choga Mami, Djeitun and Çayönü date to between about 8,000 and 9,000 years ago.
--- fact 3
Neolithic herders kept goats for milk and meat, and they used the dung as fuel and the bones, hair and sinew for tools, building materials and clothing, so that nothing of the animal was wasted.
--- fact 4
Goats still matter to the economy, giving about 2 percent of the world's annual milk supply and fibers such as cashmere and mohair, and they are valued partly for their hardiness on land too marginal for cattle or sheep.
--- fact 5
More than 1.1 billion goats were alive worldwide as of 2022, about 150 million of them in India, the largest single-country herd; India was also the largest goat-milk producer that year, followed by Bangladesh and South Sudan.
--- context
Goats were among the first animals drawn into [[event/animal-domestication|Animal Domestication]], a pillar of [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] in the Fertile Crescent, beside [[species/sheep|Sheep]], which were domesticated in a similar place and age and are often herded with them. Goats belong to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/iranian-plateau|Iranian Plateau]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact. The goat's continuing importance on land too poor for cattle or sheep suggests that its domestication gave early farming communities a way to draw food and materials from marginal ground that would otherwise have carried no livestock at all, extending the reach of the Settling into harsher terrain. Think of it as the animal that widened the map. That goats and sheep were domesticated in the same general region and period, and are still often herded together, points to an early kin-lattice of pastoral practice that treated the two species as partners rather than rivals for the same land. The reading would weaken if goat and sheep domestication are shown to have happened at meaningfully different times or in different communities rather than in close succession.
--- question 1
How closely linked were the domestication of goats and sheep: did one community likely tame both, or did the practices spread separately?
--- question 2
How is goat herding on marginal land changing as climate change shifts which regions count as marginal?

=== species/rice
src: 39d3186f9de6
mode: epic-fantasy
observer_mode: essay-self-help
--- summary
Rice is a cereal grass domesticated from a wild ancestor in the Yangtze valley of China from about 9,000 years ago, and today it is the staple food of more than half the world's people, a grain grown in water that feeds a great part of humankind.
--- fact 1
Rice was tamed from wild _Oryza rufipogon_ by Neolithic communities in the Upper and Lower Yangtze valley, in what is now China, about 9,000 years ago.
--- fact 2
The gene that keeps rice grains from shattering is identical in the two main cultivated groups, japonica and indica, pointing to a single first domestication rather than two; indica is thought to have arisen later, when japonica reached India about 4,500 years ago and crossed with a local wild or semi-wild rice.
--- fact 3
Rice farming reached Korea and Japan between about 5,500 and 3,200 years ago, island Southeast Asia and Taiwan between about 5,500 and 4,000 years ago, carried in part by Austronesian-speaking migrations, and mainland Southeast Asia by about 5,000 years ago.
--- fact 4
Rice is the staple food of more than half the world's population, and in the early 2020s China and India together consumed several hundred million tonnes of it.
--- fact 5
The Green Revolution rice variety IR8 was bred in 1966 at the International Rice Research Institute from an Indonesian and a Chinese variety, giving a short, strong-stemmed plant that stood upright under heavy fertilizer.
--- fact 6
World rice production reached about 800 million tonnes in 2023, about 90 percent of it grown in Asia, and less than 8 percent of the crop crosses borders in trade.
--- context
Rice's domestication is told in more detail under [[event/domestication-of-rice|Domestication of Rice]] and belongs to [[event/the-agricultural-revolution|The Agricultural Revolution]] beside cereals tamed elsewhere; its 20th-century breeding is part of the [[event/green-revolution|Green Revolution]]. Rice farming shaped the population and economy of the Yangtze valley and other East Asian river valleys, while Neolithic farming in the [[place/yellow-river|Yellow River]] region rested mainly on millet, with rice grown there to a lesser extent. It belongs to the [[era/neolithic|Neolithic]] and the region [[region/east-asia|East Asia]], and to the theme threads of [[theme/trade-and-economy|Trade and Economy]] and [[theme/technology|Technology]].
--- observer
The Observer notes that rice farming, especially the flooded-field methods used across much of Asia, binds a community to sustained cooperative labor. Building and maintaining paddies, canals and terraces is a task larger than any one household, and the Observer reads this as a kin-lattice pressure toward dense, cooperative settlement: the architecture of the field shapes the architecture of the village. That most of the world's rice is grown and eaten within Asia, with little traded across borders, shows a staple still tied to the regions that farm it rather than a fully global exchange-good in the way wheat has become. The reading would weaken if paddy-field cooperation proves to have mattered less to social organization than dry-field cereal farming did elsewhere.
--- question 1
Was there truly a single domestication of rice, or did separate lineages contribute more than current genetic evidence suggests?
--- question 2
How did paddy irrigation systems shape the social order of the communities that built and kept them?
