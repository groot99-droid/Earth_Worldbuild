## Summary
Doggerland was a lowland region of hunter-gatherers that connected Britain to continental Europe and lay where the North Sea is today until it was gradually flooded by rising seas.

## Facts
- After the last ice age, sea level rose as ice sheets melted, and the land now under the southern North Sea was progressively submerged.
- It was largely underwater by around 6000 BCE.
- The Storegga Slide, a huge submarine landslide off Norway, produced a tsunami around 6200 BCE that struck the coasts of the region.
- Fishing vessels have dredged up animal bones and worked bone points from the seabed.

## Context & Connections
Doggerland is a European counterpart to [[Beringia]], a lost landscape drowned by post-glacial sea-level rise, and it followed the [[Last Glacial Maximum]] and the [[Younger Dryas]]. It belongs to the theme thread of [[Earth Systems and Life]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a landscape once lived on by humans now lies under fishing grounds and wind farms, its record kept only as tools brought up in nets. The land bridge between Britain and Europe is a reminder that coastlines are provisional.

## Open Questions
- How large was the population, and how did it respond to rising water?

## Summary
Homo naledi is a small-brained human relative whose remains were found in the Rising Star cave system in South Africa in 2013, dated to about 335,000 to 236,000 years ago.

## Facts
- Fossils of at least 15 individuals were found deep in a cave.
- The brain size was about 500 cubic centimeters, yet the species lived at the same time as early Homo sapiens.
- Researchers have proposed deliberate burial and cave markings, but those claims are contested.

## Context & Connections
H. naledi lived in the same time and continent as early [[Homo sapiens]] and challenges the link between large brains and complex behavior.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that if the burial claims hold, complex treatment of the dead would not require a large brain. The claim is under active debate.

## Open Questions
- Did H. naledi bury its dead?
