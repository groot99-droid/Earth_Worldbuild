## Summary
War and conflict are organized violence between groups: raids, battles and sieges, conquests, rebellions, civil wars, and genocides, together with the treaties and settlements that end them. The vault follows them from the uneven evidence for violence in prehistory, through the chariot armies and iron-armed empires of antiquity, the steppe conquests and gunpowder states of the medieval and early modern worlds, and the colonial and revolutionary wars of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, to the industrial world wars and the nuclear standoff of the twentieth. The scale and character of organized violence has changed with technology, political organization, and the ability to feed, move, and command large forces. This hub records atrocity plainly as atrocity and notes where casualty figures and causes are disputed.

## Key Threads
- Evidence for organized violence is uneven in early prehistory and grows with settled societies. The wall and tower at [[Jericho]] (about 8000 BCE) may have served defense or protection from floods, and the early city of [[Caral]] shows no evidence of large-scale warfare.
- Bronze Age states fielded chariots and fortified centers. [[The Wheel]] and [[Bronze Metallurgy]] supplied vehicles and weapons, the [[Sintashta Culture]] built fortified settlements and the earliest known spoke-wheeled chariots, and at the [[Battle of Kadesh]] (about 1274 BCE) Egyptian and [[Hittites]] armies fought one of the largest chariot battles on record. About fifteen years later they concluded a peace treaty, of which versions survive in both archives. Around 1200 BCE many of these states collapsed within a few decades ([[Bronze Age Collapse]]; see also the [[Mycenaean Greeks]]).
- Iron Age empires built large armies and administrations. [[Iron Smelting]] made it easier to arm large populations, the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]] used mass deportation and controlled Mesopotamia, the Levant, and briefly Egypt, and the [[Achaemenid Persians]] under [[Cyrus the Great]] took Babylon in 539 BCE and built [[Persepolis]]. [[Alexander's Conquests]] (336 to 323 BCE) ended the Achaemenid Empire.
- Rome and Carthage fought three Punic Wars between 264 and 146 BCE, and Rome destroyed [[Carthage]] in 146 BCE. [[Augustus]] ended a century of civil war and began the Pax Romana; the [[Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] in 476 CE was a long process that historians debate as either collapse or transformation. See [[Romans]].
- In East and South Asia, the [[Unification of China under Qin]] in 221 BCE under [[Qin Shi Huang]] created the first unified Chinese empire. The [[Han Dynasty]] faced the [[Xiongnu]] confederation, at times paying it in gifts and marriage alliances, and [[Ashoka]] of the [[Maurya Empire]] conquered Kalinga around 261 BCE, an event his own edicts record as killing about 100,000 people and deporting 150,000, after which he supported Buddhism and promoted moral conduct.
- Steppe powers repeatedly confronted settled states. The [[Scythians]] were horse-riding nomads of the Iron Age, the [[Göktürk Khaganate]] ruled Inner Asia from about 552 CE, and from 1206 the [[Mongol Conquests]] under [[Genghis Khan]] built the largest contiguous land empire in history, including the sack of Baghdad in 1258; estimates of the deaths run to tens of millions and are uncertain. [[Timur]] later campaigned from Persia to India, sacking Delhi in 1398 and defeating the Ottoman sultan at Ankara in 1402.
- Faith, empire, and firepower drove long wars. The [[Rise of Islam]] and the [[Umayyad Caliphate]] extended Muslim rule from Iberia to Sindh, the [[Battle of Talas]] in 751 ended Tang expansion in Central Asia, the [[Crusades]] began in 1095, and [[Gunpowder]], developed in China and used by the Song in fire arrows, bombs, and early firearms, reached Europe by the 13th century. The Ottomans used very large cannon in the [[Fall of Constantinople]] in 1453.
- Conquest and resistance shaped the early modern world. The [[Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]] (1519 to 1521) relied on Indigenous allies and on smallpox, and the [[Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]] began in 1532 after an Inca civil war. The [[Mapuche]] resisted the Inca and then the Spanish for centuries, and [[Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba]] used diplomacy and war against Portuguese expansion.
- European wars built and tested states. The [[Thirty Years' War]] (1618 to 1648) killed up to about eight million people, mostly from famine and disease, and ended with the [[Peace of Westphalia]]; the [[Seven Years' War]] (1756 to 1763) was fought on several continents; and the [[Napoleonic Wars]] (1803 to 1815) ended at Waterloo and helped set off Latin American independence movements.
- Revolutions and wars of independence recast who held power. See the [[Age of Revolutions]], the [[American Revolution]], the [[French Revolution]], the [[Haitian Revolution]], and the [[Latin American Wars of Independence]], and, later, the [[Mexican Revolution]], the [[Russian Revolution]], and the [[Xinhai Revolution]].
- Wars between the Qing state, Britain, and rebels shook Asia in the 19th century: the [[Opium Wars]] opened China to Western commerce on unequal terms, the [[Taiping Rebellion]] (1850 to 1864) killed an estimated 20 to 30 million people, and the [[Indian Rebellion of 1857]] ended East India Company rule ([[British Raj]]).
- Wars of conquest and defense reshaped Africa and the settler colonies. The [[Zulu Kingdom]] under [[Shaka]] became a large military state and the [[Mfecane]] followed; the [[Scramble for Africa]] raised the share of the continent under European control from roughly 10 percent to about 90 percent between 1881 and 1914; Ethiopia won the [[Battle of Adwa]] in 1896; and [[Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]] and the [[First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]] led to dispossession and death for Native American and Aboriginal peoples.
- Industrial war reached unprecedented scale. Railways, telegraphs, and factories shaped the [[American Civil War]]; [[World War I]] killed roughly 10 million soldiers and brought down four empires; [[World War II]] killed an estimated 60 to 85 million people, including about six million Jews murdered in the [[Holocaust]]. The [[Armenian Genocide]] and the [[Second Sino-Japanese War]], including the killings at Nanjing, show mass killing of civilians.
- [[Nuclear Fission]], discovered in 1938, made nuclear weapons possible, and the [[Nuclear Age]] began with the first test in July 1945. The [[Cold War]] (about 1947 to 1991) was fought through arms races, alliances, and proxy wars, and ended with the [[Collapse of the Soviet Union]]; [[Decolonization]] was won by negotiation and by war, and the [[Partition of India]] in 1947 brought mass migration and violence.

## Through the Eras
- **Paleolithic:** Clear evidence for organized fighting between groups is scarce, and scholars disagree about how common it was. DNA shows interbreeding among [[Homo sapiens]], [[Homo neanderthalensis]], and [[Denisovans]], so contact between human groups was not only conflict. See [[Paleolithic]].
- **Neolithic:** Settled life brought walls, stored food, and land to defend ([[The Agricultural Revolution]], [[Jericho]]), though the purpose of the early wall at Jericho is debated.
- **Bronze Age:** [[Unification of Egypt]] around 3100 BCE, the king of Babylon [[Hammurabi]], the [[Sintashta Culture]] and its chariots, the [[Hittites]] and Egypt at the [[Battle of Kadesh]], the [[Mycenaean Greeks]], the [[Shang Dynasty]], and the [[Bronze Age Collapse]].
- **Iron Age:** [[Iron Smelting]], the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]], the [[Scythians]], the [[Kingdom of Kush]], which ruled Egypt as its 25th Dynasty, the [[Achaemenid Persians]] under [[Cyrus the Great]], and [[Carthage]].
- **Classical Antiquity:** [[Alexander the Great]] and [[Alexander's Conquests]], [[Rome]] and the [[Romans]] under [[Augustus]], the [[Unification of China under Qin]], the [[Han Dynasty]] and the [[Xiongnu]], the [[Maurya Empire]] and [[Ashoka]], the [[Byzantine Empire]], and the [[Fall of the Western Roman Empire]].
- **Medieval Period:** The [[Rise of Islam]], the [[Umayyad Caliphate]], the [[Battle of Talas]], [[Norse]] raids from 793, the [[Crusades]], the [[Mongol Conquests]], [[Timur]], the [[Fall of Constantinople]], and [[Gunpowder]]; the [[Classic Maya Collapse]], in which warfare is among the proposed causes; and the [[Chola Dynasty]], whose fleet struck [[Srivijaya]] around 1025.
- **Early Modern Period:** The [[Spanish Conquest of the Aztec Empire]] and [[Spanish Conquest of the Inca Empire]], the [[Thirty Years' War]], the [[Peace of Westphalia]], the [[Seven Years' War]], the [[Russian Expansion into Siberia]], the [[Dutch East India Company]], the [[Mapuche]], and [[Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba]]. Japan under the [[Tokugawa Shogunate]] enjoyed a long period of peace from 1603 to 1868.
- **Industrial Age:** The [[Napoleonic Wars]], [[Haitian Revolution]], [[Latin American Wars of Independence]], [[Opium Wars]], [[Taiping Rebellion]], [[American Civil War]], [[Scramble for Africa]], [[Battle of Adwa]], [[World War I]], [[Armenian Genocide]], [[Turkish War of Independence]], [[Second Sino-Japanese War]], [[World War II]], and the [[Holocaust]].
- **Information Age:** The [[Nuclear Age]], the [[Cold War]], [[Decolonization]], the [[Partition of India]], and the [[Collapse of the Soviet Union]].

## Regional Patterns
- **[[Mesopotamia]], the [[Levant and Anatolia]], and the [[Nile Valley]]:** The great powers of the Bronze Age and Iron Age, Egypt, the Hittites, and Assyria, fought over the Levant and left battle and treaty records on more than one side; see the [[Battle of Kadesh]] and the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]].
- **[[Central Asian Steppe]]:** Mobile pastoral powers ([[Scythians]], [[Xiongnu]], [[Göktürk Khaganate]], [[Mongol Empire]]) repeatedly confronted settled states, from the Han frontier to Baghdad and Eastern Europe.
- **[[East Asia]]:** Unification under Qin, the Han wars with the Xiongnu, Song use of gunpowder weapons, Ming construction of much of the Great Wall in its present form, and the Qing expansion to its greatest extent were followed by the [[Opium Wars]], [[Taiping Rebellion]], [[Boxer Rebellion]], and [[Second Sino-Japanese War]]; see [[Song Dynasty]], [[Ming Dynasty]], and [[Qing Dynasty]].
- **[[Sub-Saharan Africa]]:** Wars of state-building ([[Zulu Kingdom]], [[Sokoto Caliphate]]) and of resistance ([[Kingdom of Kongo]], [[Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba]], [[Battle of Adwa]], the British conquest of [[Kingdom of Benin]] in 1897) were followed by the forced labor and mass deaths of the [[Congo Free State]] (1885 to 1908).
- **[[Mesoamerica]] and the [[Andes]]:** The [[Aztec Empire]] collected tribute from conquered city-states, whose resentment helped the Spanish; the [[Inca Empire]] expanded under [[Pachacuti]]; and warfare is among the proposed causes of the [[Classic Maya Collapse]], for which no single cause is accepted.
- **[[Southeast Asia]]:** [[Champa]] was gradually conquered by Vietnam until its last remnant was annexed in 1832, the [[Pagan Kingdom]] fell to Mongol armies in 1287, [[Ayutthaya Kingdom]] was destroyed by Burmese forces in 1767, and the [[Dutch East India Company]] used force to secure monopolies.
- **[[Oceania]]:** The [[First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]] began conflict and dispossession for [[Aboriginal Australians]], and disputes over land and authority after the [[Treaty of Waitangi]] led to the New Zealand Wars of the 1840s to 1870s.
- **[[Siberia and the Arctic]]:** Russian Cossacks and traders advanced to the Pacific between 1582 and 1639 for furs and tribute ([[Russian Expansion into Siberia]]), and the [[Yakut (Sakha)]] revolted against Russian rule in 1642.

## Debates and Limits
- Scholars disagree about how common organized warfare was in prehistory, and about whether it grew mainly with settled societies and states. The evidence is mostly skeletal injury, fortification, and weapons, and it can be read in more than one way.
- Casualty figures are uncertain and sometimes politically contested: the deaths from the [[Mongol Conquests]] (tens of millions, uncertain), the [[Congo Free State]] (about 1 million to 15 million), the [[Taiping Rebellion]] (20 to 30 million), the killings at Nanjing in the [[Second Sino-Japanese War]], and the [[Armenian Genocide]], which the Turkish state denies.
- Causes are often disputed. Historians debate whether the [[Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] was a collapse or a transformation, and many regard the common claim that the [[Peace of Westphalia]] created the sovereign-state system as a later simplification. They also disagree about the scale and causes of the [[Mfecane]] and the role of [[Shaka]]. The [[Bronze Age Collapse]] and the [[Classic Maya Collapse]] have no single accepted cause.
- Many accounts come from the winners or from hostile writers: the accounts of [[Shaka]] come largely from colonial-era Europeans and oral tradition, later chronicles describe massacres by [[Timur]] with contested numbers, and later historians hostile to the Qin described harsh punishments that are debated.
- The vault covers a selection of conflicts. It has notes on only three individual battles ([[Battle of Kadesh]], [[Battle of Talas]], [[Battle of Adwa]]) and none yet on naval, air, or guerrilla warfare as topics, on the Korean War or the Vietnam War beyond their mention under the [[Cold War]], or on conflicts after 1991.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer calls this sanctioned harm: violence that a group approves through its story-glue. It notes how the same species that holds strong inhibitions against killing individuals can, under the right story-glue, organize mass killing, and it records the harm plainly as harm. It notes with equal seriousness the counter-tradition of treaties, laws of war, and nonviolent movements, which appears as early as the treaty after the [[Battle of Kadesh]] and the edicts of [[Ashoka]], and which states themselves often write. See [[Sanctioned Harm]]. This reading would weaken if organized killing proves to be explained mainly by scarcity or fear, with little role for shared stories that make it permissible.

## Open Questions
- What conditions make large-scale organized violence more or less likely?
- When did organized fighting between groups become common, and did settled life or the rise of states change its scale?
- How did new technologies, such as the horse and chariot, iron, gunpowder, railways, and nuclear weapons, change who could wage war and how much of a society's effort it took?
- Why do some wars end in durable settlements, such as the treaty after the [[Battle of Kadesh]] or the [[Peace of Westphalia]], while others lead to renewed conflict?
- How much of the death toll of war comes from famine and disease rather than combat, as in the [[Thirty Years' War]] and the [[Taiping Rebellion]]?
- How do societies remember wars, and who controls the record?
