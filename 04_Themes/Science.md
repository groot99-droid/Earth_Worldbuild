## Summary
Science is the systematic study of the natural world through observation, measurement, mathematics, and testing, together with the habits and institutions that let people check and extend one another's claims. Its practices run from the counting, calendar-keeping, and sky-watching of prehistoric peoples, through the mathematics, astronomy, and medicine of the early literate states and the scholarly centers of the medieval world, to the experimental sciences of Europe from the sixteenth century and the genomics, computing, and Earth-system research of the twenty-first. Traditions of careful observation arose independently in many regions, and modern science grew from several of them rather than from one. This hub gathers the vault's notes on scientists, institutions, instruments, and discoveries, and on the events that changed how knowledge of nature was made and passed on.

## Key Threads
- Counting, calendars, and sky-watching are among the oldest organized knowledge. The axis of [[Stonehenge]] aligns with the solstice sunrise and sunset, buildings and rock carvings at [[Chaco Canyon]] align with the sun and moon, and the [[Maya]] kept precise calendars.
- Writing turned observation into records that others could check. [[Invention of Writing]] began in [[Mesopotamia]] as accounting, and Mesopotamian scribes, from the time of the [[Sumerians]], worked in a base-60 number system that survives in the division of the hour and the circle.
- The [[Ancient Egyptians]] left medical papyri, such as the Edwin Smith papyrus of about 1600 BCE, that set out the examination and treatment of injuries case by case; see [[Nile Valley]].
- The [[Axial Age]] brought new traditions of reasoned inquiry in Greece, India, and China; see [[Confucius]] and the [[Ancient Greeks]].
- Greek natural philosophy sought general causes. [[Aristotle]] studied physics and biology, including careful observation of marine life; the [[Library of Alexandria]] supported scholars such as Eratosthenes, who estimated the circumference of the Earth; and [[Hypatia]] taught mathematics and astronomy in Alexandria. See also [[Athens]].
- The decimal place-value system with zero developed in India in the first millennium CE and spread west; see [[Zero and Place-Value Notation]] and the [[Gupta Empire]]. The age of the earliest zero, in the Bakhshali manuscript, is contested.
- Under the [[Abbasid Caliphate]], the [[Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]] rendered Greek, Persian, Syriac, and Indian works into Arabic and extended them. The arithmetic and algebra of [[Al-Khwarizmi]] gave later languages the words algorithm and algebra, and [[Ibn Sina]]'s Canon of Medicine was used in European universities into the 17th century. [[Al-Andalus]] became another center of learning.
- Observatories, libraries, and manuscript collections grew along the Silk Roads and the Saharan routes. [[Ulugh Beg]]'s observatory at [[Samarkand]] produced a star catalogue of at least 1,018 stars, and [[Timbuktu]] holds hundreds of thousands of historical manuscripts.
- Pacific navigators crossed thousands of kilometers using star paths, swells, and bird flight rather than instruments; see [[Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] and [[Polynesians]].
- Instruments extended what could be measured and shared: the [[Magnetic Compass]] in China by about the 12th century, the [[Mechanical Clock]] (Su Song's astronomical clock tower of 1088; weight-driven clocks in European towns about 1300), and the [[Movable-Type Printing Press]] of about 1440.
- The [[Scientific Revolution]] (roughly 1543 to 1687) made experiment, mathematics, and observation central; see [[Galileo Galilei]] and [[Isaac Newton]]. It drew on earlier work in the Islamic world, India, and China.
- The [[Enlightenment]] carried a preference for evidence into public life. In 1796 Edward Jenner showed that cowpox inoculation protected against smallpox, the start of [[Vaccination]]; smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980.
- The 19th and early 20th centuries brought evolution by natural selection ([[Charles Darwin]]), radioactivity ([[Marie Curie]]), relativity ([[Albert Einstein]]), and what is often regarded as the first published computer algorithm ([[Ada Lovelace]]); the [[Meiji Restoration]] and the [[Tanzimat Reforms]] were state programs to acquire the new sciences and their institutions.
- Late 20th-century science gave computation ([[Alan Turing]], [[Digital Computer]]), the structure of DNA ([[Rosalind Franklin]], [[Genomic Revolution]]), and spaceflight ([[Space Age]], [[Moon Landing]]); [[Rachel Carson]], [[Modern Climate Change]], the [[Jack Hills Zircons]], and the [[Tunguska Event]] show Earth-system science at work.

## Through the Eras
- **Paleolithic:** Working knowledge of stone, fire, animals, and plants accumulates ([[Oldowan Stone Tools]], [[Control of Fire]], [[Emergence of Symbolic Art]]). [[Aboriginal Australians]] managed the landscape with fire and passed on ecological knowledge through oral traditions and songlines. Notched bones from this era may be tallies, though their purpose is debated.
- **Neolithic:** Farmers select seed and stock over generations ([[Domestication of Maize]], [[Domestication of Rice]], [[Animal Domestication]]), and builders align monuments with the sun ([[Stonehenge]]).
- **Bronze Age:** Writing lets scribes keep records, tables, and observations ([[Invention of Writing]], [[Sumerians]], [[Ancient Egyptians]]); Mesopotamian arithmetic uses base 60, and Egyptian medical texts describe cases and treatments.
- **Iron Age:** Reasoned inquiry into nature and human affairs appears in the [[Axial Age]] in Greece, India, and China ([[Confucius]], [[Ancient Greeks]]); Polynesian voyagers build up knowledge of stars and swells ([[Polynesians]]).
- **Classical Antiquity:** [[Athens]] and [[Aristotle]], the [[Library of Alexandria]] and [[Hypatia]], and in India [[Zero and Place-Value Notation]] under the [[Gupta Empire]]. The [[Han Dynasty]] kept astronomical records, and its scholar Zhang Heng built a seismoscope in 132 CE. The [[Opening of the Silk Roads]] moved ideas along with goods.
- **Medieval Period:** The [[Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]], [[Al-Khwarizmi]], [[Ibn Sina]], and [[Al-Andalus]]; the [[Song Dynasty]]'s [[Magnetic Compass]] and [[Gunpowder]]; the [[Maya]] calendar and astronomy; [[Chaco Canyon]]; [[Ulugh Beg]] and the [[Timurid Empire]]; [[Timbuktu]]; [[Ibn Khaldun]]'s theory of how states rise and fall; the [[Mechanical Clock]]; the [[Renaissance]].
- **Early Modern Period:** The [[Scientific Revolution]] ([[Galileo Galilei]], [[Isaac Newton]]), the [[Enlightenment]], and [[Vaccination]] (1796).
- **Industrial Age:** The [[Industrial Revolution]] created new demand for measurement and engineering. [[Charles Darwin]], [[Ada Lovelace]], [[Marie Curie]], [[Albert Einstein]], [[Antibiotics]], [[Nuclear Fission]], and the [[Electrical Power Grid]] belong here, as do state modernization programs such as the [[Meiji Restoration]] and [[Tanzimat Reforms]].
- **Information Age:** [[Alan Turing]] and the [[Digital Computer]], [[Rosalind Franklin]] and the [[Genomic Revolution]], the [[Space Age]] and [[Moon Landing]], [[Rachel Carson]] and [[Modern Climate Change]], the [[Green Revolution]], and the [[COVID-19 Pandemic]], in which mRNA vaccines were developed and authorized within about a year.

## Regional Patterns
- **[[Mesopotamia]] and the [[Nile Valley]]:** The earliest written mathematics, astronomy, and medicine are tied to temple and palace administration; see [[Sumerians]] and [[Ancient Egyptians]].
- **[[South Asia]] and the Islamic world:** Place-value notation with zero arose in India and was carried west by scholars of the [[Abbasid Caliphate]] and [[Al-Andalus]]; Latin translations of Arabic texts fed European scholarship in the 12th century.
- **[[East Asia]]:** Paper, printing, the compass, and gunpowder were documented first in China, and the [[Song Dynasty]] was a center of technical and commercial innovation.
- **Central Asia:** The oases were a crossroads for learning. [[Ibn Sina]] was born near Bukhara, [[Ulugh Beg]] built his observatory at [[Samarkand]], and the [[Timurid Empire]] patronized it; see [[Central Asian Oases]].
- **[[Oceania]]:** Polynesian voyagers navigated without instruments ([[Polynesians]]), the [[Kuk Early Agricultural Site]] preserves at least 7,000 years of wetland gardening, and the [[Jack Hills Zircons]] in Western Australia hold the oldest known pieces of Earth's crust, about 4.4 billion years old.
- **[[Mesoamerica]] and the [[Andes]]:** The [[Maya]] kept precise calendars and used the concept of zero, and the Andean [[Quipu]] recorded numbers in a decimal system on knotted cords.
- **[[Sub-Saharan Africa]]:** Scholarship in [[Timbuktu]] and much of the fossil evidence for human origins ([[Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]], [[Origin of Homo sapiens]]) come from this region.
- **[[Siberia and the Arctic]]:** The [[Tunguska Event]] of 1908 was first studied on site by an expedition in 1927, and Arctic peoples such as the Inuit built up detailed knowledge of ice, weather, and animal behavior for survival ([[Inuit and the Thule Expansion]]).

## Debates and Limits
- Whether star-lore, navigation, and Indigenous ecological knowledge should be called science, or only the experimental and mathematical tradition of recent centuries, is disputed. This hub uses a broad definition and treats the narrower one as a later development.
- Historians debate whether the [[Scientific Revolution]] was a single revolution and how much it owed to earlier work in the Islamic world, India, and China. The exact nature of the House of Wisdom is also debated, and the [[Library of Alexandria]] declined over centuries rather than in one fire.
- Credit is contested in many cases: the earliest zero ([[Zero and Place-Value Notation]]), the calculus, which [[Isaac Newton]] developed independently of Leibniz, natural selection, which [[Charles Darwin]] and Alfred Russel Wallace reached separately, the use of [[Rosalind Franklin]]'s X-ray data in the double-helix model, and Lise Meitner's exclusion from the 1944 Nobel Prize for [[Nuclear Fission]].
- Science that was carried by oral teaching, songlines, and cord records left few written texts, so it is easy to undercount.
- The vault's science notes lean toward Europe, the Islamic world, and named individuals. It has few notes on scientists in Africa south of the Sahara, Oceania, the Andes, and Mesoamerica, and none yet on Indian astronomy or Chinese mathematics as topics in their own right.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer calls this pattern-taming: the species' long effort to find regularities in nature, record them, and use them to predict. Counting, calendars, and careful observation appear in many places long before the modern period, so what looks distinctive about the modern form is its institutionalized doubt, in which strangers agree to check one another's claims and to publish what they find. That habit rests on memory-outsourcing (writing, paper, printing) and on exchange-webs that carried instruments, texts, and people, and it works against ancestor-weight, since the older authority is often the first claim to be tested. This reading would weaken if institutionalized doubt proves to have been as old and as widespread as counting itself, or if it turns out to depend on something other than writing and travel.

## Open Questions
- How did the culture of open criticism emerge and spread, and where did it exist in institutions before the seventeenth century?
- How much of the [[Scientific Revolution]] rested on knowledge that moved from the Islamic world, India, and China, and how can that debt be measured?
- What can be recovered of scientific traditions that were carried by oral teaching, songlines, and cord records?
- Why did centers of learning such as the [[Library of Alexandria]], Baghdad, and [[Samarkand]] decline, and what was lost when they did?
- Why do major discoveries so often arise independently in more than one mind or place, as with the calculus and natural selection?
