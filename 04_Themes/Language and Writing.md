## Summary
Language and writing are the systems by which people communicate and store information: speech, writing systems, numerals, record-keeping devices such as knotted cords, and the media that copy and carry them. Spoken language is universal and very old, though when it arose is uncertain; writing appeared much later, independently in a few places, and spread by contact, conquest, religion, and trade. The vault follows them from the symbolic behavior of early Homo sapiens, through clay tablets, alphabets, paper, and printing, to the telegraph and digital networks, and it also covers the spread of language families across continents. It gives equal weight to peoples who kept records without alphabets.

## Key Threads
- Spoken language is at least many tens of thousands of years old and is universal in [[Homo sapiens]], a species notable for language, symbolic thought, and cumulative culture. Speech leaves no direct fossil trace, so its date is inferred from indirect evidence such as symbolic behavior; see [[Emergence of Symbolic Art]].
- Writing was invented independently at least three or four times. The first was in Mesopotamia about 3400 to 3200 BCE, where the earliest proto-cuneiform tablets from [[Uruk]] are accounting records ([[Invention of Writing]], [[Sumerians]]). Egyptian hieroglyphs appear by about 3200 BCE ([[Ancient Egyptians]]), and Chinese writing is securely attested by about 1250 BCE in the oracle-bone inscriptions of the [[Shang Dynasty]].
- Bronze Age states left archives in scripts that took long to read or remain unread. The [[Hittites]] wrote in cuneiform at Hattusa and spoke the oldest recorded Indo-European language; the [[Mycenaean Greeks]] wrote Linear B, an early form of Greek, deciphered in 1952; the [[Minoans]] used Linear A and Cretan hieroglyphs, which have not been deciphered; and the script of the [[Indus Valley Civilization]] has not been deciphered either.
- The [[Alphabet]] made literacy more accessible. The first alphabet, Proto-Sinaitic, appeared about 1800 BCE, adapted by Semitic-speaking workers from Egyptian hieroglyphs. The [[Phoenicians]] spread an alphabet of about 22 letters, the Greeks added vowels in the 8th century BCE, and Latin, Cyrillic, Hebrew, Arabic, and many Indian scripts descend from or are related to early alphabets. The Nabataean script is widely considered an ancestor of the Arabic script ([[Nabataeans]]), and the [[Kingdom of Saba]] wrote in the South Arabian script.
- Mesoamerican writing appears by roughly 600 BCE, and possibly earlier. [[Maya]] writing combines signs for words and syllables and is largely deciphered, and the Danzantes carvings at [[Monte Alban]] show glyphs from an early Mesoamerican writing tradition.
- Not every complex society used writing. The [[Quipu]] recorded numbers on knotted cords in the Andes, and whether the cords also encoded other information is debated; the Vedas were transmitted orally for centuries with elaborate memorization ([[Vedic Period]]); griots preserved the epic of [[Sundiata Keita]]; and [[Aboriginal Australians]] passed on knowledge through oral traditions, songlines, and ceremony.
- Rulers used writing and standard scripts to govern. The [[Unification of China under Qin]] standardized the script, [[Ashoka]] had edicts inscribed on rocks and pillars, [[Hammurabi]]'s law code was carved on a stone stele and displayed publicly, the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]] kept a library at Nineveh with tens of thousands of clay tablets, and the [[Library of Alexandria]] supported scholarship in the Hellenistic world.
- Decipherment has depended on bilingual texts and chance finds. The [[Rosetta Stone]], inscribed in 196 BCE in hieroglyphs, Demotic, and Greek, was found in 1799, and Champollion announced his decipherment of hieroglyphs in 1822. The Orkhon inscriptions of the [[Göktürk Khaganate]], erected in 732 to 735 in Mongolia and discovered in 1889, are the oldest known Turkic writing, and the Meroitic script of the [[Kingdom of Kush]] has been read phonetically although the language is not well understood.
- Paper and print lowered the cost of copying. [[Paper]] was developed in China by the 2nd century CE, [[Woodblock Printing]] produced the Diamond Sutra of 868, and [[Johannes Gutenberg]]'s [[Movable-Type Printing Press]] of about 1440 spread across Europe within decades. Printing helped spread Reformation pamphlets, and Luther's German New Testament appeared in 1522 and the whole Bible in 1534 ([[Protestant Reformation]]).
- Languages spread as administrative languages and lingua francas. Greek spread widely through [[Alexander's Conquests]] and blended with local traditions; Latin and Greek tied the [[Romans]]' world together; the language of the [[Sogdians]] served as a lingua franca along the Silk Roads; Abd al-Malik made Arabic the language of government under the [[Umayyad Caliphate]]; and Swahili, a Bantu language with many Arabic loanwords, grew up on the East African coast ([[Swahili Coast City-States]]).
- Translation moved knowledge between languages. The [[Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]] rendered Greek, Persian, Syriac, and Indian works into Arabic, and scholars such as [[Al-Khwarizmi]] carried the decimal place-value system with zero west ([[Zero and Place-Value Notation]]); Fibonacci's 1202 book popularized it in Europe.
- Language families spread with migrations. The [[Indo-European Language Spread]] carried a family now spoken as a first language by roughly 40 to 46 percent of the world's population, though its homeland is disputed; Austronesian languages spread from Taiwan from about 5,000 to 5,500 years ago as far as Madagascar and Rapa Nui, and the family has well over a thousand languages ([[Austronesian Expansion]]); and the [[Bantu Expansion]], over about four thousand years, gave rise to roughly 500 languages spoken by about 350 million people.
- Language and law meet in modern treaties. The English and Maori texts of the [[Treaty of Waitangi]] differ in key words about sovereignty and chieftainship, and te reo Maori became an official language of New Zealand in 1987 ([[Maori]]); Article 17 of the Treaty of Wuchale read differently in Amharic and Italian, and the dispute led to war ([[Battle of Adwa]]).
- The telegraph and digital media are the latest forms. [[Telegraph and Submarine Cables]] let messages cross oceans in minutes from the 1840s, and the [[Digital Computer]] and the [[Digital Revolution]] brought the internet (1969), the World Wide Web (1989 to 1991), and more than five billion internet users; see also [[Who Writes the Record]].

## Through the Eras
- **Paleolithic:** [[Homo sapiens]] and the symbolic behavior of [[Emergence of Symbolic Art]]. Speech is inferred rather than preserved, and the earliest evidence is engraved ochre, beads, and cave paintings; see [[Paleolithic]].
- **Neolithic:** There was no writing yet, but farming may have carried language families. The [[Austronesian Expansion]] relied on a crop package that included rice, and one hypothesis links the origin of Indo-European to Anatolia and the spread of farming, which is debated ([[Indo-European Language Spread]], [[The Agricultural Revolution]]).
- **Bronze Age:** [[Invention of Writing]], the [[Sumerians]], [[Uruk]], [[Hammurabi]], the [[Hittites]], the [[Mycenaean Greeks]], the [[Minoans]], the [[Shang Dynasty]], the [[Alphabet]], the oral [[Vedic Period]], and the [[Indus Valley Civilization]].
- **Iron Age:** The [[Phoenicians]], the [[Kingdom of Saba]], the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]], and the [[Bantu Expansion]].
- **Classical Antiquity:** [[Aristotle]], [[Alexander's Conquests]], the [[Romans]], the [[Library of Alexandria]], the [[Rosetta Stone]], [[Paper]], the [[Unification of China under Qin]] and [[Qin Shi Huang]], [[Ashoka]], [[Monte Alban]], the [[Sogdians]], and [[Zero and Place-Value Notation]].
- **Medieval Period:** The [[Maya]], the [[Umayyad Caliphate]], [[Woodblock Printing]], the [[Translation Movement and the House of Wisdom]], [[Al-Khwarizmi]], the [[Göktürk Khaganate]], the [[Swahili Coast City-States]], the [[Quipu]], [[Sundiata Keita]], [[Ibn Battuta]], whose account was dictated to the scholar Ibn Juzayy, and [[Johannes Gutenberg]] and the [[Movable-Type Printing Press]].
- **Early Modern Period:** The [[Protestant Reformation]] and the spread of print across Europe. English words including canoe, hammock, hurricane, and barbecue come from [[Taino]].
- **Industrial Age:** The [[Rosetta Stone]] was found in 1799 and hieroglyphs were deciphered in 1822; [[Usman dan Fodio]] wrote widely in Arabic and Fulfulde; and the [[Telegraph and Submarine Cables]] (from 1844), the differing English and Maori texts of the [[Treaty of Waitangi]] (1840), and the differing Amharic and Italian texts behind the [[Battle of Adwa]] (1896) show how wording and speed of transmission shaped politics.
- **Information Age:** The [[Digital Computer]], the [[Digital Revolution]], and the questions about authorship and archives raised in [[Who Writes the Record]].

## Regional Patterns
- **[[Mesopotamia]], the [[Nile Valley]], and the [[Levant and Anatolia]]:** Writing began in Mesopotamia and Egypt, and the alphabet arose among Semitic-speaking workers in the Levant; the Hittites and Mycenaeans show writing systems used by different language families.
- **[[East Asia]]:** Oracle-bone writing under the Shang, script standardization under Qin, and the invention of paper, woodblock printing, and movable type (in China about 1040 and in Korea in metal in 1377) made East Asia a center of the written word; see [[Paper]] and [[Woodblock Printing]].
- **[[South Asia]]:** The Vedas were transmitted orally, the decimal place-value system with zero developed here, Ashoka's edicts were inscribed across the subcontinent, and the Indus script remains undeciphered.
- **[[Mesoamerica]] and the [[Andes]]:** Mesoamerica developed full writing systems, of which Maya writing is the best known, and the Andes developed cord records in the [[Quipu]] without conventional writing; the two show different routes to keeping records.
- **[[Sub-Saharan Africa]]:** The [[Bantu Expansion]] spread roughly 500 languages, Swahili developed on the coast, griots kept oral epics, and [[Timbuktu]] holds hundreds of thousands of historical manuscripts.
- **[[Oceania]] and [[Southeast Asia]]:** Austronesian languages spread from Taiwan through the islands to Rapa Nui ([[Austronesian Expansion]]), [[Aboriginal Australians]] spoke more than 250 languages before 1788, and te reo Maori became an official language of New Zealand ([[Maori]]).
- **[[Central Asian Oases]] and the [[Central Asian Steppe]]:** The [[Sogdians]] spread a lingua franca along the Silk Roads, the Orkhon inscriptions preserve early Turkic writing, and paper mills were operating in [[Samarkand]] and Baghdad by the later 8th century.
- **[[Siberia and the Arctic]]:** The [[Yakut (Sakha)]] are a Turkic-speaking people of northeastern Siberia, a region that came under Russian rule from the 17th century ([[Russian Expansion into Siberia]]).

## Debates and Limits
- When and how spoken language arose is unknown. Speech leaves no fossil, and estimates rest on indirect evidence from anatomy, symbolic behavior, and genetics, so they range widely.
- Scholars agree that writing arose independently in more than one place but not on how many. The vault counts at least three or four, and whether Egyptian hieroglyphs arose independently of Mesopotamian writing has been debated; the date of the earliest Mesoamerican writing is also uncertain.
- Several scripts remain undeciphered or partly understood: the Indus script, Linear A, and Cretan hieroglyphs are unread, the language behind Meroitic is not well understood, and whether the quipu encode more than numbers is debated ([[Quipu]]).
- Language families, genes, and archaeology do not map neatly onto one another. The homeland of Indo-European is disputed, the [[Bantu Expansion]] involved intermarriage and language shift as well as migration, and counts of languages depend on how a language is defined (estimates of the Bantu languages range from about 440 to 680).
- The vault's coverage is uneven. It has no notes yet on the Chinese, Arabic, or Cyrillic scripts as topics, on language endangerment and loss, or on signed languages, and it has few notes on the languages of Oceania, the Americas, and Siberia.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer calls these the sound-code and the mark-code. It notes that language is the species' main means of accumulating knowledge across generations, and that the mark-code turns that accumulation into memory-outsourcing on a scale no single lifetime could hold; see [[The Great Outsourcing of Memory]]. The record shows the sound-code as universal and old and the mark-code as late, independently invented in a few places, and unevenly adopted, with some peoples running large states on cords, oral epics, and memorized texts instead. What gets written also shapes what is remembered; see [[Who Writes the Record]]. This reading would weaken if unwritten systems such as oral epics and cord records prove to preserve knowledge across centuries as reliably as writing does.

## Open Questions
- When and how did spoken language arise?
- Can the Indus script, Linear A, and Cretan hieroglyphs be deciphered, and what would they add to the record?
- Did the [[Quipu]] record narrative as well as numbers?
- Why did writing arise independently in some places and not in others, such as the Andes, where cords served instead?
- What decides whether a language spreads or disappears: conquest, trade, religion, prestige, or technology?
- How are digital media changing the ways people speak, write, and store records?
