## Summary
Earth systems and life cover the physical planet, its climate, and the history of living things, from the formation of the Solar System about 4.57 billion years ago to the present. The vault follows the planet's changing atmosphere, oceans, continents, and ice; the origin and diversification of life, punctuated by mass extinctions; the evolution of hominins and humans through ice-age cycles; and the recent period in which human activity has become a major influence on the planet. Human history sits inside this longer story and depends on it, on the warm and relatively stable climate of the Holocene, on domesticated plants and animals, and on the diseases and environmental changes that societies met along the way. This hub also covers the scientific work that reconstructed this history.

## Key Threads
- The Solar System formed about 4.57 billion years ago from a collapsing cloud of gas and dust, and Earth's age of about 4.54 billion years comes from radiometric dating of meteorites and of Earth's oldest lead ([[Formation of the Solar System and Earth]]). A Mars-sized body called Theia is thought to have struck the young Earth and formed the Moon ([[Theia Impact and the Formation of the Moon]]). The [[Jack Hills Zircons]] of Western Australia, about 4.4 billion years old, are the oldest known pieces of Earth's crust, and the [[Late Heavy Bombardment]], a hypothesized spike in asteroid impacts about 4.1 to 3.8 billion years ago, remains debated.
- Life arose at least 3.5 billion years ago, and how it began is not known ([[Origin of Life]]). [[Stromatolites]] in the Dresser Formation of Western Australia, about 3.48 billion years old, are the oldest widely cited evidence, although some researchers dispute that they are biological. [[Cyanobacteria]] perform oxygen-producing photosynthesis, and their earliest unambiguous fossils are about 2.15 billion years old.
- Oxygen rose in the atmosphere beginning about 2.4 billion years ago as photosynthetic microbes saturated chemical sinks such as dissolved iron, an episode recorded in banded iron formations ([[Great Oxidation Event]]). Eukaryotes, the cells from which animals, plants, and fungi are built, arose roughly 2 billion years ago, when bacteria that became mitochondria were taken into a host cell ([[Origin of Eukaryotes]]).
- The late Proterozoic brought glaciations, the Sturtian and Marinoan of about 717 to 635 million years ago, in which ice may have reached the tropics; scientists debate whether the oceans froze completely ([[Snowball Earth]]). Soon afterward the first large, complex multicellular organisms appear ([[Ediacaran Biota]]), and beginning 538.8 million years ago most major animal body plans appear over roughly 13 to 25 million years ([[Cambrian Explosion]]). [[Trilobites]] thrived for about 270 million years.
- Plants, then arthropods and vertebrates, moved onto land beginning about 470 million years ago ([[Colonization of Land]]), and [[Tiktaalik]], a 375-million-year-old fish with limb-like fins found on Ellesmere Island, is a transitional form toward four-limbed animals. The first two of the "Big Five" mass extinctions came in this era: the [[Late Ordovician Extinction]], which eliminated an estimated 85 to 86 percent of species, and the [[Late Devonian Extinction]], with about 75 percent lost.
- The [[Permian-Triassic Extinction]] about 251.9 million years ago was the largest in Earth's history, killing an estimated 80 to 96 percent of marine species, probably through Siberian volcanism, and recovery took roughly ten million years. The [[Triassic-Jurassic Extinction]] about 201.4 million years ago coincided with the [[Breakup of Pangaea]] and cleared space for the [[Dinosaurs]], which then dominated land for about 165 million years.
- Birds are living descendants of dinosaurs; [[Archaeopteryx]], a feathered, winged dinosaur from Late Jurassic Bavaria about 150 million years ago, shows the link. [[Flowering Plants]] appear about 130 million years ago and now make up most land plant species, and most crops descend from them. The [[Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]] about 66 million years ago wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs and about three-quarters of species after an asteroid struck what is now Mexico.
- The Cenozoic reshaped climate and geography. A burst of warming about 56 million years ago, in which a large release of carbon raised temperatures by roughly 5 to 6 degrees Celsius for about 200,000 years, acidified the ocean ([[Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum]]). The [[Collision of India and Asia]], from about 50 million years ago, raised the Himalaya and shaped the South Asian monsoon; the [[Messinian Salinity Crisis]] cut the Mediterranean off from the Atlantic between about 5.96 and 5.33 million years ago; and the [[Closure of the Isthmus of Panama]] about 3 million years ago joined North and South America.
- The human lineage split from that of chimpanzees and bonobos between roughly 6 and 8 million years ago ([[Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]]). [[Sahelanthropus tchadensis]] is a roughly 7-million-year-old fossil ape whose status as a hominin is debated, [[Australopithecus afarensis]] ("Lucy") walked upright about 3.9 to 2.9 million years ago, and [[Homo erectus]] was the first known hominin to spread widely beyond Africa. Small-brained relatives such as [[Homo naledi]] and [[Homo floresiensis]] lived alongside modern humans.
- Modern humans emerged in Africa by about 300,000 years ago ([[Origin of Homo sapiens]]) and lived through ice-age cycles. The [[Toba Eruption]] of about 74,000 years ago was one of the largest in two million years, though most researchers now consider a severe human population bottleneck unlikely. At the [[Last Glacial Maximum]], about 26,500 to 19,000 years ago, sea level stood some 120 to 135 meters lower, exposing [[Beringia]] and joining New Guinea and Australia; see [[Out of Africa Dispersal]], [[Peopling of Sahul]], and [[Peopling of the Americas]].
- Between roughly 50,000 and 10,000 years ago a large share of the world's biggest land animals went extinct ([[Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions]]); recent global analyses link the declines more closely to the expansion of Homo sapiens than to climate change, though regional debate continues. The [[Woolly Mammoth]] survived on Wrangel Island until about 4,000 years ago. The [[Younger Dryas]], a return to near-glacial cold from about 12,900 to 11,700 years ago, ended abruptly and marks the start of the [[Holocene Epoch]]; rising seas later drowned [[Doggerland]] by about 6000 BCE.
- The Holocene's warm, relatively stable climate is the setting for farming, cities, and writing. [[Animal Domestication]] and the [[Domestication of Maize]] reshaped food supplies, and [[Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]] shows peoples enriching soils. Environment and disease acted on societies as well: droughts are among the proposed causes of the [[Classic Maya Collapse]], the [[Black Death]] killed an estimated 30 to 60 percent of Europe's population, the [[Columbian Exchange]] moved crops, animals, and diseases between hemispheres, and the popular story of self-inflicted ecological collapse on [[Rapa Nui]] is contested.
- Understanding this history is itself recent. [[Charles Darwin]] published On the Origin of Species in 1859; Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912, and plate tectonics gained wide acceptance in the 1960s; the first scientific expedition to the [[Tunguska Event]] site came in 1927; and [[Rachel Carson]]'s Silent Spring (1962) documented the harm that pesticides caused to birds and ecosystems.
- Human activity is now a major influence on the planet. The [[Great Acceleration]] since about 1950 saw population rise from about 2.5 billion to about 8 billion, with steep rises in energy and fertilizer use, and the [[Green Revolution]] raised grain yields. In [[Modern Climate Change]], atmospheric carbon dioxide has risen from about 280 parts per million to about 423 in 2024; the [[COVID-19 Pandemic]], first identified in late 2019, became a global outbreak within months. The [[Anthropocene]] is a proposed interval that the International Union of Geological Sciences declined to formalize as an epoch in March 2024.

## Through the Eras
- **Paleolithic:** Ice-age cycles, the evolution of [[Homo neanderthalensis]], [[Denisovans]], and [[Homo sapiens]], the [[Out of Africa Dispersal]], the [[Last Glacial Maximum]], [[Beringia]], and the [[Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions]]; see [[Pleistocene Epoch]].
- **Neolithic:** The end of the ice age ([[Younger Dryas]], [[Holocene Epoch]]), the flooding of [[Doggerland]], [[Animal Domestication]], the [[Domestication of Maize]], [[The Agricultural Revolution]], and the bargain described in [[The Settling and Its Bargain]].
- **Bronze Age:** The eruption of Thera damaged Aegean settlements ([[Minoans]]), dated by radiocarbon to about 1600 BCE and by links with Egyptian chronology to about 1500 BCE, and drought and earthquakes are among the proposed causes of the [[Bronze Age Collapse]].
- **Iron Age:** The [[Incense Trade]] depended on Boswellia trees growing in southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa, the great dam at Marib irrigated the land of the [[Kingdom of Saba]] until it failed about the 6th century CE, and the [[Bantu Expansion]] carried farming across much of Africa.
- **Classical Antiquity:** The dark, fertile soils and earthworks of [[Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]] and the [[Upano Valley Settlements]], and the water channels and cisterns of the [[Nabataeans]] at Petra.
- **Medieval Period:** The [[Classic Maya Collapse]], the reservoirs and canals of [[Angkor]], the decline of [[Cahokia]] after about 1250 for debated reasons, the settlement of [[Rapa Nui]], and the [[Black Death]] (1346 to 1353).
- **Early Modern Period:** The [[Columbian Exchange]] after 1492, the [[Sugar Plantation Complex]], whose crop originated in New Guinea, and the [[Russian Expansion into Siberia]], driven by the fur trade.
- **Industrial Age:** [[Charles Darwin]]'s theory of evolution, the [[Industrial Revolution]]'s shift to coal and steam, the [[Tunguska Event]] of 1908, the [[1918 Influenza Pandemic]], and the start of the warming measured in [[Modern Climate Change]] against an 1850 to 1900 baseline.
- **Information Age:** [[Rachel Carson]], the [[Green Revolution]], the [[Great Acceleration]], the [[Genomic Revolution]], whose ancient-DNA work has changed accounts of prehistory, the [[COVID-19 Pandemic]], and the proposed [[Anthropocene]].

## Regional Patterns
- **[[Sub-Saharan Africa]]:** Most of the early fossil record of the human lineage comes from here: [[Sahelanthropus tchadensis]], [[Australopithecus afarensis]], [[Homo naledi]], and the emergence of [[Homo sapiens]] ([[Origin of Homo sapiens]], [[Divergence of the Hominin Lineage]]).
- **[[Southeast Asia]]:** The [[Toba Eruption]] in Sumatra about 74,000 years ago, the island human species [[Homo floresiensis]] on Flores, and the sea crossings toward Sahul.
- **[[Oceania]]:** The [[Jack Hills Zircons]] preserve the oldest known crust, the [[Peopling of Sahul]] required open-sea crossings at least 50,000 years ago, and [[Rapa Nui]] is a remote island whose settlement and ecological history are debated.
- **[[Siberia and the Arctic]]:** [[Beringia]] joined Siberia and Alaska at the Last Glacial Maximum, the [[Woolly Mammoth]] lived on into the Holocene on Wrangel Island, [[Tiktaalik]] was found in the Canadian Arctic, and the [[Tunguska Event]] flattened about 2,000 square kilometers of Siberian forest.
- **[[Mesoamerica]] and the [[Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]]:** The Chicxulub impact crater lies beneath the Yucatan Peninsula ([[Cretaceous-Paleogene Extinction]]), maize was domesticated in Mexico, droughts are among the proposed causes of the [[Classic Maya Collapse]], and Amazonian peoples enriched soils into dark earths.
- **[[Andes]]:** [[Monte Verde]] in southern Chile is among the sites that overturned the "Clovis-first" model of the peopling of the Americas, though its age is now debated, and the [[Chinchorro Culture]] lived on the Atacama Desert coast.
- **[[South Asia]]:** The [[Collision of India and Asia]] built the Himalaya and shaped the South Asian monsoon, and the [[Toba Eruption]]'s ash fell across the subcontinent.
- **[[Europe]] and the [[Mediterranean Basin]]:** [[Archaeopteryx]] comes from Bavaria, [[Doggerland]] was drowned by the North Sea, and the Mediterranean nearly dried out in the [[Messinian Salinity Crisis]].

## Debates and Limits
- Many dates and causes in deep time are uncertain or disputed. How life began is unknown, older claims of ancient stromatolites are disputed, whether the [[Late Heavy Bombardment]] happened at all is debated, oceans may or may not have frozen completely in [[Snowball Earth]], and the timing of the [[Closure of the Isthmus of Panama]] and the extent of drying in the [[Messinian Salinity Crisis]] are argued.
- The [[Anthropocene]] is a proposed geological interval, not an official epoch. The International Union of Geological Sciences declined to formalize it in March 2024; proposed start dates range from the beginning of agriculture to about 1950, and the term remains widely used.
- The human role in past environmental change is contested. Scholars disagree about how far hunting or climate drove the [[Quaternary Megafauna Extinctions]], whether the [[Toba Eruption]] nearly ended humanity, and how far human action, rats, disease, and slave raids each contributed to the decline of Rapa Nui; no single cause is accepted for the [[Classic Maya Collapse]].
- The record is biased. Earth's earliest crust has been largely destroyed by plate tectonics and erosion, so the Jack Hills zircons are the only direct samples of Hadean crust, and the fossil record favors organisms with hard parts and environments where sediment accumulates.
- The vault's coverage is uneven. It has notes on the Big Five extinctions and on 18 species, but none yet on the ice-age cycles as a topic, on plate tectonics beyond a few events, on ocean and atmospheric circulation, or on climate episodes within recorded history such as the Little Ice Age, and disease appears mainly through a few pandemics.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer treats the planet as the frame for everything else, and the species as a recent, fast-moving part of a system that has ended and restarted many times. The record shows life already reshaping the air, when oxygen-producing microbes drove the [[Great Oxidation Event]], and mass extinctions repeatedly clearing the ground for new lineages, so a single species altering the planet is new in its cause rather than in kind (see [[The Species as a Geological Force]]). What is also new is awareness: this is the first such shift whose cause can study and describe it, and can choose otherwise. The Observer treats the stable climate of the Holocene, in which farming and cities arose, as a correlation to be tested rather than a settled cause. This reading would weaken if the present rate of change proves to match earlier natural transitions in both cause and speed, or if the stability of the Holocene proves irrelevant to the rise of agriculture.

## Open Questions
- How do current changes compare with past planetary transitions, such as the [[Permian-Triassic Extinction]] and the [[Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum]], in rate and in cause?
- How did life begin, and how much earlier than 3.5 billion years ago might it have arisen?
- Did the stable Holocene climate make agriculture possible, or would farming have arisen anyway?
- What caused each of the Big Five extinctions, and how did recovery proceed?
- How large was the human role in the Quaternary megafauna extinctions compared with climate?
- How should the [[Anthropocene]] be dated, or should it be formalized at all?
