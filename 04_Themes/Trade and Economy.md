## Summary
Trade and economy cover the production, exchange, and taxation of goods, labor, and money, and the institutions that organize them: markets, credit, coinage, companies, tribute, and forced labor. The vault traces exchange from the long-distance movement of stone and shells in prehistory, through Bronze Age metal trade, the Silk Road, Indian Ocean, and trans-Saharan networks, and the global commerce created after 1492, to the industrial production and digital economy of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. Trade moved ideas, religions, crops, and diseases along with goods, and it also included the coerced trade in people. This hub gathers the vault's notes on routes, trading states, commodities, and economic systems.

## Key Threads
- Long-distance exchange of stone and shells goes back tens of thousands of years. The [[Lapita Culture]] moved obsidian and other goods over long distances across the western Pacific, and the hunter-gatherer-fishers of [[Poverty Point]] in Louisiana brought in stone and other materials from hundreds of kilometers away between about 1700 and 1100 BCE.
- Farming supported denser populations and permanent settlement ([[The Agricultural Revolution]]), and the first cities followed ([[The Urban Revolution]], [[Uruk]], [[Sumerians]]). [[Invention of Writing]] began as a way to record goods and obligations, and the earliest tablets from Uruk are accounting records. [[Bronze Metallurgy]] made trade essential because tin was scarce and had to be carried over long distances from places such as Afghanistan and later Cornwall.
- Bronze Age networks tied the eastern Mediterranean, Anatolia, Egypt, and the Indus. The [[Indus Valley Civilization]] traded with Mesopotamia and made goods to standard weights, the [[Minoans]] traded across the eastern Mediterranean, and [[Hatshepsut]] sponsored a trading expedition to Punt. The [[Bronze Age Collapse]] around 1200 BCE ended the palace economies, and the breakdown of trade networks is among the proposed causes.
- Caravan and sea traders grew rich as middlemen. The [[Incense Trade]] carried frankincense and myrrh from southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa by camel caravan; the [[Kingdom of Saba]] and the [[Nabataeans]] profited from taxing and guiding the caravans; and the [[Phoenicians]] of the Levant coast founded colonies, including [[Carthage]], and spread a simple alphabet.
- The [[Opening of the Silk Roads]] from the second century BCE linked East Asia, South Asia, Central Asia, and the Mediterranean. Goods included silk, horses, glass, spices, and paper, and disease also traveled the routes ([[Black Death]]). The [[Han Dynasty]] paid the [[Xiongnu]] annual gifts, the [[Kushan Empire]] connected Rome, Parthia, India, and Han China commercially, and the [[Sogdians]] became the leading Silk Road merchants, acting as caravan leaders, interpreters, and bankers.
- Large states built economies around roads, taxation, and records. The [[Romans]] were tied together by Latin, Greek, and trade, [[Augustus]] reorganized the army, provinces, and taxation, the [[Kingdom of Aksum]] controlled Red Sea trade between Rome, Arabia, and India and minted its own coins, and the Persepolis tablets of the [[Achaemenid Persians]] record rations and administration ([[Persepolis]]).
- The [[Indian Ocean Trade Network]] used the monsoon winds and was busiest from about the 8th to the 15th century, carrying spices, cotton textiles, ivory, gold, and Chinese porcelain. The [[Swahili Coast City-States]], such as Kilwa, which minted its own coins, grew rich from it; [[Srivijaya]] controlled shipping through the Strait of Malacca; the [[Chola Dynasty]] traded with China and Southeast Asia; and the [[Malacca Sultanate]] became the leading port of Southeast Asia in the 15th century before the Portuguese took it in 1511 ([[Portuguese Sea Route to India]]).
- Trans-Saharan trade in gold, salt, and ivory sustained the [[Ghana Empire]], the [[Mali Empire]], and the [[Songhai Empire]]. [[Mansa Musa]]'s pilgrimage in 1324 reportedly disrupted the gold market in Cairo, and [[Timbuktu]] became a hub of trade and scholarship. Farther south, [[Great Zimbabwe]] traded gold and ivory with the Swahili coast, and the [[Kingdom of Mapungubwe]] received glass beads and Chinese celadon through East African harbors.
- East Asia was a commercial center. The [[Tang Dynasty]]'s capital Chang'an held about a million people; the [[Song Dynasty]] issued some of the world's first government paper money; American silver, arriving through the Manila trade, became a major part of the [[Ming Dynasty]] economy; and the [[Tokugawa Shogunate]] limited foreign trade to Dutch and Chinese merchants at Nagasaki.
- Exchange and tribute in the pre-Columbian Americas took forms unlike those of Eurasia. [[Caral]] combined coastal fishing with inland farming, [[Teotihuacan]] had wide trade and political links, [[Chaco Canyon]] was a ceremonial and trade center with roads radiating from it, the [[Aztec Empire]] collected tribute from conquered city-states, and the [[Inca Empire]] exacted labor service (mit'a) rather than money taxes and kept records on [[Quipu]].
- After 1492 trade became global. The [[Columbian Exchange]] moved plants, animals, people, and diseases between the hemispheres; the [[Manila Galleon Trade]] (1565 to 1815) carried Chinese goods to the Americas and American silver to Asia, much of it from [[Potosi]], where the Spanish organized labor through the mita; and the [[Dutch East India Company]], chartered in 1602, was among the first companies to issue tradable shares and had powers to wage war and build forts.
- Coerced labor underpinned much of the Atlantic economy. The [[Atlantic Slave Trade]] forcibly transported about 12.5 million Africans to the Americas, the [[Sugar Plantation Complex]] drove much of the demand, the [[Haitian Revolution]] arose in Saint-Domingue, the largest sugar producer in the world in the late 18th century, and the [[Abolition of Slavery]] followed between 1807 and 1888.
- The [[Industrial Revolution]] began in Britain about 1760 with the shift to machine production driven by coal and steam, and economic output per person began to rise steadily for the first time in history. The [[Steam Engine]], [[Railways]], the [[Telegraph and Submarine Cables]], the [[Electrical Power Grid]], and the [[Suez Canal]], which cut the voyage between Europe and India by thousands of kilometers, reshaped trade in the 19th century.
- Modern economies were shaped by empire and by state policy. The [[Opium Wars]] opened China to Western commerce on unequal terms, the [[Scramble for Africa]] and the [[Congo Free State]] built forced-labor extraction, and after 1950 the [[Great Acceleration]] raised output and energy use steeply. [[Chinese Reform and Opening]] (from December 1978) brought market reforms and decades of rapid growth, the [[Digital Revolution]] spread computing and networks, and the [[COVID-19 Pandemic]] disrupted supply chains worldwide.

## Through the Eras
- **Paleolithic:** Stone, shell, and pigment moved between hunter-gatherer groups over long distances, but the evidence is fragmentary and the vault has no dedicated note on Paleolithic exchange; see [[Paleolithic]].
- **Neolithic:** Farming and herding ([[The Agricultural Revolution]], [[Animal Domestication]]) created storable surpluses and traction animals, and [[Pottery]] allowed food and liquids to be stored and transported.
- **Bronze Age:** [[The Urban Revolution]], [[Uruk]], [[Sumerians]], [[Invention of Writing]], [[Bronze Metallurgy]], [[The Wheel]], the [[Indus Valley Civilization]], the [[Minoans]], the [[Hittites]], [[Hatshepsut]]'s expedition to Punt, [[Caral]], [[Poverty Point]], the [[Lapita Culture]], and the [[Bronze Age Collapse]].
- **Iron Age:** The [[Incense Trade]], the [[Kingdom of Saba]], the [[Phoenicians]], [[Carthage]], the [[Scythians]], [[Samarkand]], the [[Olmecs]], the [[Achaemenid Persians]], and [[Iron Smelting]].
- **Classical Antiquity:** The [[Opening of the Silk Roads]], the [[Han Dynasty]] and [[Xiongnu]], the [[Romans]] and [[Augustus]], the [[Nabataeans]], the [[Kushan Empire]], the [[Sogdians]], the [[Kingdom of Aksum]], [[Teotihuacan]], the [[Maurya Empire]], and [[Zero and Place-Value Notation]], which made calculation easier.
- **Medieval Period:** The [[Indian Ocean Trade Network]], the [[Swahili Coast City-States]], [[Srivijaya]], the [[Abbasid Caliphate]], the [[Tang Dynasty]] and [[Song Dynasty]], the [[Ghana Empire]], [[Mali Empire]], and [[Timbuktu]], [[Great Zimbabwe]], the [[Kingdom of Mapungubwe]], [[Chaco Canyon]], [[Cahokia]], the [[Mongol Empire]], whose relay postal system sped communication across Eurasia, the [[Norse]], who traded in eastern Europe for silver from the Islamic world, the [[Malacca Sultanate]], [[Zheng He]], [[Ibn Battuta]], and the [[Black Death]].
- **Early Modern Period:** The [[Columbian Exchange]], the [[Portuguese Sea Route to India]], the [[Manila Galleon Trade]], [[Potosi]], the [[Dutch East India Company]], the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]], the [[Sugar Plantation Complex]], the [[Ming Dynasty]], the [[Mughal Empire]], which by 1700 ruled about 100 million people and made India one of the world's largest economies, the [[Tokugawa Shogunate]], and the [[Russian Expansion into Siberia]], driven by the fur trade.
- **Industrial Age:** The [[Industrial Revolution]], [[Railways]], the [[Telegraph and Submarine Cables]], the [[Electrical Power Grid]], the [[Suez Canal]], the [[Trans-Siberian Railway]], the [[Opium Wars]], the [[British Raj]], the [[Scramble for Africa]], the [[Congo Free State]], the [[Abolition of Slavery]], and the [[Bengal Famine of 1943]], which Amartya Sen analyzed as an entitlement failure: food supply did not fall sharply, but economic pressures kept some groups from buying it.
- **Information Age:** [[Decolonization]], the [[Green Revolution]], the [[Great Acceleration]], the [[Digital Revolution]], [[Chinese Reform and Opening]], and the [[COVID-19 Pandemic]].

## Regional Patterns
- **[[Sub-Saharan Africa]]:** Gold, salt, and ivory sustained the Sahelian empires and Swahili city-states, and the interior states of Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe reached the Indian Ocean; from the 16th century the [[Kingdom of Kongo]] was drawn into the Atlantic slave trade ([[Atlantic Slave Trade]]), and between 1885 and 1908 the [[Congo Free State]] extracted rubber and ivory by forced labor.
- **[[Central Asian Oases]] and the [[Central Asian Steppe]]:** The [[Sogdians]] of the oases were the leading Silk Road merchants, and steppe powers such as the [[Xiongnu]] and the [[Mongol Empire]] taxed, protected, and raided the routes; see [[Samarkand]].
- **[[Southeast Asia]]:** Control of the straits made [[Srivijaya]] and the [[Malacca Sultanate]] rich, [[Champa]] traded across the South China Sea, and later the [[Manila Galleon Trade]] and the [[Dutch East India Company]] (which founded Batavia in 1619) tied the region to European and American markets.
- **[[Andes]]:** [[Caral]] linked coast and highland, the [[Inca Empire]] ran a state economy on labor service and recorded goods on [[Quipu]], and after the Spanish conquest the silver of [[Potosi]], worked under a forced-labor draft adapted from an Inca system, supplied world trade.
- **[[Mesoamerica]]:** [[Domestication of Maize]] made a staple crop for the Americas, [[Teotihuacan]] had wide trade links, and the [[Aztec Empire]] collected tribute from conquered city-states for [[Tenochtitlan]].
- **[[Oceania]]:** The [[Lapita Culture]] and later the [[Polynesians]] moved goods and people across thousands of kilometers of sea, and the [[Treaty of Waitangi]] of 1840 was followed by conflict over land and authority.
- **[[Siberia and the Arctic]]:** European demand for furs drove the [[Russian Expansion into Siberia]] between 1582 and 1639, and the [[Yakut (Sakha)]] paid yasak, a fur tribute, until 1917; the [[Trans-Siberian Railway]] later linked the region to markets. See [[Frontiers of Extraction]].
- **[[East Asia]]:** The Tang and Song economies, the Ming reliance on American silver, Tokugawa restriction of foreign trade, the Opium Wars, and [[Chinese Reform and Opening]] mark shifts between openness and control.

## Debates and Limits
- The origins of markets and money are disputed. Whether exchange began as gift-giving, barter, credit, or tribute is argued, and the earliest written records, such as the tablets of Uruk, show accounting for obligations rather than simple barter.
- The size and reach of early exchange are inferred from scattered goods, and estimates change. The extent and end of the [[Ghana Empire]] are debated, and older population estimates for [[Great Zimbabwe]] of 10,000 to 20,000 have been revised down to no more than about 10,000.
- What counts as trade is contested, because exchange, tribute, and coercion overlap. The mita at [[Potosi]], yasak in Siberia, forced rubber collection in the [[Congo Free State]], and the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]] are extraction, and scholars disagree about how much slave-based commerce contributed to industrialization.
- Why Britain and Europe industrialized first, and why sustained growth began when it did, is debated, and the [[Industrial Revolution]] note does not settle the causes.
- The vault's coverage is uneven. It has one note on a company ([[Dutch East India Company]]) and none yet on coinage, paper money, credit, or double-entry bookkeeping as institutions, and it has few notes on the internal economies of Africa, Oceania, and the Americas.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer calls these exchange-webs and notes that they have no owner or center. Trade repeatedly makes strangers into partners, and transmits pathogens as readily as products. The record suggests that exchange-webs rest on trust that has to be built through shared accounts, coinage, law, and other story-glue, and that they sit beside coercive extraction, such as tribute, forced labor, and the trade in people, which the Observer records in the same ledger rather than in a separate one (see [[Frontiers of Extraction]]). Networks that carried goods also carried ideas, religions, crops, and diseases, so the Observer does not treat them as merely economic. This reading would weaken if long-distance trade proves to have been driven mainly by states and elites, with little of the decentralized quality the term implies.

## Open Questions
- How did early markets and money emerge?
- How much long-distance exchange before writing can be reconstructed from materials alone, and how reliable are the inferences?
- Why did some exchange routes last for centuries, such as the Silk Roads, the Indian Ocean, and the trans-Saharan routes, while others collapsed, as in the [[Bronze Age Collapse]]?
- How did trade, tribute, and coercion interact in empires such as the [[Aztec Empire]], the [[Inca Empire]], the [[Romans]], and the [[Qing Dynasty]]?
- Why did sustained growth in output per person begin when and where it did?
- How far did trade spread disease, from the [[Black Death]] to the [[1918 Influenza Pandemic]] and the [[COVID-19 Pandemic]], and how did societies respond?
