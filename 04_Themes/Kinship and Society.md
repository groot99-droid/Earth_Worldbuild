## Summary
Kinship and society cover how people organize families, descent, marriage, households, communities, and social ranks, and how those arrangements scale from foraging bands to states and beyond. Kin relations are among the most variable features of human cultures. The vault traces them from the small mobile groups of the Paleolithic, through farming villages, chiefdoms, cities, and empires, to the modern struggles over citizenship, equality, and status, and it includes the migrations and conquests that mixed populations along the way. It also covers gender, slavery and freedom, and the ways groups define who belongs.

## Key Threads
- Modern humans ([[Homo sapiens]]) emerged in Africa about 300,000 years ago and spread across the world ([[Out of Africa Dispersal]]). DNA shows contact and interbreeding with other human groups: present-day non-African populations carry up to about 2 percent Neanderthal ancestry ([[Homo neanderthalensis]]), and Oceanian populations carry much more Denisovan ancestry ([[Denisovans]]). Native American ancestry has two roots, East Asian and Ancient North Eurasian ([[Mal'ta and the Ancient North Eurasians]]).
- Foraging peoples lived in small, kin-linked groups for most of human history, and some kept settled village life without farming. [[Aboriginal Australians]] spoke more than 250 languages before 1788 and passed on knowledge orally; the [[Jomon Culture]] lived in settled villages for about 14,000 years before rice farming arrived; and the [[Chinchorro Culture]] preserved men, women, and children of the community, not only an elite.
- Farming brought new forms of property, storage, and inheritance ([[The Agricultural Revolution]]; see [[The Settling and Its Bargain]]). [[Çatalhöyük]] held several thousand people in houses packed together and entered from the roof, with the dead buried under the floors, and some evidence shows that early farmers were shorter and sicker than foragers.
- Cities required cooperation among unrelated people ([[The Urban Revolution]], [[Uruk]], [[Sumerians]]). Written law made the rules visible: the Code of Hammurabi, carved about 1754 BCE, lists 282 laws with penalties that vary by social class ([[Hammurabi]]).
- Women held supreme power in several states, usually through dynastic kinship. [[Hatshepsut]] took the title of pharaoh about 1473 BCE after acting as regent for her stepson; [[Cleopatra VII]] belonged to the Ptolemaic dynasty; [[Wu Zetian]] was the only woman to rule China in her own name (690 to 705 CE); [[Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba]] led resistance to Portuguese expansion; [[Cixi]] ruled as regent for most of the period from 1861 to 1908; and [[Rani Lakshmibai]] ruled Jhansi as regent and led its defense in 1858.
- Kin language and kin structures were used to organize larger unions. The [[Haudenosaunee Confederacy]] is governed under the oral Great Law of Peace, and its clan mothers appoint and remove chiefs; [[Maori]] society was organized around kin groups (whanau, hapu, and iwi); [[Genghis Khan]] built an army on merit and loyalty rather than clan ([[Mongol Empire]]); and [[Timur]], who was not a descendant of Genghis Khan, ruled through a puppet Genghisid khan and took the title güregen (royal son-in-law) through marriage into that line.
- Thinkers and states wrote about the ties that bind. [[Confucius]] stressed humaneness, ritual propriety, and filial piety, and his ideas about family and government shaped East Asian societies for over two thousand years; [[Ibn Khaldun]] argued that group solidarity (asabiyyah) helps found dynasties and weakens as rulers grow comfortable; the [[Inca Empire]] exacted labor service (mit'a) rather than money taxes; and the griots, hereditary oral historians, preserved the story of [[Sundiata Keita]].
- Migrations carried language and kin across regions. Austronesian speakers spread from Taiwan from about 5,000 to 5,500 years ago as far as Madagascar and Rapa Nui ([[Austronesian Expansion]], [[Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]); the [[Bantu Expansion]] involved migration, intermarriage, and language shift, not only population replacement; the [[Indo-European Language Spread]] carried a family now spoken as a first language by roughly 40 to 46 percent of the world's population; and the Thule ancestors of the Inuit largely replaced the earlier Dorset population ([[Inuit and the Thule Expansion]]).
- Herders and river and forest peoples built varied forms of social order. The [[Botai Culture]] hunter-herders of Kazakhstan used horses, the [[Sintashta Culture]] built fortified settlements, and the [[Xiongnu]] were united as a steppe confederation in 209 BCE; in the Amazon, dispersed households, plazas, roads, and gardens formed low-density settlements ([[Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]], [[Upano Valley Settlements]], [[Casarabe Culture]], [[Xingu Garden Cities]]).
- Slavery was central to the Atlantic economy. The [[Atlantic Slave Trade]] forcibly transported about 12.5 million Africans to the Americas, the [[Haitian Revolution]] under leaders such as [[Toussaint Louverture]] created the first republic founded by formerly enslaved people, and the [[Abolition of Slavery]] followed across the Atlantic world between 1807 and 1888.
- The [[Age of Revolutions]] spread ideas of popular sovereignty, citizenship, and nationalism, though slavery and the rights of women were contested and largely excluded at first. See the [[French Revolution]], which adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen in August 1789, the [[Latin American Wars of Independence]], and [[Simón Bolívar]].
- [[Women's Suffrage Movements]] succeeded country by country between the 1890s and the late 20th century, beginning with New Zealand in 1893 and reaching Swiss national elections in 1971; many women were excluded in practice by race or class.
- Colonial rule remade who counted as a citizen or a subject. The [[British Raj]] governed the Indian subcontinent from 1858 to 1947; the [[Scramble for Africa]] drew borders with little regard for existing societies; about 16,000 Cherokee were forced to march west in 1838 to 1839 ([[Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]]); the British claimed Australia under the doctrine of terra nullius, which the High Court rejected in 1992 ([[First Fleet and the Colonization of Australia]]); and the English and Maori texts of the [[Treaty of Waitangi]] differ on key words about sovereignty. The [[Zulu Kingdom]] shows a different path, in which [[Shaka]] turned one of many small Nguni clans into a large military state.
- Persecution and the redrawing of belonging marked the 20th century. See the [[Armenian Genocide]], the [[Holocaust]], the [[Partition of India]], which displaced an estimated 12 to 20 million people, and [[Decolonization]], through which the United Nations grew from 51 members in 1945 to 193. [[Nelson Mandela]] and [[Mahatma Gandhi]] led movements against apartheid and colonial rule.

## Through the Eras
- **Paleolithic:** [[Homo sapiens]], the [[Out of Africa Dispersal]], [[Homo neanderthalensis]], [[Denisovans]], [[Aboriginal Australians]], the [[Peopling of Sahul]], [[Beringia]], [[Mal'ta and the Ancient North Eurasians]], and the [[Peopling of the Americas]], known largely through DNA and scattered sites; kin ties in small groups are inferred rather than recorded.
- **Neolithic:** [[The Agricultural Revolution]], [[Çatalhöyük]], [[Jericho]], [[Chinchorro Culture]], [[Jomon Culture]], [[Botai Culture]], and [[Domestication of Rice]].
- **Bronze Age:** [[The Urban Revolution]], [[Uruk]], [[Sumerians]], [[Hammurabi]], the [[Ancient Egyptians]], the [[Sintashta Culture]], the [[Indo-European Language Spread]], and the [[Austronesian Expansion]].
- **Iron Age:** [[Confucius]], the [[Bantu Expansion]], and the [[Polynesians]].
- **Classical Antiquity:** [[Cleopatra VII]], the [[Xiongnu]], the [[Romans]], who extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants in 212 CE, [[Amazonian Urbanism and Dark Earths]], the [[Upano Valley Settlements]], and the [[Marajoara Culture]].
- **Medieval Period:** [[Wu Zetian]], [[Genghis Khan]] and the [[Mongol Empire]], [[Sundiata Keita]], the [[Maori]], the [[Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]], the [[Inuit and the Thule Expansion]], [[Yakut (Sakha)]], [[Ibn Khaldun]], the [[Inca Empire]], the [[Haudenosaunee Confederacy]], the [[Taino]], the [[Casarabe Culture]], and the [[Xingu Garden Cities]].
- **Early Modern Period:** The [[Atlantic Slave Trade]], [[Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba]], the [[Mapuche]], the [[Taj Mahal]], built by Shah Jahan for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, and the [[Qing Dynasty]], founded by the Manchus.
- **Industrial Age:** The [[Age of Revolutions]], [[Haitian Revolution]], [[Toussaint Louverture]], [[Abolition of Slavery]], [[Zulu Kingdom]] and [[Mfecane]], [[Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears]], [[British Raj]], [[Rani Lakshmibai]], [[Cixi]], [[Emperor Meiji]], [[Women's Suffrage Movements]], [[Scramble for Africa]], [[Congo Free State]], [[Armenian Genocide]], and the [[Holocaust]].
- **Information Age:** [[Decolonization]], the [[Partition of India]], [[Nelson Mandela]], and the [[Genomic Revolution]], whose ancient-DNA work has transformed accounts of ancestry and prehistory.

## Regional Patterns
- **[[Oceania]]:** [[Aboriginal Australians]] spoke more than 250 languages and kept knowledge in oral traditions and songlines, and Polynesian societies ranged from chiefdoms to kingdoms; the [[Maori]] organized around whanau, hapu, and iwi, and the [[Lapita Culture]] carried Austronesian-speaking ancestors of the Polynesians across the western Pacific.
- **[[Andes]] and the [[Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]]:** The [[Chinchorro Culture]] treated the whole community alike in death, the [[Inca Empire]] ran a state on labor service, the [[Mapuche]] kept their independence from both the Inca and the Spanish for centuries, and Amazonian peoples built low-density settlements with plazas, roads, and gardens.
- **[[Sub-Saharan Africa]]:** The [[Bantu Expansion]] spread languages and farming over about four thousand years; the [[Mali Empire]] founder [[Sundiata Keita]] is known largely through griots; the [[Kingdom of Benin]] was ruled by an oba with guilds of craftsmen; and the [[Zulu Kingdom]] grew from clans into a military state.
- **[[Central Asian Steppe]]:** The [[Xiongnu]] confederation, the merit-based army of [[Genghis Khan]], and [[Timur]]'s claim to a Genghisid place through marriage show how steppe rulers combined descent with loyalty.
- **[[Siberia and the Arctic]]:** [[Beringia]] joined Siberia and Alaska, and genetic evidence suggests, though this is debated, that ancestors of Native Americans were isolated there for several thousand years; the [[Inuit and the Thule Expansion]] and the [[Yakut (Sakha)]] mark later herding and hunting societies.
- **[[Southeast Asia]]:** Austronesian speakers moved through the Philippines and Indonesia into Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia, relying on outrigger sailing canoes and a crop package that included rice, pigs, dogs, and chickens ([[Austronesian Expansion]]).
- **[[East Asia]] and [[South Asia]]:** [[Confucius]] made the family a model for government, the [[Qing Dynasty]] was founded by the Manchus, and the [[British Raj]] and the [[Partition of India]] reshaped identity in South Asia.
- **[[North America]] and the [[Caribbean and Atlantic World]]:** The [[Haudenosaunee Confederacy]] joined nations under an oral constitution, the [[Taino]] lived in villages ruled by chiefs called caciques, and slavery and its abolition transformed the Atlantic world ([[Atlantic Slave Trade]], [[Haitian Revolution]]).

## Debates and Limits
- Whether large-scale societies replaced kin-based organization or built on it is disputed. Dynasties, clans, and confederations rested on descent and marriage even inside states, and recent work argues that early societies varied more in their forms of organization than older stage models of bands, tribes, chiefdoms, and states allowed.
- Social organization in prehistory is known indirectly, from house plans, burials, and DNA, and each can be read in more than one way; the layout of [[Çatalhöyük]], for example, is compatible with several kinds of household and community organization.
- Genes, language, and archaeology do not map neatly onto one another. The homeland of the [[Indo-European Language Spread]] is disputed, the [[Bantu Expansion]] involved intermarriage and language shift as well as movement, and how contact between Thule and Dorset peoples proceeded is not established.
- Numbers are uncertain: the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]] transported about 12.5 million people, of whom about 10.7 million are recorded as surviving the voyage, and deaths in the [[Partition of India]] are commonly given as 1 to 2 million, with some sources higher.
- The vault's coverage is uneven. Most of its People notes are rulers, scholars, and reformers, and it has no notes yet on marriage systems, caste, feudalism, or urban class structure as topics, and few on the lives of women, children, and non-elite people.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer calls this the kin-lattice. It notes that the species can extend kin language, such as 'brother' and 'motherland', to groups of millions. The record suggests that the lattice is reused rather than replaced as societies grow: dynasties, clans, and confederations rest on descent and marriage, rulers such as Timur took titles by marrying into an older line, and modern states appeal to kin words while resting on law. Rules of descent, marriage, and status also decide who counts as a full person, and the record shows them changing unevenly, often through long struggles such as those over slavery and suffrage. This reading would weaken if large-scale societies prove to run almost entirely on impersonal institutions, with kin language serving only as decoration.

## Open Questions
- How did large-scale societies replace kin-based organization, or did they build on it?
- How varied was social organization among early foragers and farmers, and what do house plans, burials, and DNA say about rank and equality?
- How did descent and marriage rules shape the spread of languages and peoples such as the Bantu, Austronesian, and Indo-European speakers?
- How did societies decide who counted as a full person, and how did those boundaries change through slavery, citizenship, and suffrage?
- What can be recovered of the lives of women, children, and non-elite people from sources written mostly by and about elites?
- How have DNA studies changed accounts of ancestry, migration, and belonging, and how should groups' own accounts be weighed alongside them?
