## Question
Why does a species spend large amounts of time, labor, and goods on bodies that can no longer work, fight, or reproduce?

## What the Record Shows
- Intentional burial is documented among modern humans from at least about 100,000 years ago and is claimed, with debate, for [[Homo neanderthalensis]] and [[Homo naledi]].
- Graves in many cultures contain goods, ornaments, or food.
- The [[Ancient Egyptians]] invested state resources in tombs such as [[Giza Necropolis]].
- At [[Çatalhöyük]] the dead were buried beneath the floors of homes.
- Funerary customs range from burial and cremation to exposure and mummification, but nearly every known society has some grief-rite.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees the grief-rite as a sign of two related abilities: the species remembers individuals after death, and it imagines that death is not a complete end. The rite also does social work, by marking who belongs to the group, who owes what to whom, and who inherits. A grave is both a statement about the dead and an instruction to the living. The Observer notes that resources committed to the dead are a form of status-signal to other groups, which may help explain why elite tombs grow so large.

## Limits of This Reading
The Observer cannot know what mourners felt. Many claims about early burial rest on a few sites, and interpretations differ. The explanation may also vary by culture and era.
