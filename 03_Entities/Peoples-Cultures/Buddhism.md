## Summary
Buddhism began in northeastern India between the sixth and fourth centuries BCE with the teaching of Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, and spread across Asia into several major traditions, teaching a path out of suffering through insight and practice rather than through a creator god.

## Facts
- Siddhartha Gautama, born a member of a ruling family in the region of present-day Nepal and India, is said to have reached enlightenment and began teaching in his mid-thirties.
- His core teaching, the Four Noble Truths, describes suffering, its cause in craving, its end, and a path to that end, aiming at release from the cycle of rebirth, called nirvana.
- The faith spread through missionary activity, trade routes and royal patronage, notably under the Mauryan emperor Ashoka.
- It developed into three major living traditions: Theravada, dominant in Sri Lanka and mainland Southeast Asia; Mahayana, dominant in China, Japan, Korea and Vietnam; and Vajrayana, dominant in Tibet, Bhutan and Mongolia.
- Buddhism has roughly 500 million adherents today, about 6 percent of the world's population.

## Context & Connections
Buddhism arose in [[South Asia]] during the [[Iron Age]] with the teaching of [[Siddhartha Gautama]], part of the broader [[Axial Age]] of new philosophical and religious movements, and spread widely under patrons such as [[Ashoka]]. See [[Religion and Belief]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that this meaning-engine spread across a continent not primarily by conquest but by teaching, trade and a ruler's patronage, and diversified into traditions different enough in practice that observers sometimes debate whether to treat them as one religion. A specialist could argue that Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana differ enough in text, practice and cosmology that grouping them under one name obscures more than it reveals, which would weaken any single account of "Buddhist belief."

## Open Questions
