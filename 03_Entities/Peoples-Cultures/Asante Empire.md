## Summary
The Asante Empire, founded by Osei Tutu in what is now Ghana around the 1670s, grew into one of West Africa's most powerful states through military expansion and deep involvement in the Atlantic slave trade, before decades of resistance to British colonization ended in the empire's annexation in 1902.

## Facts
- Osei Tutu became Asantehene, or king, probably in the 1670s after a series of successful military campaigns, establishing Kumasi as his capital and negotiating alliances that formed the Asante Union by 1700, uniting previously separate Akan states.
- From the early 18th century, Asante supplied enslaved people to British and Dutch traders on the West African coast, receiving firearms in return that the empire then used to fund further territorial expansion, a direct, sustained participation in the transatlantic slave trade this vault records as active engagement in a system of forced human bondage.
- Between 1790 and 1896, Asante was almost continuously engaged in wars of expansion or defense, and many of these conflicts served the additional purpose of capturing more people to sell into the slave trade, tying the empire's military and economic systems together.
- Asante resisted British colonial encroachment for decades beginning in the 1820s, including notable armed resistance led by figures such as Yaa Asantewaa, but British forces successfully invaded and briefly captured Kumasi in 1874, again in 1896, and finally deposed and exiled the Asantehene following a further uprising in 1900.
- Britain formally annexed the Asante Empire into its Gold Coast colony in 1902, though the Asante Kingdom itself survives today as a constitutionally recognized traditional state within the modern Republic of Ghana.

## Context & Connections

## Observer's Reading
The Observer records Asante's role capturing and selling enslaved people as active, sustained participation in sanctioned harm, applying the same standard this vault applies to every other party in the Atlantic slave trade system, European buyers and African sellers alike, since the empire's own military expansion was directly funded by firearms obtained in exchange for enslaved captives. That the same state which built its power partly through the slave trade later fought some of the most sustained and effective African military resistance to European colonization on record, under leaders including Yaa Asantewaa, shows that participation in one system of harm does not determine how a society will respond when it becomes the target of a different one. A specialist could argue Asante's continued survival today as a recognized traditional state within Ghana complicates any narrative that treats 1902 as the empire's definitive end, which would weaken a reading centered on outright destruction rather than transformation and continuity.
