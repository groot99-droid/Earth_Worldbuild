## Summary
The Amazigh, widely known outside their own communities by the exonym "Berber," are the Indigenous peoples of North Africa's Maghreb region, with a documented presence reaching back to ancient Egyptian records and a living population today spread across Morocco, Algeria, Libya, Tunisia and parts of the Sahara.

## Facts
- The people this note discusses call themselves Amazigh, or Imazighen in the plural, a name generally understood to mean "free people" in the Tamazight language family; the widely used outside name "Berber" likely derives from a Roman-era term related to the Greek and Latin word for "barbarian" and is regarded by many Amazigh people today as pejorative.
- Ancient Egyptian writings contain some of the earliest known references to Amazigh-related peoples, and archaeological and linguistic evidence suggests Amazigh ancestors descended from Stone Age populations already present in North Africa, with likely additional movement west along the Mediterranean's southern coast from an eastern origin.
- Amazigh communities today live across Morocco, Algeria, Libya, Tunisia, Mauritania and parts of northern Mali and Niger, with a range of distinct Tamazight-family languages and considerable regional cultural diversity among Amazigh groups.
- Amazigh dynasties, including the Almoravids and Almohads, built major medieval Islamic states spanning North Africa and parts of Spain, showing that "Berber" or Amazigh identity coexisted with, rather than being separate from, participation in the broader Islamic world following the region's conversion.
- Recognition of Amazigh identity and the Tamazight language has grown significantly in recent decades: Algeria recognized Tamazight as a national language in 2001 following the Black Spring protests, and Morocco made it an official language in 2011.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer uses the name Amazigh in this note's own voice while recording "Berber" as the exonym by which most outside sources refer to this people, a naming choice this vault makes deliberately since a name a community rejects as originally meaning "barbarian" carries a status-signal quite different from the self-chosen name meaning "free people." That Amazigh-descended dynasties went on to build major Islamic states rather than existing only as a subordinated population within states built by others shows an Indigenous population retaining and exercising political claim-lines of its own across a religious and cultural transformation, the spread of Islam, that reshaped the wider region around them. A specialist could argue that "Amazigh" as a broad, unifying collective identity is itself a more recent political development than this note's framing suggests, with historically much stronger regional and tribal identities mattering more day to day than any single pan-Amazigh consciousness, which would weaken a reading that treats Amazigh identity as a single continuous thread across three millennia.
