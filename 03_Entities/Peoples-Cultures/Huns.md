## Summary
The Huns were a nomadic, multi-ethnic confederation that appeared on the Volga steppe around 370 CE and, under Attila, built a short-lived but formidable empire across Central and Eastern Europe before collapsing within two decades of his death.

## Facts
- Where the Huns originated is genuinely disputed: some historians connect them to the Xiongnu confederation that had troubled China centuries earlier, others place their more immediate origins in Central Asia, and genetic studies show the European Hunnic population was itself highly mixed rather than descended from one migrating group.
- The Huns' arrival on the Volga around 370 CE set off a chain reaction, pushing Gothic and other peoples toward the Roman frontier and contributing to the pressures that weakened the Western Roman Empire.
- Attila ruled jointly with his brother Bleda from 434 CE until Bleda's death in 445, after which Attila ruled alone, leading campaigns deep into the Eastern and Western Roman Empires and extracting large tribute payments.
- The Hunnic Empire was a loose, multi-ethnic tribal confederation rather than a centralized state, holding together numerous subject peoples more through Attila's personal authority and military success than through shared institutions.
- After Attila's death in 453, his sons fought over the succession, subject peoples revolted, and the confederation fragmented within about fifteen years, after which the Huns largely disappeared from the historical record as a distinct group.

## Context & Connections

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Huns held together an exchange-web of subject peoples through one man's personal authority rather than through the kind of institutions that outlast a single ruler, so that the confederation's collapse within fifteen years of Attila's death shows how fragile a claim-line built on reputation and fear alone can be once the person behind it is gone. That genetic and historical evidence both point to a genuinely mixed, multi-ethnic population rather than one migrating tribe complicates any simple story of "the Huns" as a single people invading Europe. A specialist could argue the confederation's speed of collapse reflects standard steppe political organization rather than any special fragility, which would weaken a reading centered on Attila's individual authority.

## Open Questions
- Where precisely did the peoples who became the European Huns originate, and how directly are they connected to the Xiongnu?
- How much of Hunnic material culture and social organization can be reconstructed given how little the Huns themselves left in writing?
