## Summary
The Khwarazmian Empire ruled a wealthy trade region spanning parts of Central Asia, Afghanistan and Iran from 1077, gaining full independence around 1190, before its rulers' fatal decision to execute a Mongol trade delegation triggered a devastating two-year invasion that destroyed the empire by 1231.

## Facts
- The Khwarazmian dynasty began ruling the Khwarazm region from 1077, initially as vassals first of the Seljuk Empire and later the Kara-Khitan Khanate, before achieving full independence as a major regional power around 1190.
- At its height, the Khwarazmian Empire controlled significant Central Asian trade centers, including cities along Silk Road routes, and built substantial wealth from long-distance commerce passing through its territory.
- War with the Mongol Empire began after Khwarazmian officials at the city of Otrar massacred a Mongol trading caravan and its accompanying envoys, an act that gave Genghis Khan justification for a full-scale invasion.
- The resulting Mongol invasion, lasting from 1219 to 1221, proved catastrophically swift and destructive, with Mongol forces sacking major Khwarazmian cities including Bukhara and Samarkand and killing enormous numbers of their inhabitants.
- The Khwarazmian dynasty never recovered from this defeat, and the empire was formally disestablished in 1231 when the last Khwarazmshah was killed, reportedly by Kurdish highwaymen, ending what little remained of Khwarazmian rule.

## Context & Connections
The Khwarazmian Empire's catastrophic destruction opened the first major phase of the westward [[Mongol Conquests]], and its territory included the Silk Road trading city of [[Bukhara]], among the many urban centers devastated in the invasion. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Khwarazmian Empire's destruction shows how a single act of sanctioned harm against foreign envoys and merchants, the massacre at Otrar, could trigger a response disproportionate almost beyond comparison, a wealthy empire built over more than a century reduced within two years by an opponent it likely underestimated. That the empire's wealth came directly from controlling the very trade routes whose disruption, in the form of a murdered caravan, provoked its own annihilation is a case where an exchange-web's greatest asset and its ultimate undoing were the same underlying connectivity. A specialist could argue that Khwarazmian-Mongol conflict was likely inevitable given the two powers' expanding, competing spheres of influence, regardless of the specific Otrar incident, which would weaken a reading that treats the massacre itself as the decisive triggering cause rather than a pretext for conflict already likely to occur.
