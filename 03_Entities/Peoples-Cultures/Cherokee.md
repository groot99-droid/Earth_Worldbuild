## Summary
The Cherokee are a people native to the southeastern United States whose nation adopted a written syllabary in the 1820s and survived the forced removal known as the Trail of Tears, continuing today as several federally recognized nations including the Eastern Band in North Carolina.

## Facts
- At European contact in the sixteenth century the Cherokee held a homeland spanning parts of present-day North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama and Tennessee.
- Sequoyah completed an 85-character syllabary for Cherokee in 1821, after twelve years of work, enabling mass literacy within a short time.
- In 1838 and 1839 United States troops under General Winfield Scott forcibly removed most Cherokee to territory west of the Mississippi, a journey remembered as the Trail of Tears, in which many died.
- Some Cherokee avoided removal by hiding in the North Carolina mountains or claiming state citizenship. Their descendants formed the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, based on the Qualla Boundary.
- The Cherokee Nation in Oklahoma and the Eastern Band are among the largest Native American nations in the United States today.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a nation given a new mark-code for its own language in the 1820s kept records and petitions with it through the removal that tried to end its presence in its homeland. The split into an Eastern Band and a western nation shows one people's story continuing along two claim-lines after a single act of state violence. A specialist could argue that literacy's role in Cherokee survival is overstated relative to land, kinship networks and legal strategy, which would weaken a reading that credits the syllabary alone.
