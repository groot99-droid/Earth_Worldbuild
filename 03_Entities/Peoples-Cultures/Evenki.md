## Summary
The Evenki are an Indigenous Tungusic-speaking people of Siberia, numbering around 70,000 today across a vast territory between the Urals and the Pacific, whose ancestors developed reindeer-riding pastoralism and who continue practicing reindeer herding, hunting and fishing despite centuries of pressure toward assimilation.

## Facts
- The Evenki's ancestors likely inhabited the area between Lake Baikal and the Amur River from as early as the Neolithic period, and they are considered, along with Samoyedic-speaking peoples, among the groups responsible for the original domestication of the reindeer.
- Evenki people developed a distinctive method of riding domesticated reindeer, using a specialized saddle, a technique they adopted roughly 400 years ago that let them travel and hunt across the rugged taiga terrain of Siberia far more effectively than on foot.
- The Evenk are the most widely dispersed of Siberia's many small Indigenous peoples, living in small, scattered communities across a territory stretching from the Ural Mountains to the Pacific Ocean and from the Arctic Ocean south to the Mongolian steppe, with most of today's roughly 70,000 Evenki divided between Russia and China.
- Evenki society traditionally organized itself into patrilineal clans, and the Evenki language belongs to the Manchu-Tungusic language family, related to other Indigenous Siberian languages including Even and Negidal.
- Since the 17th century, Evenki communities have faced sustained pressure toward assimilation into Russian and Chinese culture, intensifying under Soviet-era policies of forced sedentarization that specifically targeted the nomadic, reindeer-herding way of life central to Evenki tradition.

## Context & Connections
Evenki communities across Siberia were drawn into sustained contact with, and later assimilation pressure from, the state structures that expanded through the [[Russian Expansion into Siberia]] from the 17th century onward. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer records the Soviet-era forced sedentarization of Evenki communities as a form of sanctioned harm directed against a specific way of life, heat-domestication and tool-lineage adapted over many centuries to a nomadic reindeer economy that a distant state judged inconvenient to govern. That Evenki reindeer-riding technique, adopted only about four centuries ago according to current evidence, transformed the group's relationship to an already ancient domesticated animal shows that even a very old human-animal partnership can still undergo major technological change relatively recently in its history. This reading would weaken if further ethnographic and historical evidence shows Evenki communities retained substantially more continuity in their traditional practices through the Soviet period than the forced-sedentarization narrative suggests.
