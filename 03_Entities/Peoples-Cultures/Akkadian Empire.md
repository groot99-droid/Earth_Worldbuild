## Summary
The Akkadian Empire, founded by Sargon of Akkad around 2334 BCE, united the Sumerian city-states of southern Mesopotamia under one ruler and is widely regarded as the world's first empire, extending its reach from the Persian Gulf toward the Mediterranean before collapsing about 180 years later.

## Facts
- Sargon, whose rise from relatively humble origins to king is recorded in later Mesopotamian tradition, founded the city of Akkad and, from there, conquered the independent Sumerian city-states of southern Mesopotamia, uniting them under a single central authority for the first time.
- Sargon's campaigns extended Akkadian influence into parts of Syria, Anatolia and Elam in western Iran, and later Akkadian rulers, including his grandson Naram-Sin, continued to expand and defend this reach through further military campaigns.
- The Akkadian language, a Semitic language distinct from Sumerian, spread as the empire's language of administration, and this period saw Akkadian and Sumerian coexist and blend in official records and literature across Mesopotamia.
- Akkadian rule introduced administrative practices, standardized weights, measures and a more centralized bureaucracy, that later Mesopotamian states, including Babylon, continued to draw on.
- The empire declined and collapsed by around 2154 BCE, a process historians attribute to a combination of internal rebellion, pressure from surrounding peoples and, according to some researchers, a severe regional drought that undermined agricultural production, though the relative importance of these causes remains debated.

## Context & Connections
The Akkadian Empire united the city-states of the [[Sumerians]] under central rule for the first time, and its administrative innovations shaped the practices later continued by [[Babylon]] and other Mesopotamian successor states. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Sargon's empire shows the first recorded instance of a pattern this vault records again and again afterward: a single ruler's military success converting a patchwork of independent city-states into one administered claim-line, with a centralized language and bureaucracy imposed on top of older local traditions rather than replacing them outright. That the empire's own collapse is blamed partly on drought, alongside more familiar causes such as rebellion and external pressure, is a reminder that even the earliest political tool-lineage of empire-building was never insulated from earth-systems beyond any ruler's control. A specialist could argue that internal succession struggles and rebellion, well documented in the surviving record, mattered more than climate in the empire's fall, which would weaken a reading that treats drought as a primary cause.
