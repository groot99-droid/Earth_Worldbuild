## Summary
The Golden Horde was the western successor state of the Mongol Empire, founded by Batu Khan and ruling over vast territories of the Russian steppe and beyond from its capital at Sarai, exacting tribute from Russian principalities for over two centuries until Moscow finally cast off Mongol overlordship in 1480.

## Facts
- Batu Khan, a grandson of Genghis Khan, led Mongol forces on a devastating westward campaign beginning in 1237, sacking Ryazan, destroying the then-small town of Moscow, devastating Vladimir, and capturing Kiev by 1240, before formally establishing the Golden Horde as a khanate in 1242.
- The Golden Horde ruled from its capital at Sarai, near modern Astrakhan, and Russian princes were required to travel there to receive a khan-issued charter, or yarlyk, formally confirming their right to rule and their obligation to pay tribute.
- The Golden Horde converted to Islam under Khan Öz Beg in the 14th century, shifting the khanate's religious identity while its rulers continued exacting tribute and confirming Russian princely authority for another century and more.
- Golden Horde power gradually weakened through internal succession disputes, plague losses, and the rising strength of Muscovy under a succession of ambitious Russian princes.
- Ivan the Great of Moscow formally ended tribute payments and diplomatic recognition of the khanate in 1476, and after a final standoff, Khan Ahmad's retreating forces saw their leader assassinated by a rival in early 1481, an episode Russian historiography treats as marking Russia's definitive liberation from Mongol overlordship.

## Context & Connections

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Golden Horde ruled Russian territory for over two centuries not primarily through direct administration but through a claim-line maintained by paperwork and periodic tribute, requiring princes to travel to Sarai for a document that could be granted or withheld, a form of control that let a comparatively small ruling elite extract wealth from a much larger subject population without needing to govern it directly day to day. That "liberation" from this system is remembered in Russian historiography as a single dramatic standoff and retreat in 1480-1481, when the underlying weakening of Mongol power had in fact been building for generations through succession disputes and Muscovy's own rising strength, shows how a gradual shift in real power can later be compressed into one memorable date in national memory. A specialist could argue that framing 1480 as the decisive end of "the Mongol yoke" overstates the abruptness of a transition that was already well underway for decades, which would weaken a reading that treats it as a single sharp turning point.

## Open Questions

- How did the Golden Horde's conversion to Islam under Öz Beg change its relationship with the Orthodox Christian Russian principalities it ruled?
