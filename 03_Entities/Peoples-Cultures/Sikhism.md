## Summary
Sikhism began in the Punjab region of South Asia with the teaching of Guru Nanak, born in 1469, and developed through a line of ten human teachers into a monotheistic tradition with its own scripture and, since 1699, a formal order of initiated members called the Khalsa.

## Facts
- Guru Nanak (1469 to 1539) taught belief in one God, social equality across caste lines, and rejection of ritualism, gathering the first Sikh community in Punjab.
- Nine more teachers followed him, together the Ten Gurus, regarded by Sikhs as sharing one continuous spirit of guidance.
- In 1699 the tenth guru, Gobind Singh, founded the Khalsa, a discipline of initiated Sikhs marked by the Five Ks, articles of faith including uncut hair, organized in part to defend the faith.
- Before his death, Gobind Singh declared that Sikhs should treat their scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib, a compilation of hymns by the gurus and other spiritual figures, as their living guru, ending the line of human gurus.
- About 25 to 30 million Sikhs live today, mostly in Punjab, India, where they form a majority of the region's population, with further communities across the global Sikh diaspora.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that when the line of living teachers ended in 1699, the community shifted memory-outsourcing onto a book, declaring a text itself a guru, an unusual solution other traditions solve through institutions or bloodlines. The Khalsa also shows a meaning-engine adopting sanctioned self-defense as part of its identity in response to persecution. A specialist could argue that ending the line of human gurus reflected particular circumstances of Gobind Singh's own time as much as settled theology, which would weaken a reading of it as inevitable.
