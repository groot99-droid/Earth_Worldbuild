## Summary
The Chimú built the kingdom of Chimor along Peru's northern coast from around 900 CE, ruling from the enormous adobe capital of Chan Chan and eventually controlling roughly 1,300 kilometers of coastline, before the expanding Inca Empire conquered them in 1470 by cutting off Chan Chan's water supply.

## Facts
- Chimú civilization developed along Peru's northern coast beginning around 900 CE, with the capital city of Chan Chan believed to have been constructed starting around 850 CE, eventually growing into an estimated population of 40,000 to 60,000 people.
- Chan Chan was not a single unified monument but an entire urban landscape of nine massive royal palace compounds, or ciudadelas, each functioning as a largely self-contained citadel with its own administrative halls, ceremonial courtyards, royal tombs and reservoir gardens.
- Under the ruler Minchançaman around 1400 CE, Chimú influence and control reached its greatest extent, stretching approximately 1,300 kilometers along the northern Peruvian coastline, making Chimor one of the largest pre-Inca states in the Andes.
- Chimú artisans were renowned for sophisticated metalworking, particularly in gold, silver and copper alloys, along with fine ceramic and textile production that reflected the wealth and organizational capacity of the Chimor state.
- The Inca ruler Tupac Inca Yupanqui led a military campaign that conquered the Chimú around 1470 CE, with Chimú leaders ultimately surrendering only after Inca forces cut off Chan Chan's water supply, a siege tactic that ended Chimú independence roughly six decades before Spanish conquest reached the Andes.

## Context & Connections
Chimú civilization developed along the same Peruvian coastline earlier shaped by the [[Moche Culture]], and its rise to regional dominance occurred as influence from the declining [[Wari Empire]] receded from the northern coast. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Inca conquest of Chan Chan through cutting off its water supply, rather than through direct assault on its massive palace compounds, shows a claim-line broken not by overwhelming its defenses but by attacking the basic earth-systems dependency, water, that made the city's dense urban population possible at all, a tactic this vault records as a recurring pattern wherever a desert or arid-climate city's vulnerability to siege depended more on its water infrastructure than its walls. That Chan Chan's nine separate royal compounds, each apparently built anew by a successive ruler rather than simply inherited and expanded, suggests a political system built around each new ruler establishing an independent claim-line through fresh monumental construction rather than one accumulating single palace complex, a pattern of dynastic self-assertion this vault records in some other societies as well. A specialist could argue that the nine-compound structure of Chan Chan reflects administrative or religious functions distinct from simple sequential royal succession, which would weaken a reading that treats each compound primarily as evidence of individual rulers asserting independent legitimacy through new construction.
