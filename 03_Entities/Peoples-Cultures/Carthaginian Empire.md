## Summary
Carthage, founded by Phoenician settlers from Tyre around 814 BCE on the North African coast, grew into the wealthiest and most powerful state in the western Mediterranean before three devastating wars with Rome ended in the city's total destruction in 146 BCE.

## Facts
- Phoenician settlers from the city of Tyre founded Carthage around 814 BCE on the North African coast near modern Tunis; tradition credits the Phoenician queen Dido (Elissa) with the founding, though her individual historicity is not established with certainty.
- By the time conflict with Rome began, Carthage had become the largest, wealthiest and most powerful political entity in the Mediterranean, built on extensive maritime trade networks reaching across North Africa, Iberia, Sicily and Sardinia.
- Carthage and Rome fought three wars, known as the Punic Wars, from 264 to 146 BCE, a term deriving from the Roman name "Poeni" for the Carthaginians, referencing their Phoenician origin.
- The wars saw dramatic reversals of fortune on both sides, including the Carthaginian general Hannibal's famous invasion of Italy across the Alps during the Second Punic War, before Rome ultimately gained the decisive upper hand.
- The Third Punic War ended in 146 BCE when Roman forces stormed Carthage after a prolonged siege, and after seven days of intense fighting the city surrendered; Roman forces killed or enslaved its population and completely demolished the city, ending nearly 700 years of Carthaginian civilization.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer records the killing and enslavement of Carthage's population in 146 BCE as suffering on a scale meant to erase, not merely defeat, a rival claim-line, a documented case where the victor's goal was not conquest and continued exploitation but total removal of a competing exchange-web from the historical map. That Carthage had been the Mediterranean's wealthiest power within living memory of its own destruction shows how completely a dominant civilization's material success can fail to protect it once a rival commits to its total elimination rather than negotiated subordination. A specialist could argue that Roman accounts of the Third Punic War's brutality may be somewhat exaggerated or shaped by later Roman self-justification, which would weaken confidence in the precise details, though not the basic fact, of Carthage's destruction.
