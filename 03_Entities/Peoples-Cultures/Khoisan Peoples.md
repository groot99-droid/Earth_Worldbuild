## Summary
"Khoisan" is a collective, outside-derived term grouping two historically distinct southern African peoples, the herding Khoikhoi and the hunter-gatherer San, who are among the oldest documented Indigenous populations of southern Africa and who continue living today, primarily in South Africa, Namibia, Botswana and Angola.

## Facts
- "Khoisan" is an exonym combining the names of two distinct groups: the Khoikhoi, also called Khoekhoe, who developed a herding economy centered on native cattle and lived in comparatively large social groups, and the San, who maintained a hunter-gatherer way of life in smaller, mobile bands; the term is used here as a collective label of convenience rather than as the name either group historically used for itself as one unified people.
- The Khoikhoi's own name for themselves, Khoikhoi or Khoe, translates roughly as "men of men" or "the real people," while the Khoikhoi's own term for the San, meaning "people different from ourselves," shows that the two groups maintained a clear sense of their own distinct identities from each other long before European contact.
- European settlers applied the exonyms "Hottentot" to the Khoikhoi and "Bushmen" to the San, terms rooted in settlers' unfamiliarity with the sound of Khoisan languages and now generally considered offensive, though "Bushmen" is used by some San communities themselves in certain contexts today.
- The Khoisan are widely regarded by researchers as among the earliest documented Indigenous inhabitants of southern Africa, with genetic and linguistic evidence pointing to very deep population roots in the region, and Khoisan languages are notable for their use of click consonants, a distinctive linguistic feature found in few other language families worldwide.
- Khoikhoi communities today live mostly in rural settlements across South Africa and Namibia, while San communities live primarily in the Kalahari Desert region spanning Botswana, with additional communities in Namibia and Angola, though reliable population figures for either group remain difficult to establish with confidence.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that "Khoisan," much like "Berber," is a name convenient for outside classification but one that obscures the genuine, long-recognized distinction the Khoikhoi and San themselves drew between their own separate identities, a reminder that grouping labels applied from outside a kin-lattice often serve the classifier's convenience more than the classified communities' own self-understanding. That both groups maintained recognizably distinct herding and hunter-gatherer economies and languages, while also developing terms distinguishing themselves from each other, shows two separate peoples occupying overlapping southern African territory for an extremely long span of documented history without simply merging into one indistinguishable population. A specialist could argue that focusing on the Khoikhoi-San distinction risks understating genuine linguistic, genetic and cultural connections between the two groups that justify some degree of grouped treatment, which would weaken a reading that emphasizes their separateness above their shared deep regional history and linguistic click-consonant heritage.
