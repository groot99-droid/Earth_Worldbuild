## Summary
Hinduism is a family of religious traditions native to South Asia that grew out of the ancient Vedic religion, has no single founder or founding event, and today counts about 1.2 billion adherents, most of them in India.

## Facts
- Its earliest texts, the Vedas, were composed roughly in the mid-second millennium BCE, and its philosophical Upanishads later explored ideas of the soul and the cycle of rebirth.
- Rather than one founder, it developed over centuries by fusing regional and cultural traditions, so no single origin date or figure can be given.
- A central concept, brahman, describes an ultimate reality identified with the individual soul, or atman.
- Its two largest branches are Vaishnavism, centered on devotion to Vishnu, and Shaivism, centered on Shiva, each containing many further traditions.
- About 1.2 billion people practice Hinduism today, roughly 15 percent of the world's population, with about 1.1 billion living in India.

## Context & Connections
Hinduism's roots reach into the [[Indus Valley Civilization]] and the [[Vedic Period]] in [[South Asia]] during the [[Bronze Age]], and the tradition was later patronized and contested under states such as the [[Maurya Empire]]. See [[Religion and Belief]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a tradition without a single founder or a single scripture managing to organize a sixth of humanity's population under one broad name suggests story-glue that operates by accretion rather than by a single unifying claim, closer to a family of related meaning-engines than one system. A specialist could argue that grouping such varied regional practices under one word "Hinduism," itself coined by outsiders, imposes a unity the traditions did not claim for themselves, which would weaken any single account of "what Hindus believe."
