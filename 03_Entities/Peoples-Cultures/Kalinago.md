## Summary
The Kalinago, called Caribs by Europeans, are an Indigenous people of the Lesser Antilles who resisted colonization for nearly two centuries after Columbus, and today live mainly in the Kalinago Territory on Dominica.

## Facts
- The Kalinago descend from earlier inhabitants of the Lesser Antilles and were there before Columbus reached the region in 1493.
- They gained a reputation as effective resistance fighters, and their raids helped keep Europeans from permanent settlement on some islands for close to two centuries.
- Dominica's mountainous volcanic interior gave them ground colonizers could not easily penetrate, and the island became a refuge for other Indigenous people fleeing colonization.
- France claimed Dominica in 1635, but Kalinago resistance blocked permanent settlement for a time. Catholic missionaries arriving in 1642 recorded Kalinago language and life.
- Kalinago raids on plantations on neighboring islands continued into the eighteenth century, and their descendants remain in the Kalinago Territory today.

## Context & Connections
The Kalinago inhabited the [[Caribbean and Atlantic World]] into the [[Early Modern Period]], sharing the region with the [[Taino]] and resisting the disruptions of the [[Columbian Exchange]] longer than most neighbors. See [[War and Conflict]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that difficult terrain, more than any single tactic, let one Caribbean people hold off colonization for two centuries after most of the region had fallen, then drew refugees from neighboring islands, making a stronghold a shared last refuge. Their presence today contradicts accounts that treat the Caribbean's Indigenous peoples as vanished. A specialist could argue that European rivalries left gaps in colonization as much as Kalinago strength did, which would weaken a reading that credits resistance alone.
