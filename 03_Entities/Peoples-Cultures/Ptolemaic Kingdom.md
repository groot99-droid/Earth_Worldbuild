## Summary
The Ptolemaic Kingdom was a Greek-Macedonian dynasty that ruled Egypt from 305 to 30 BCE, founded by Alexander the Great's general Ptolemy I Soter and ending with the death of Cleopatra VII, blending Greek and Egyptian traditions from its capital at Alexandria.

## Facts
- Alexander the Great took Egypt from Achaemenid Persian control in 332 BCE, and after his death in 323 BCE his general Ptolemy secured Egypt as his own territory, formally declaring himself king in 305 BCE.
- Ptolemy I built Alexandria into a major center of Hellenistic culture and learning, founding institutions traditionally associated with the Lighthouse of Alexandria and the Great Library of Alexandria.
- The Ptolemaic rulers, though ethnically Macedonian Greek, adopted many pharaonic customs and religious practices to legitimize their rule over Egypt's much larger native population, presenting themselves in Egyptian art and ritual as traditional pharaohs.
- The dynasty ruled Egypt for nearly three centuries, marked by periods of prosperity from Mediterranean and Red Sea trade alongside dynastic infighting, including repeated succession struggles among Ptolemy's descendants.
- Cleopatra VII, the dynasty's last ruler, allied with Rome's Mark Antony; their combined forces were defeated by Octavian at the Battle of Actium in 31 BCE, and following their suicides in 30 BCE, Egypt became a Roman province, ending Ptolemaic rule.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Ptolemies solved a claim-line problem common to conquerors ruling a much larger native population: they wore Egyptian religious meaning as a costume over Greek political and cultural identity, appearing as traditional pharaohs in temple reliefs while running the state largely along Hellenistic administrative lines, a status-signal deliberately aimed at two audiences at once. That this dual identity sustained Ptolemaic rule for nearly three centuries, longer than most conquest dynasties manage, suggests the strategy worked reasonably well as story-glue even without full cultural assimilation in either direction. A specialist could argue that Ptolemaic Egypt's stability owed more to its wealth from Mediterranean trade and Nile agriculture than to this cultural balancing act, which would weaken a reading centered on religious performance as the dynasty's key to longevity.
