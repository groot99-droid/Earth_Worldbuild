## Summary
The Pala Empire, founded in 750 CE when the chieftain Gopala was elected king to end a period of regional anarchy, ruled Bengal and eastern Bihar for four centuries as South Asia's last major Buddhist royal dynasty, sponsoring the great monastic universities of Nalanda and Vikramashila before falling to the Sena dynasty in 1161.

## Facts
- Gopala rose from local chieftain to king in 750 CE, reportedly chosen to end a prolonged period of political disorder and lawlessness in the Bengal region, and became the first ruler of the Pala dynasty as well as, according to tradition, the first Buddhist king in the region.
- At its height, the Pala Empire controlled territory across what is now Bengal, Bihar, Orissa and Assam, ruling from major cities including Gauda, Vikramapura and Pataliputra for approximately four centuries.
- Pala rulers were significant patrons of Buddhism, founding major monasteries including Odantapuri and Somapura Mahavihara, and providing crucial royal patronage to the renowned Buddhist universities of Nalanda and Vikramashila, centers of learning that drew students and scholars from across Asia.
- The Pala dynasty stands out among major South Asian ruling houses of this era as a Buddhist dynasty in a region where Hindu ruling houses were far more common, making Pala patronage particularly significant to Buddhism's continued institutional survival in the subcontinent during this period.
- Pala power gradually weakened over the 12th century, and Vijayasena of the rising Sena dynasty finally expelled the last Pala ruler, Madanapala, from Bengal, ending four centuries of Pala rule and Buddhist royal patronage in the region.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Pala patronage of Nalanda and Vikramashila shows a state's political power directly underwriting a meaning-engine's institutional survival, since major centers of Buddhist learning required sustained royal investment in land grants, buildings and protection that only a stable, wealthy dynasty could reliably provide across four centuries. That the Pala dynasty stood out specifically as a Buddhist royal house in a broader South Asian political landscape increasingly dominated by Hindu dynasties suggests that a ruling family's own religious identity could function as a genuine claim-line shaping which meaning-engine received a particular region's state resources, rather than religious patronage being uniformly distributed regardless of a dynasty's own beliefs. A specialist could argue that Pala support for Buddhism, while real and consequential, has sometimes been romanticized by later Buddhist historiography in ways that understate the Palas' own political and military conduct, which followed patterns of warfare and succession dispute common to South Asian dynasties of any religious affiliation.
