## Summary
The Normans descended from Norse settlers granted the duchy of Normandy in 911 CE, and within two centuries their descendants had conquered England and southern Italy and Sicily, building some of medieval Europe's most consequential and militarily aggressive states.

## Facts
- The Frankish king granted the Viking leader Rollo control of what became Normandy in 911, and within a few generations Rollo's Norse-descended followers had adopted French language, Christianity and local customs while retaining a distinct military and political identity.
- William, Duke of Normandy, invaded England in 1066, defeating and killing King Harold II at the Battle of Hastings on 14 October and being crowned King of England that Christmas Day, a conquest that killed thousands and violently reorganized English landholding and language.
- Norman French became the language of the English court and legal system for centuries afterward, permanently reshaping the English language with a large infusion of French vocabulary.
- Separately, Norman lords from Apulia and Calabria conquered Sicily from its Muslim Emirate between 1061 and 1091, and Roger II was crowned the first King of Sicily in 1130.
- Norman Sicily became known for blending Christian, Muslim and Byzantine populations, administration and architecture more successfully than most other medieval states, though this coexistence existed alongside continued warfare and unequal status among these communities.
- Norman rule in Sicily ended in 1194 when the Hohenstaufen dynasty took the kingdom through marriage inheritance.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that within four generations the Normans moved from Norse raiders receiving land as tribute-avoidance to a ruling elite exporting conquest across a continent, sanctioned harm converted into inherited land titles that outlasted the violence that won them. That the same people who devastated English landholding at Hastings built, in Sicily, one of the more religiously pluralistic states of the period suggests conquest and coexistence were not opposites for the Normans but two tools in the same claim-making tool-lineage, deployed as circumstances allowed. A specialist could argue Norman Sicily's pluralism reflected pragmatic governance of a population too diverse to rule any other way rather than genuine tolerance, which would weaken a reading that credits Norman institutions themselves.
