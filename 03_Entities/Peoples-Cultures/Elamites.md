## Summary
The Elamites built one of the ancient Near East's oldest civilizations in what is now southwestern Iran, centered on the city of Susa, persisting from around 3200 to 539 BCE as a distinct culture and rival to Mesopotamian powers before being absorbed into the Achaemenid Persian Empire.

## Facts
- Elamite civilization is dated from about 3200 to 539 BCE, making it, alongside Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley and Egypt, one of the oldest documented civilizations in the ancient world.
- Elam's territory combined lowland plains near the Tigris and Euphrates river system with immediate highland areas to the north and east, an unusual geographic combination that shaped its distinct political and cultural development from neighboring Mesopotamian city-states.
- Susa, Elam's capital, was constructed as early as 4395 BCE according to some estimates and remained Elam's principal city for millennia, later becoming one of the most important cities of the Achaemenid Persian realm as well.
- The Elamite state reached the height of its power around 1210 BCE, but suffered a severe blow in 640 BCE when the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal invaded, sacked Susa, and deported a portion of its leading citizens to Samaria in Palestine, a documented act of forced displacement this vault records as suffering.
- Following the rise of the Achaemenid Persian Empire in 539 BCE, Elam became a Persian satrapy, and Darius I rebuilt Susa as one of his imperial capitals; Elamite religious practices, and the Elamite language itself, written in cuneiform, continued in official use within the Achaemenid administration for generations afterward.

## Context & Connections
Elam developed alongside and in periodic conflict with the [[Sumerians]] and other Mesopotamian powers, suffered destruction at the hands of the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]], and was ultimately absorbed into the [[Achaemenid Persians]]' empire, which preserved elements of Elamite religion, language and administrative practice. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Elam's absorption into the Achaemenid Empire was unusually generous by the standards this vault records for conquered peoples: rather than erasing Elamite religious practice and language, the conquering Achaemenids kept both in active administrative use, a claim-line drawn around territory and taxation while leaving much of the underlying meaning-engine and mark-code intact. This contrasts with Ashurbanipal's earlier sack of Susa and forced deportation of its elite, recorded here as sanctioned harm, showing that a single ancient people could experience both violent destruction and, under a later conqueror, comparatively respectful incorporation within the span of a single century. A specialist could argue that Achaemenid preservation of Elamite language and religion reflects practical administrative convenience rather than genuine cultural respect, which would weaken a reading that treats the continuity as evidence of unusually tolerant governance.
