## Summary
The Kingdom of Israel and Judah names the polity or polities the Hebrew Bible describes as a united monarchy under Saul, David and Solomon before it split into a northern Kingdom of Israel and a southern Kingdom of Judah, a narrative whose earliest chapters remain genuinely contested among archaeologists and historians.

## Facts
- According to the Hebrew Bible, a united monarchy over Israel and Judah existed as early as the 11th century BCE under Saul, David and Solomon, with the kingdom dividing into separate northern and southern states following Solomon's death, traditionally dated to around 930 BCE.
- No archaeological evidence for the united monarchy commands scholarly consensus, and historians disagree sharply on how much of the biblical account reflects a genuinely powerful, centralized early kingdom versus a smaller chiefdom later magnified by later religious and national tradition.
- Some archaeologists point to monumental architecture at sites including Megiddo and Gezer as possible evidence for a strong united monarchy, while others argue these structures cannot be confidently attributed to Solomon's reign and may date from significantly later periods.
- Independent of the united monarchy debate, historians and archaeologists broadly agree that the separate Kingdom of Israel in the north existed by about 900 BCE and the Kingdom of Judah in the south by about 850 BCE, giving the divided kingdoms a firmer evidentiary basis than their claimed unified predecessor.
- The Neo-Assyrian Empire destroyed the northern Kingdom of Israel in 722 BCE, and the Neo-Babylonian Empire under Nebuchadnezzar II conquered Jerusalem and destroyed the southern Kingdom of Judah's First Temple in 586 BCE, deporting part of its population.

## Context & Connections
This kingdom's religious traditions form the historical root of [[Judaism]], its capital and holiest city was [[Jerusalem]], and its southern kingdom's destruction came at the hands of [[Babylon]]'s Neo-Babylonian rulers. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that this kingdom's earliest chapters exist in an unusual evidentiary position: a richly detailed religious narrative describing a golden age of unified rule exists alongside an archaeological record too thin to confirm or clearly refute it, so this vault records the united monarchy as contested tradition rather than established history, while treating the later, better-attested divided kingdoms with somewhat greater confidence. That later religious and national tradition had strong reasons to remember a unified, powerful founding kingdom, regardless of the underlying historical reality, is itself a case of a meaning-engine shaping how a society remembers its own claim-line to a homeland, independent of what archaeology alone could establish. A specialist could argue that continued excavation at sites like Megiddo and Gezer may yet resolve the united monarchy question more definitively in either direction, which would strengthen rather than weaken the case for treating the debate as genuinely still open.

## Open Questions
- Does the archaeological evidence at Megiddo, Gezer and other sites ultimately support or undermine the existence of a powerful united monarchy under David and Solomon?
- How much of the biblical narrative about the united monarchy reflects later religious and national reconstruction rather than contemporary historical memory?
