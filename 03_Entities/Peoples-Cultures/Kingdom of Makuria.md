## Summary
The Kingdom of Makuria was a Christian Nubian state along the Nile in what is now Sudan, whose 7th-century treaty with its Muslim Egyptian neighbors, the Baqt, became one of the longest-observed diplomatic agreements in recorded history before the kingdom's eventual decline in the 14th century.

## Facts
- Makuria emerged as one of three Christian Nubian kingdoms, alongside Nobatia and Alodia, ruling from its capital at Dongola, and had converted to Christianity by the end of the 6th century CE.
- When an Arab army invaded Nubian territory in 651 CE, Makurian forces repulsed it, and the two sides subsequently agreed to the Baqt, a treaty establishing territorial rights and annual exchanges of goods, including enslaved people and agricultural products, between Makuria and Muslim Egypt.
- The Baqt maintained a relative peace between Makuria and Egypt lasting, with interruptions, until the 13th century, and the treaty as a whole is often cited as one of the longest-lasting diplomatic agreements on record, in effect for roughly seven centuries before its final dissolution.
- Makuria's cultural and architectural peak came in the 9th to 11th centuries, producing monumental buildings including the Throne Hall of Dongola, a great cruciform church, and the Banganarti monastery complex.
- Internal succession disputes and renewed external pressure from Egypt weakened Makuria from the 13th century onward, and the kingdom's political structure had effectively collapsed by the mid-14th century, ending nearly a thousand years of Christian Nubian statehood in the region.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Baqt shows a claim-line maintained for nearly seven centuries not by continuous warfare but by a negotiated exchange, goods and enslaved people moving in one direction, security and autonomy preserved in the other, a durable peace built on a bargain this vault records as containing real, documented harm even as it prevented greater warfare. That two states of different meaning-engines, Christian Makuria and Muslim Egypt, sustained this arrangement across dynastic changes on both sides for centuries suggests that diplomatic story-glue, once established and mutually beneficial, can outlast religious difference as a source of conflict. A specialist could argue the Baqt's endurance owed more to Egypt's own periods of internal weakness and external distraction than to the treaty's inherent stability, which would weaken a reading that credits the agreement itself as unusually durable diplomacy.
