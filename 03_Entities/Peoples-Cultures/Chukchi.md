## Summary
The Chukchi are an Indigenous people of Russia's northeasternmost Arctic region, historically divided into inland reindeer-herding and coastal maritime-hunting communities, who emerged as a distinct cultural group roughly 800 to 1,000 years ago and today number a little over 16,000 people.

## Facts
- The Chukchi likely coalesced as a distinct cultural group approximately 800 to 1,000 years ago in Chukotka, the northeasternmost region of Siberia, and today number roughly 16,000 people according to recent Russian census figures.
- Chukchi society traditionally divided into two related groups: the Reindeer Chukchi, nomadic herders who called themselves "Chauchu," meaning "a man who is rich in reindeer," and the Maritime Chukchi, known as "Anqallyt" or "the sea people," who hunted whales, walrus and seals from fixed coastal settlements.
- Reindeer Chukchi communities practiced a fully nomadic lifestyle, undertaking seasonal treks of roughly 320 kilometers lasting up to nine months into the northern tundra, partly to escape insects that could otherwise devastate their herds with disease.
- Maritime Chukchi communities lived in semi-subterranean dwellings and used wood-framed, skin-covered boats to hunt large sea mammals, a way of life adapted to Arctic coastal conditions distinct from the inland reindeer economy.
- The Chukchi speak a Luorawetlan language within the Paleosiberian language group, linguistically and culturally related to neighboring Koryak and Itelmen peoples, and Chukchi communities today continue both traditional herding and maritime hunting practices alongside the pressures of contact with the wider Russian state.

## Context & Connections
Chukchi communities encountered sustained outside contact and pressure as Russian control expanded through the [[Russian Expansion into Siberia]], reaching Chukotka later and less completely than it did other Siberian regions given the area's remoteness. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Reindeer and Maritime Chukchi, though considered one people, developed two genuinely distinct earth-systems relationships, one built around a mobile land animal and one around hunting from the sea, a reminder that a single ethnic or linguistic identity can encompass more than one fundamentally different way of extracting a living from a harsh environment. That Chukotka's extreme remoteness let Chukchi communities retain more independence from Russian imperial control for longer than many other Siberian peoples this vault records shows how geography itself can function as a kind of claim-line protecting a kin-lattice from outside domination, at least for a time. This reading would weaken if historical evidence shows Russian administrative and economic control over Chukotka was in fact established earlier and more thoroughly than the region's reputation for remoteness suggests.
