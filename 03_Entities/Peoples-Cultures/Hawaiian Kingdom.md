## Summary
The Hawaiian Kingdom was a Native Hawaiian monarchy united by Kamehameha I between about 1795 and 1810 and overthrown in 1893. The United States annexed the islands in 1898; the annexation's legality remains disputed.

## Facts
- Kamehameha I conquered Oʻahu in 1795 and gained Kauaʻi and Niʻihau by negotiation in 1810; sources differ on the founding year.
- The Hawaiian Kingdom's farmers worked taro terraces and fishponds in mountain-to-sea ahupuaʻa; exports shifted from sandalwood to sugar, boosted by an 1875 United States treaty.
- The kapu system of taboos ended in 1819, a written constitution followed in 1840, and Britain and France recognized independence in 1843.
- Introduced diseases cut the Native Hawaiian population to about 71,000 by 1853. After the 1848 Great Māhele, commoners' successful claims under the 1850 Kuleana Act covered under one percent of the land.
- An armed militia forced the 1887 Bayonet Constitution on Kalākaua. In 1893 a Committee of Safety, with United States Marines ashore, deposed Liliuokalani. About 21,000 petitioned against annexation in 1897; Congress annexed the islands in 1898.

## Context & Connections
The Hawaiian Kingdom, from [[Kamehameha I]] to [[Liliuokalani]], ruled descendants of the [[Polynesians]] ([[Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]]) and, later, many immigrants in [[Oceania]] during the [[Industrial Age]]. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Hawaiian Kingdom merged rival chiefdoms into one kin-lattice, then entered treaties and constitutions in its recognizers' mark-code. The same tools, including the Māhele's tradable claim-lines, let American- and European-descended businessmen gather power against the monarchy. A specialist could argue that coercion in 1887 and 1893, not the tools, caused the loss, which would weaken a reading that finds the loss built into them.
