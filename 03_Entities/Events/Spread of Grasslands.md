## Summary
The spread of grasslands was the expansion of open grass-dominated vegetation across continents, from about 25 to 5 million years ago, in the Miocene. It changed animal life and set up the plant family that now supplies most of the world's staple crops.

## Facts
- Grass pollen and microscopic plant silica are known from the latest Cretaceous or early Paleogene, but grasses were a minor part of vegetation for tens of millions of years.
- Open grasslands expanded from the early to middle Miocene, about 20 to 15 million years ago. Grasses using the C4 pathway, which suits hot, dry conditions and low carbon dioxide, spread widely about 8 to 6 million years ago, as shown by carbon isotopes in fossil soils and teeth.
- Proposed drivers include cooling and drying, seasonal rainfall, falling carbon dioxide and fire. Their relative roles are debated.
- Grazing mammals evolved high-crowned teeth and long limbs for running; horses shifted from browsing to grazing, and grazing antelope, cattle and their relatives diversified.
- The cereals, including wheat, rice and maize, are grasses; see [[The Agricultural Revolution]].

## Context & Connections
The Spread of Grasslands belongs mostly to the [[Neogene Period]] of the [[Cenozoic Era]] and is a background to the story of the Miocene apes and early hominins. Its crop lineage is the source of [[Domestication of Rice]] and [[Domestication of Maize]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[Earth Systems and Life]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the staple foods of nearly every large human society come from a plant family that became widespread only in the last few tens of millions of years. Open landscapes are also the setting of a long-running hypothesis about human origins, which the Observer treats as a hypothesis, not a finding. It would weaken if early hominins proved to have lived mainly in closed woodland.
