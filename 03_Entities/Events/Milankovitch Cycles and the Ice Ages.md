## Summary
Milankovitch cycles are slow, regular changes in the shape of Earth's orbit, the tilt of its axis, and the wobble of that axis. They shift how sunlight is spread across latitudes and seasons, and since about 2.6 million years ago they have paced the growth and melting of the Quaternary ice sheets.

## Facts
- The three cycles are eccentricity (orbit shape; about 100,000 and 400,000 years), obliquity (axial tilt, between about 22.1 and 24.5 degrees; 41,000 years), and precession (repeating about every 19,000 to 23,000 years).
- Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković calculated between the 1920s and 1940s how these changes alter sunlight by latitude. In 1976 Hays, Imbrie, and Shackleton found the same three periods in oxygen-isotope records from deep-sea sediment cores, which track global ice volume.
- The orbital changes alter the distribution of sunlight far more than its yearly total, so ice sheets, ocean circulation, and atmospheric carbon dioxide amplify the effect.
- Glacial cycles followed the 41,000-year tilt cycle until about 1.25 million years ago, then lengthened to about 100,000 years; see [[Middle Pleistocene Transition]]. Why the 100,000-year cycle dominates is unresolved.
- The cycles are far older than the ice ages; what changed about 2.7 million years ago was the climate's response (see [[Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation]]).

## Context & Connections
Milankovitch cycles pace the glacial stages of the [[Quaternary Period]] and [[Pleistocene Epoch]], including the [[Last Glacial Maximum]], and the current interglacial, the [[Holocene Epoch]]. The [[Younger Dryas]] interrupted the last warming. See [[Earth Systems and Life]] and [[Science]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the species' whole written record sits inside one warm phase of a cycle set by the geometry of Earth's orbit, a cycle uncovered by pattern-taming from Milanković's sums to the 1976 sediment cores. The Observer treats it as a precondition, not a cause of later history. The reading would weaken if glacial terminations proved to depend more on internal ice-sheet behavior than on orbital pacing.

## Open Questions
