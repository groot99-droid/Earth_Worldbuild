## Summary
From the 1500s, European colonizers built large sugar plantations, worked mainly by enslaved Africans, in Brazil and the Caribbean, forming one of the most profitable and brutal systems in early modern trade.

## Facts
- Sugarcane originated in New Guinea and was spread across Asia before reaching the Mediterranean and the Atlantic islands of Madeira and Sao Tome.
- The plantation model, with large-scale production for export by enslaved labor, developed on the Atlantic islands and spread to Brazil in the 16th century.
- The Caribbean sugar boom, beginning in Barbados in the 1640s, expanded the use of enslaved labor.
- Saint-Domingue, the French colony that became Haiti, was the largest sugar producer in the world in the late 18th century.
- Sugar plantations drove much of the demand for the Atlantic slave trade.

## Context & Connections
The sugar economy was the engine of the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]] and a leading part of the [[Columbian Exchange]], after the collapse of the [[Taino]] population. It was overthrown in Saint-Domingue by the [[Haitian Revolution]].
