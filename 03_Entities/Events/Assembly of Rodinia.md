## Summary
The Assembly of Rodinia was the joining of most of Earth's continental blocks into one supercontinent between about 1.3 and 0.9 billion years ago. The reconstruction rests on mountain belts and magnetic data, and the arrangement of the parts is debated.

## Facts
- Rodinia formed as separate continental blocks collided between about 1.3 and 0.9 billion years ago, raising long mountain belts, including the Grenville belt of eastern North America.
- Mountain belts of Grenville age occur on several continents, and magnetic data from ancient rocks give the past latitudes of the blocks. Competing reconstructions place North America at the core but arrange the other blocks differently.
- Rodinia followed the proposed supercontinent Nuna, and the supercontinent cycle later produced Pangaea; see [[Breakup of Pangaea]].
- Rodinia began to rift apart about 800 to 750 million years ago. A large volcanic province of about 720 million years ago in northern Canada coincides with the start of the first Cryogenian glaciation, and rifting is proposed as one trigger; that link is a hypothesis.

## Context & Connections
The Assembly of Rodinia spans the [[Mesoproterozoic Era]] and the start of the [[Neoproterozoic Era]], and its break-up precedes [[Snowball Earth]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[Earth Systems and Life]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a planet's early maps are reconstructed as much as observed: Rodinia is known from mountain roots and magnetic minerals, not from coastlines. The Observer holds the detailed arrangement loosely and treats the broad claim, that the continents joined and split repeatedly, as firmer. The reading would weaken if the magnetic data proved too sparse to distinguish the competing layouts.
