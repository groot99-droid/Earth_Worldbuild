## Summary
The Boring Billion is an informal name for the interval from about 1.8 to 0.8 billion years ago, a span with no well-established ice ages and little apparent change in the fossil record. Whether it was really uneventful is debated.

## Facts
- The interval covers the [[Mesoproterozoic Era]] and the edges of the eras on either side of it, and it lies between the [[Great Oxidation Event]] and the ice ages of [[Snowball Earth]].
- Chemical records suggest that atmospheric oxygen stayed well below today's level and that some waters at middle depth were rich in sulfide. Shortages of nutrients such as nitrogen and trace metals have been proposed as limits on life.
- Eukaryotes were present and diversifying slowly. The red alga Bangiomorpha, dated in different studies to between roughly 1.05 and 1.2 billion years ago, belongs to this interval.
- The supercontinent Nuna broke apart and Rodinia began to assemble.
- Some researchers argue that the name misleads, because records show fluctuations in oxygen and ecological change within the interval.

## Context & Connections
The Boring Billion lies within the [[Proterozoic Eon]] and follows the [[Origin of Eukaryotes]]. It ends with the changes of the Neoproterozoic, including the break-up of Rodinia and the glaciations of [[Snowball Earth]]. The event belongs to the theme thread of [[Earth Systems and Life]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that "boring" is a verdict on the surviving record, not on the planet, and that a billion years is long enough for slow processes to matter. The reading would weaken if better records showed genuine stasis in oxygen, climate and eukaryote diversity across the whole interval.

## Open Questions
- Was the interval truly stable, or does a sparse record hide change?
- What kept oxygen and complex life at low levels for so long?
