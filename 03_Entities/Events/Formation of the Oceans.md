## Summary
The Formation of the Oceans is the time, probably between about 4.4 and 4.0 billion years ago, when liquid water first collected on Earth's surface as the young planet cooled. The date is uncertain and rests on indirect evidence.

## Facts
- Oxygen-isotope ratios in some Jack Hills zircons, dated to about 4.4 billion years ago, have been read as evidence that their parent magma had interacted with liquid water; that reading is contested. See [[Jack Hills Zircons]].
- Earth's water came from the material that built the planet and from later impacts of water-rich bodies. Asteroids of the carbonaceous chondrite type match the hydrogen isotopes of ocean water well, but the shares from each source are debated.
- The young Sun gave only about 70 percent of its present light, so liquid water at the surface needed a strong greenhouse effect from gases such as carbon dioxide.
- The oldest rocks that clearly formed in water, including sediments and pillow-shaped lavas at Isua in Greenland, are about 3.7 to 3.8 billion years old, so oceans existed by then.
- The depth of the early oceans and the amount of land above them are debated.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer files the oceans under preconditions: every later subject in the record assumes liquid water. That the planet had it depended on its size, its distance from the Sun and the contingent delivery of water, and the Observer treats the outcome as favourable circumstance, not design. The reading would weaken if water proved a near-inevitable product of planet formation at this distance.
