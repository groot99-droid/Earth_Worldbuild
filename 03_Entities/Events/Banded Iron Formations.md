## Summary
Banded iron formations are ancient sedimentary rocks of alternating iron-rich and silica-rich layers, deposited mostly between about 3.8 and 1.8 billion years ago. They are a major source of the world's iron ore.

## Facts
- The layers are usually millimetres to centimetres thick. The oldest are about 3.8 billion years old, in Greenland, and deposition peaked about 2.7 to 2.4 billion years ago, with great deposits in Western Australia, South Africa and the Lake Superior region of North America.
- The leading explanation is that oxygen-poor seawater held dissolved iron, which was oxidized and settled out when oxygen or other oxidants appeared. Whether oxygen-producing bacteria or iron-using photosynthetic bacteria did most of the work is debated.
- Deposition largely ended about 1.8 billion years ago, probably as the deeper ocean lost its dissolved iron, and a few late formations appear during the Cryogenian ice ages; see [[Snowball Earth]].
- Ore derived from these rocks is a major source of the iron used in steelmaking today.

## Context & Connections
Banded iron formations record the slow oxidation of the oceans before and during the [[Great Oxidation Event]], and they tie the [[Origin of Photosynthesis]] and the work of [[Cyanobacteria]] to changes in ocean chemistry. They belong to the [[Archean Eon]] and continue into the [[Proterozoic Eon]], and to the theme thread of [[Earth Systems and Life]].
