## Summary
Amazonian urbanism is the growing evidence that parts of the Amazon basin supported large, planned settlements linked by roads and earthworks, from about 500 BCE to the 1500s CE, and that Indigenous peoples enriched the forest soils and landscape.

## Facts
- Aerial laser survey (lidar) has revealed settlement networks at [[Upano Valley Settlements]] in Ecuador, [[Casarabe Culture]] sites in Bolivia, and [[Xingu Garden Cities]] in Brazil.
- The [[Marajoara Culture]] built large mounds at the mouth of the Amazon.
- Dark, fertile soils (terra preta, or Amazonian Dark Earths), rich in charcoal and often found with pre-Columbian pottery, occur across the basin and are associated with settlement. Whether people created them or only used natural deposits is disputed; a 2021 study of one site in the Brazilian Amazon argued for a largely natural, alluvial origin.
- These settlements were 'low-density' compared with cities elsewhere: dispersed households, plazas, roads, and gardens rather than compact walled towns.

## Context & Connections
The evidence changes the picture of the [[Amazon Basin and South American Lowlands]] before the [[Columbian Exchange]] and links to wider questions in [[The Urban Revolution]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a city need not look like a city elsewhere. Dispersed, garden-based settlements challenge the assumption that dense stone towns are the mark of complexity.

## Open Questions
