## Summary
The Second Sino-Japanese War (1937 to 1945) was the full-scale war between Japan and China that began at the Marco Polo Bridge near Beijing and later merged into World War II. It included mass killings of civilians, among them at Nanjing, whose death toll is disputed.

## Facts
- Full-scale war began on 7 July 1937 after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident near Beijing. It was an undeclared war that quickly grew into large-scale fighting in northern China.
- The incident hastened the formation of the Second United Front between the Kuomintang and the Chinese Communist Party.
- Japanese forces took Nanjing on 13 December 1937 and carried out mass killings there beginning that day. Estimates of the number of Chinese killed range from about 100,000 to more than 300,000. One scholarly assessment gives about 50,000 killed within the city walls in the first days and more than 100,000 but fewer than 200,000 in the city and surrounding counties by March 1938.
- The figures are contested. Some Japanese revisionists give much lower numbers, while some Chinese official figures are higher than the scholarly range.

## Context & Connections
The war followed Japan's industrialization and expansion after the [[Meiji Restoration]] and merged into [[World War II]] after 1941. See [[Sanctioned Harm]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer records the mass killings at Nanjing as an atrocity, whatever the argument over the count. That the number itself has become a claim-line between governments and historians shows how a single figure can carry the weight of a whole nation's memory.
