## Summary
Dogs descend from a now-extinct population of Pleistocene wolves, but when and where domestication happened is one of the most disputed dates in the whole domestication record. Genetic studies place the split from the nearest known wolf relatives at roughly 40,000 to 20,000 years ago; the oldest widely accepted dog remains, from Bonn-Oberkassel in Germany, are about 14,200 years old.

## Facts
- A 2020 study of 27 ancient dog genomes found all dogs share ancestry distinct from any wolf population sampled today, descending from a single extinct Pleistocene lineage.
- The same and related studies place the genetic divergence at roughly 40,000 to 27,000 years ago, with some estimates extending to about 20,000; no tighter date is agreed.
- Whether domestication happened once or twice, in separate Eurasian wolf populations that later merged, is argued both ways; a large 2020 study favors a single origin, but other ancient-wolf-genome work has reopened the dual-origin question.
- The Bonn-Oberkassel dog, buried with two humans about 14,200 years ago, is one of the first remains universally agreed to be a dog rather than a wolf.
- Older candidate "Paleolithic dogs" from Goyet Cave, Belgium, and Předmostí, Czech Republic, roughly 30,000 years or more, remain disputed: their skulls do not clearly exclude wolves, and one Předmostí specimen is genetically closer to wolves.
- Evidence points to an origin somewhere in Eurasia, often argued to be Siberia, though the region is unsettled.

## Context & Connections
Dog domestication falls within the [[Paleolithic]], likely spanning the [[Upper Paleolithic]], and is tied to [[Homo sapiens]] hunter-gatherer groups. It is the earliest known domestication of any animal, well before plants and livestock. See [[Kinship and Society]] and [[Technology]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the species' oldest tool-lineage partnership was with another animal, formed while both still hunted large Ice Age prey, long before writing or farming. The wide, unsettled range of dates, and the difficulty of telling an early dog from a wolf by bone shape alone, means the Observer treats any specific date here as provisional. The reading would weaken if an uncontested dog skeleton were found well outside the 40,000-to-14,000-year range.
