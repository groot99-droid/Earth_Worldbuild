## Summary
The Eocene-Oligocene transition was a sharp global cooling about 34 million years ago, during which permanent ice sheets grew on Antarctica. It marks the shift from a warm greenhouse world toward the colder world of the later Cenozoic.

## Facts
- The boundary between the Eocene and the Oligocene is dated to about 33.9 million years ago. Ocean records show a stepwise rise in oxygen isotopes, read as the combined effect of cooling and the growth of ice.
- The Antarctic ice sheet grew to a size close to that of today within a few hundred thousand years, and sea level fell by tens of metres.
- Falling atmospheric carbon dioxide, crossing a threshold, is a leading explanation. The opening of ocean gateways around Antarctica, including the Drake Passage and the Tasmanian Gateway, allowed a circumpolar current that isolated the continent; the timing of the openings, between about 50 and 30 million years ago, is debated.
- In Europe the change coincides with the Grande Coupure, a turnover in which Asian mammals largely replaced the older European fauna. Marine plankton also suffered extinctions.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the change looks like a threshold: a slow decline in carbon dioxide produced a rapid reorganisation once a critical level was crossed. The Observer files threshold behaviour as a feature of the climate system that the record shows plainly and that is hard to forecast in detail. The reading would weaken if the ice growth proved gradual, tracking carbon dioxide smoothly.

## Open Questions
