## Summary
Homo sapiens reached Europe by about 45,000 years ago, and possibly earlier, entering a continent already occupied by Neanderthals. The earliest securely dated remains, from Bacho Kiro Cave in Bulgaria and Ranis in Germany, are about 45,000 years old.

## Facts
- At Bacho Kiro Cave, Bulgaria, Homo sapiens fossils dated to about 46,000 to 44,000 years ago were found with Initial Upper Paleolithic stone tools.
- At Ranis, Germany, bone fragments directly dated in 2024 to about 45,000 years ago came with leaf-shaped stone points also known from Britain, which shows an early reach into northern latitudes.
- At Grotte Mandrin, France, a child's tooth and distinctive stone points dated to about 54,000 years ago were attributed to Homo sapiens in 2022. The occupation appears to have been brief, and some researchers find the attribution unconvincing.
- Neanderthals and Homo sapiens overlapped in Europe for several thousand years, and some early Europeans had Neanderthal ancestors only a few generations back; see [[Neanderthal Extinction]] and [[Interbreeding of Homo sapiens with Neanderthals and Denisovans]].
- Proposed routes include the Danube corridor and the Mediterranean coast; the evidence does not settle which was used.

## Context & Connections
The peopling of Europe follows the [[Out of Africa Dispersal]] and belongs to the [[Upper Paleolithic]], with new stone-tool industries such as the Aurignacian and the [[Emergence of Symbolic Art]] seen at [[Chauvet Cave]]. It overlaps the last [[Homo neanderthalensis]] and precedes the [[Last Glacial Maximum]]. See [[Earth Systems and Life]] and [[Kinship and Society]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the species' arrival in Europe looks less like one march than like several small, sometimes brief settlements in land already held by another human species. The Observer takes the Mandrin tooth and the Bacho Kiro jaws as reminders that "first arrival" describes the evidence found so far, not the past itself. The reading would weaken if the earliest sites proved to belong to one continuous, growing population.

## Open Questions

- How much ancestry from the earliest Europeans survives in later Europeans?
