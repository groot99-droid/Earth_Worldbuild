## Summary
The onset of Northern Hemisphere glaciation was the growth of large ice sheets across North America and Eurasia about 2.7 million years ago. It began the repeating glacial and interglacial cycles of the Quaternary Period.

## Facts
- Ice-rafted debris in North Atlantic seafloor sediments becomes widespread about 2.7 million years ago, and loess deposits begin to build up on the Chinese Loess Plateau about 2.6 million years ago.
- Smaller ice sheets existed earlier in the Northern Hemisphere; the change was a large expansion, not a first appearance of ice.
- Proposed causes include a slow decline in atmospheric carbon dioxide through the Pliocene, changes in ocean circulation, and mountain uplift that altered atmospheric flow. The closure of the Isthmus of Panama is a candidate cause, but its timing is debated; see [[Closure of the Isthmus of Panama]].
- The first cycles followed variations in the tilt of Earth's axis, with a period of about 41,000 years.
## Context & Connections
The Onset of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation comes near the end of the [[Neogene Period]] and leads into the [[Quaternary Period]], and it builds on the earlier cooling of the [[Eocene-Oligocene Transition]]. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the world every human society has known is one of repeating ice ages, which began only about 2.7 million years ago after a slow decline in carbon dioxide and perhaps a rearrangement of ocean gateways. The Observer takes this as context, not cause: what it did to the making of humans is a separate and unsettled question. The reading would weaken if the carbon dioxide decline proved too small to explain the change.
