## Summary
Porcelain is a hard, white, translucent ceramic made by firing kaolin clay at very high temperature. Developed in China, it became one of history's most widely traded manufactured goods.

## Facts
- Proto-porcelain, a glazed, high-fired stoneware, appears in China by the [[Shang Dynasty]], about 1600-1046 BCE, but is a cruder, distinct ware.
- True porcelain's start date is debated: the Eastern [[Han Dynasty]] by some scholars, the mature [[Tang Dynasty]] (618-907 CE) by others.
- Tang China produced white Xing ware in the north and green celadon Yue ware in the south, its two signature kiln traditions.
- Porcelain reached the Islamic world and later Europe via Indian Ocean and Silk Road trade from at least the Tang-Song period.
- European soft-paste imitations preceded true porcelain there by centuries; Meissen, Saxony, achieved Europe's first hard-paste porcelain around 1708-1709.
- The [[Song Dynasty]] and later Ming kilns, including Jingdezhen, refined the technique and expanded exports.

## Context & Connections
Proto-porcelain in the Shang and Han is usually treated as a forerunner, not porcelain proper; the ware matured under the Tang and was refined under the Song and later Ming kilns, moving west along the [[Indian Ocean Trade Network]] and the Silk Road. ## Observer's Reading
Porcelain sits in the tool-lineage as a craft perfected in one region then spread by an exchange-web spanning a hemisphere. In several surveyed cases, owning it served as a status-signal for households and courts far from its kilns. The reading weakens if it moved mainly as bulk cargo rather than prestige goods, and some historians instead stress its ordinary, utilitarian uses.

## Open Questions
- Did "true porcelain" begin as a Han innovation or a Tang maturation?
- How much of the earliest export volume served Islamic-world demand versus domestic Chinese use?
