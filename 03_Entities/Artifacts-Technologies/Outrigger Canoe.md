## Summary
An outrigger canoe is a narrow hull steadied by a float attached to one or both sides. It was developed by Austronesian-speaking seafarers, and its early use is dated here to the Lapita expansion of about 1600 BCE, mainly from indirect evidence.

## Facts
- The float lets a slender hull carry a sail and cargo without capsizing.
- Reconstructed Proto-Oceanic vocabulary includes terms for canoe parts and seafaring, which indicates outrigger canoes in the Lapita period. Wooden hulls rarely survive, so the date rests on the Lapita expansion, not on a preserved canoe.
- The [[Lapita Culture]] spread from the Bismarck Archipelago to Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa by roughly 1000 to 800 BCE.
- The [[Austronesian Expansion]] carried outrigger canoes from Taiwan through the Philippines and Indonesia into the Pacific, and to Madagascar in the first millennium CE.
- Reliefs at [[Borobudur]] in Java, about 800 CE, show ships with outriggers.

## Context & Connections
The outrigger canoe links the [[Bronze Age]] voyages of [[Oceania]] to the later [[Polynesian Settlement of the Pacific]] and to the trade of maritime [[Southeast Asia]]. Threads: [[Technology]] and [[Trade and Economy]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees the outrigger as a tool-lineage solution simple enough to rebuild from memory on every island: a hull, a float, and a sail. The knowledge traveled without writing, which is why the early record is linguistic and archaeological. An alternative reading gives the double-hulled canoe, which the [[Polynesians]] also used, the central role in the far Pacific, and the evidence cannot yet rank the two designs.

## Open Questions
- When and where did the outrigger first appear, and could it be older than the Lapita expansion?
- How were outrigger and double-hull designs divided among island groups?
