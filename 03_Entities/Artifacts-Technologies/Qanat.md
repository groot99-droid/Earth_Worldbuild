## Summary
A qanat is an underground channel that carries groundwater by gravity from a water-bearing slope to fields, reached by a line of vertical shafts. The earliest written record is an Assyrian account of 714 BCE describing qanats near Ulhu in Iran, and the system spread across Asia and North Africa.

## Facts
- Sargon II of Assyria's account of his 714 BCE campaign against Urartu describes a district near Ulhu that stayed green with no river; later readers identify the source as qanats. The system may be older, and its origin is debated.
- Workers dig a gently sloping tunnel from the settlement up into the water table, with vertical shafts at intervals for removing soil and for ventilation.
- The qanat of Gonabad in northeastern Iran, one of the longest, has 427 shafts and about 33 kilometers of tunnels.
- More than 37,000 qanats are recorded in Iran; UNESCO inscribed eleven as "The Persian Qanat" in 2016.
- Similar systems are called karez, foggara, and falaj elsewhere in Asia and North Africa.
- Motor pumps in the 20th century lowered water tables and dried up many qanats.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees the qanat as a tool-lineage branch whose skill lived in specialist well-diggers, passed on by apprenticeship rather than text. A qanat also binds a community, since one tunnel serves everyone below it, making it story-glue and infrastructure at once. The reading would weaken if qanats were usually run by landlords or the state rather than by village agreement.
