## Summary
The internet is a global network of interconnected computer networks. Its ancestor, ARPANET, sent its first message between UCLA and the Stanford Research Institute on 29 October 1969, and the shared TCP/IP protocols that define the modern internet were adopted network-wide on 1 January 1983.

## Facts
- ARPANET, funded by the US Department of Defense's Advanced Research Projects Agency, aimed to let researchers at different universities share scarce computing resources over long-distance connections.
- The first message attempt, intended to spell "LOGIN," crashed the system after transmitting only "LO," a detail often cited as the internet's accidental first word.
- ARPANET originally ran on the Network Control Program; on 1 January 1983, a coordinated switchover known as "flag day" replaced it with TCP/IP, a protocol suite flexible enough to let independent networks interconnect as equals.
- This TCP/IP standardization, more than ARPANET's original 1969 launch, is what let separately built networks worldwide combine into the single system historians generally call the internet.
- Public and commercial use expanded through the 1980s and 1990s as academic, government and eventually private networks adopted the same protocols, setting the stage for the World Wide Web's arrival in 1989 to 1991.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees the internet's true founding moment as a matter of definition: 1969 gave two machines a wire between them, but 1983's protocol switch gave any two networks, anywhere, a shared sound-code for exchanging data without a central authority translating between them. This layered history, invention followed years later by standardization, shows that a tool-lineage's most consequential step is sometimes not its first working demonstration but the later agreement that lets many separate inventions interoperate. This reading would weaken if evidence shows commercial and public adoption would have proceeded just as quickly under a continuation of the older Network Control Program.

## Open Questions
