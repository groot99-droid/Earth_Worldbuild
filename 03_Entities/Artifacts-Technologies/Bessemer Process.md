## Summary
The Bessemer process converts molten pig iron into steel by blowing air through it to burn off impurities, sharply cutting production costs. Henry Bessemer patented and announced it in England in 1856; American ironmaster William Kelly developed a similar method earlier, a genuinely disputed priority claim.

## Facts
- Bessemer publicly described his air-blown steelmaking process at Cheltenham, England, on 11 August 1856, having already filed British patents on it in 1855.
- American ironmaster William Kelly tested a similar air-blast method at Eddyville, Kentucky, from the late 1840s; the exact start date is debated.
- In 1857 the US Patent Office ruled Kelly held priority and issued him US Patent 17,628 that June.
- Kelly did not commercialize his process at industrial scale before the dispute was settled.
- By 1857 both the Bessemer and Kelly patents were licensed to American ironworks, merging the rival claims into one commercial process.

## Context & Connections
The process succeeded the older [[Crucible Steel]] method, which made only small, costly batches. Cheap steel reshaped construction, transport, and manufacturing across [[Europe]] during the [[Industrial Age]], ranking among the defining production breakthroughs in [[Technology]].

## Observer's Reading
Read as tool-lineage, the process shows a technical bottleneck producing near-simultaneous solutions once furnace technology and demand reached a threshold, undercutting any single-inventor story. Bessemer's version prevailed through faster patenting and licensing, not necessarily superior chemistry; the American claim, upheld by patent examiners, is a documented rival case, not a footnote. This reading would weaken if evidence showed direct copying rather than independent convergence.
