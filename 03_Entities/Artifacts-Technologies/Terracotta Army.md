## Summary
The Terracotta Army is a buried set of life-size clay soldiers, horses, and chariots built for the tomb of [[Qin Shi Huang]], first emperor of unified China, and found only in 1974.

## Facts
- On 29 March 1974, farmers digging a well near Lintong, outside Xi'an, struck pottery fragments near the [[Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang]].
- More than 2,000 figures have been excavated from three pits; estimates put the full total above 8,000, most still unexcavated.
- Figures were individually finished, with distinct faces, hairstyles, and armor, and were originally painted in bright pigment that flakes away within minutes of air exposure.
- UNESCO inscribed the Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor, including the army, as a World Heritage Site in 1987.
- The army dates to the reign of Qin Shi Huang (unified China 221 BCE, died 210 BCE); whether construction began at his accession in 246 BCE or later is debated.

## Context & Connections
The army belongs to the funerary complex of the [[Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang]], built under [[Qin Shi Huang]] after the [[Unification of China under Qin]]. It is a [[Classical Antiquity]] site of [[East Asia]]. Threads: [[Art and Ritual]] and [[Religion and Belief]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer reads this as a grief-rite on an extraordinary scale, turning a ruler's command into a buried status-signal meant to outlast him. The individualized faces suggest the makers aimed at a functioning army, hinting that the meaning-engine behind it treated the afterlife as continuous with ordinary rule. The reading would weaken if the unexcavated majority proved mold-repeated rather than individuated. An alternative reading treats the project mainly as status-signal for the living court, with religious intent secondary.
