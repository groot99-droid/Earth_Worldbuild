## Summary
Papyrus is a writing material made from strips of the papyrus plant, first used in Egypt. A blank roll from the tomb of Hemaka at Saqqara dates to about 2900 BCE, and the oldest inscribed papyri, from Wadi al-Jarf, to about 2560 BCE.

## Facts
- A blank papyrus roll was found in the tomb of Hemaka, an official under the First Dynasty king Den, at Saqqara.
- A French team led by Pierre Tallet and Gregory Marouard found the oldest inscribed papyri at Wadi al-Jarf on the Red Sea coast in 2013. They include the logbook of an inspector named Merer, from about the 26th year of Khufu, recording limestone shipments for the Great Pyramid.
- Sheets were made from crossed layers of pressed stem strips and joined into rolls.
- Papyrus was exported around the Mediterranean and was a main writing material of Greece and Rome.
- Parchment and, from about the 10th to 11th century CE, [[Paper]] largely replaced it.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer treats papyrus as an early step in memory-outsourcing that was light enough to travel: records kept in one place could be read in another. The oldest text found so far is a work logbook, not literature, which fits the view that administration drove early mark-code use. The reading would weaken if older religious or literary papyri turn up.
