## Summary
The astrolabe is an astronomical instrument that models the celestial sphere on a flat plate, letting users tell time, find direction, and make astronomical observations. Its underlying mathematics developed in Hellenistic Greek astronomy before the instrument was refined and produced across the medieval Islamic world.

## Facts
- The earliest surviving dated astrolabe is signed by Nastulus and dated 315 AH (927/928 CE), made in the Islamic world in Iraq.
- The instrument's core geometry, stereographic projection, is often credited to Hipparchus (2nd century BCE), though direct evidence is lacking.
- Theon of Alexandria wrote a treatise on the small astrolabe in the 4th century CE, the earliest detailed surviving account of its theory.
- The 8th-century astronomer al-Fazari is traditionally credited with building the first Islamic-world astrolabe, but no example survives.
- Medieval Islamic astrolabes served timekeeping, finding the direction of prayer, surveying, and astronomical calculation.
- In the 10th century al-Sufi catalogued over 1,000 distinct uses for the instrument.

## Context & Connections

## Observer's Reading
The Observer reads the astrolabe as tool-lineage in miniature: pattern-taming for reading the sky, sketched by Hellenistic theorists and turned into a dateable working object by Islamic-world workshops by the early 900s. Its use for finding the direction of prayer shows one instrument serving both a meaning-engine's ritual needs and secular pattern-taming such as timekeeping. The reading would weaken if an earlier dated example surfaced.
