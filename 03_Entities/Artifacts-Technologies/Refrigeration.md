## Summary
Refrigeration is engineered cooling used to preserve food and other perishables. It moved from an 1834 lab-scale prototype to reliable industrial compression systems adopted by brewers, meatpackers, and shippers from the 1850s to the 1880s.

## Facts
- In 1834, Jacob Perkins, US-born and working in Britain, patented a closed-cycle vapor-compression refrigerator using ether: a proof of concept, not commercially developed at the time.
- Between 1850 and 1853, Alexander Twining patented ether vapor-compression ice-making and is credited with early US commercial refrigeration by the mid-1850s.
- James Harrison built an experimental ice machine at Geelong, Australia, in 1851 and a commercial one in 1854; a dozen systems served breweries and meatworks by 1861.
- In 1882 the ship Dunedin, fitted with a compressor, completed the first successful frozen-meat shipment from New Zealand to Britain, after an 1876 attempt had failed.
- Carl von Linde patented an improved ammonia compression process in Germany in the mid-1870s and founded a refrigeration firm in 1879 that had sold about 747 machines by 1890.

## Context & Connections
Refrigeration exemplifies [[Technology]] taking decades to move from principle to industrial reliability, part of the wider [[Industrial Revolution]]. Uptake tracked [[Industrial Age]] [[Europe]]'s brewing and meatpacking industries from the 1850s, and 1880s refrigerated shipping extended [[Trade and Economy]] to long-distance perishable goods.

## Observer's Reading
Seen as a single tool-lineage, refrigeration looks assembled by many hands over five decades: Perkins's 1834 device supplied a working principle that later engineers independently re-derived once materials and refrigerants matured. Its larger effect was widening the exchange-web, letting perishable food cross oceans and loosen diets from local seasons. This reading weakens if earlier, comparably reliable compression devices turn up.
