## Summary
An aqueduct is a channel or bridge that carries water over a distance, often across a valley. One of the earliest known stone examples is the aqueduct at Jerwan in northern Iraq, built for the Assyrian king Sennacherib about 690 BCE to supply Nineveh.

## Facts
- The Jerwan aqueduct dates to about 703 to 690 BCE and carried a canal across a valley on a stone bridge about 280 meters long, built of more than two million dressed limestone blocks.
- The Oriental Institute of Chicago excavated it in 1933 (Thorkild Jacobsen and Seton Lloyd), and the results appeared in 1935.
- It was one part of Sennacherib's canal system for Nineveh, which his royal inscriptions describe.
- The excavators found no comparable structure in the record before Roman times.
- The Aqua Appia of 312 BCE was the first of the aqueducts that eventually brought water to Rome.

## Context & Connections
The aqueduct extended the canal work described in [[Irrigation Canals]] over obstacles, and it stands in the [[Iron Age]] record of [[Mesopotamia]] and the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]]. Later, the [[Romans]] built the best-known aqueducts. The [[Qanat]] solved a similar problem underground, and [[Sanitation and Sewers]] carried used water away. Thread: [[Technology]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees the aqueduct as a tool-lineage step from ditch to bridge: it needed no new principle, only the will to carry water over an obstacle instead of around it. Such works demanded labor and stone in quantity, so they also served as status-signals of imperial power, and Sennacherib recorded his waterworks in royal inscriptions. The reading would weaken if many similar Assyrian works turned up, which would make Jerwan common rather than singular.
