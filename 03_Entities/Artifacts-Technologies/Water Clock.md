## Summary
A water clock, or clepsydra, measures time by the steady flow of water into or out of a vessel. The oldest surviving example is the alabaster clepsydra from Karnak, made in the reign of Amenhotep III in the 14th century BCE, and a written record points to an earlier Egyptian origin.

## Facts
- The Karnak clepsydra was found in 1904 and is now in a Cairo museum. Its alabaster body has twelve columns of hour-marks, and water drips through a small hole in its base.
- A tomb inscription of the official Amenemhet, from the 16th century BCE, credits him with devising a water clock: earlier written evidence than the Karnak vessel itself.
- As the water level fell, the flow slowed, so many designs used sloped sides or constant-level inflow to keep the rate steady.
- In Athens, water clocks timed speeches in the law courts.
- Chinese and Islamic makers built elaborate water-driven clocks; Su Song's 1088 astronomical clock tower is the best known. See [[Mechanical Clock]].

## Context & Connections
The water clock was a [[Bronze Age]] instrument of the [[Nile Valley]] that measured hours when a sundial could not, alongside the [[Calendars]] that counted days. Threads: [[Science]] and [[Technology]]. It later shaped the [[Mechanical Clock]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer treats the water clock as pattern-taming turned into a device: an hour becomes something a vessel can count without anyone watching the sky. Courts using it to fix the length of speeches show a shared measure of time working as story-glue. The reading would weaken if early water clocks proved to be mostly ritual objects with little practical use.
