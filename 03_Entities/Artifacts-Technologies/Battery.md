## Summary
A battery stores chemical energy and releases it as a steady electric current. The first practical version, Alessandro Volta's voltaic pile, was announced in 1800, starting the era of on-demand electricity.

## Facts
- In 1800, Alessandro Volta announced the voltaic pile, stacked zinc and copper discs separated by brine-soaked cloth, in a letter to London's Royal Society.
- The pile was the first device producing a steady, continuous current, unlike earlier brief-discharge devices such as the Leyden jar.
- Volta built it partly to counter Luigi Galvani's "animal electricity" theory, arguing the current came from contact between dissimilar metals, not living tissue.
- Some sources date Volta's construction to late 1799, with public description in 1800; the credited year varies.
- A Parthian or Sasanian-era jar with a copper cylinder and iron rod, found near Baghdad and called the "Baghdad Battery," is rejected as an ancient battery by mainstream archaeologists, who cite missing wiring and favor a scroll-storage use.

## Context & Connections
The voltaic pile placed [[Technology]] and experimental [[Science]] within reach of ordinary workshops, feeding later work on electrochemistry and electromagnetism. Built and copied across [[Europe]], it became an enabling instrument of the [[Industrial Age]] and, much later, of the [[Electrical Power Grid]]. Older ancient-battery claims are not part of this documented lineage.

## Observer's Reading
The pile sits early in the tool-lineage, technology passed down and reworked from one maker to the next: an instrument built to settle a personal quarrel over "animal electricity" that quickly became shared apparatus for further pattern-taming. Crediting one named inventor understates how contested the underlying insight was; this reading would weaken if evidence showed independent earlier construction elsewhere.

## Open Questions
- Was the pile complete by 1799, with 1800 marking only its public announcement?
- How much credit for the design belongs to Volta's response to Galvani's frog experiments?
