## Summary
CRISPR gene editing uses a bacterial defense system, repurposed as a molecular tool, to cut DNA at a chosen location with far greater ease and precision than earlier methods. Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna published the key demonstration in 2012, showing the system could be programmed to target any DNA sequence.

## Facts
- CRISPR sequences (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats) and their associated Cas proteins evolved in bacteria as an immune defense against viruses, storing snippets of past viral DNA to recognize and cut it on reinfection.
- Charpentier and Doudna's 2012 paper showed that the Cas9 protein could be directed, using a single programmable guide RNA, to cut any specified DNA sequence, turning a bacterial immune mechanism into a general-purpose gene-editing tool.
- Following groups rapidly demonstrated CRISPR-Cas9 editing in human and other eukaryotic cells within about a year, driving explosive growth in genetic research, agricultural applications and medical trials.
- Charpentier and Doudna received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing the method, a relatively short eight years after the original publication.
- Clinical applications have included approved treatments for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassemia, while ethical debate continues over heritable human genome editing, sharpened by a Chinese scientist's unauthorized 2018 creation of gene-edited babies.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees CRISPR as pattern-taming borrowed directly from another organism's own tool-lineage: bacteria had already solved the problem of recognizing and cutting specific DNA sequences, and the species' contribution was recognizing and redirecting that solution rather than inventing the mechanism from scratch. The unauthorized 2018 gene-edited births show how quickly a widely accessible, cheap technique can outrun the meaning-engine of ethical consensus meant to govern it. This reading would weaken if evidence shows comparably precise, general-purpose gene-editing tools were already close to independent discovery through non-CRISPR approaches before 2012.

## Open Questions
- How should heritable human genome editing be governed internationally, given how widely accessible CRISPR tools have become?
- How much of CRISPR's rapid clinical translation depended specifically on the 2012 discovery, as against unrelated advances in delivery methods for gene therapies?
