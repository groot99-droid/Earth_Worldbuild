## Summary
Sericulture is the raising of silkworms to produce silk thread. It began in China, where silk fragments from Qianshanyang in Zhejiang date to about 2700 BCE, and silk later became a leading good of Eurasian trade and diplomacy.

## Facts
- Silk fragments from Qianshanyang, a Liangzhu culture site in Zhejiang, date to about 2700 BCE. Earlier claims (a fabric at Qingtaicun in Henan, about 3630 BCE, and possible silk protein at Jiahu) are treated here with more caution.
- The domesticated silkworm, Bombyx mori, descends from the wild moth Bombyx mandarina.
- From the 2nd century BCE, the [[Han Dynasty]] sent silk to the [[Xiongnu]] as gifts under peace arrangements.
- Silk moved west along the routes described in [[Opening of the Silk Roads]].
- According to the Byzantine writer Procopius, two monks brought silkworm eggs to Emperor Justinian in 552 CE, hidden in bamboo rods. This account comes from Byzantine sources, and its details are not independently confirmed.

## Context & Connections
Sericulture is an [[East Asia]] technology of the [[Bronze Age]] whose products shaped the [[Byzantine Empire]] as well as Chinese diplomacy. Threads: [[Technology]] and [[Trade and Economy]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees sericulture as a tool-lineage that could be held as a monopoly of knowledge: a craft that depends on one insect and a few skills can stay within one region for a very long time, and the smuggling stories show how much that knowledge was worth. Silk also worked as a portable status-signal and as a currency of diplomacy. The reading would weaken if the secrecy proved to be later legend rather than practice.
