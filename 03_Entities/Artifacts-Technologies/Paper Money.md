## Summary
Jiaozi, paper notes from private merchants in Chengdu, Sichuan, appeared in the late 10th century as a lighter alternative to heavy iron coin. The [[Song Dynasty]] government took over issuance in the 1020s, usually called the first government-issued paper money.

## Facts
- Private merchant firms in Chengdu began issuing jiaozi, paper receipts redeemable for coin, from around the late 10th century; the exact founding year is undocumented.
- Several private issuers became insolvent and could not redeem their notes, prompting Song restrictions on private issuance.
- In 1023 CE the Song government founded a state monopoly bureau, the jiaozi wu, in Chengdu; standardized government notes circulated from 1024 CE, and sources differ on which year marks the true start.
- Song government jiaozi were valid for limited terms and required periodic paid reissue, unlike a permanent currency.
- Under the Yuan dynasty (13th-14th century) paper currency called jiaochao became the empire's main medium of exchange; Ming notes issued from 1375 later lost much value to overprinting.

## Context & Connections
Jiaozi built on established Chinese [[Paper]] manufacturing and merchant credit practices in Song commerce, part of a wider expansion of markets and credit across the [[Medieval Period]] in [[East Asia]]. Thread: [[Trade and Economy]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer reads jiaozi as story-glue extended into an exchange-web: a printed promise substitutes for metal the species would otherwise carry, as long as everyone keeps believing it. In several surveyed cases that belief broke, when private issuers and later Yuan and Ming states overprinted notes, weakening any reading of paper money as inherently stable. An alternative, narrower reading treats it as simply a practical fix for a local coin shortage.

## Open Questions
