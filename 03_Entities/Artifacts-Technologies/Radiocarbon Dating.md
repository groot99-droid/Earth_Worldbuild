## Summary
Radiocarbon dating estimates the age of organic material by measuring its remaining carbon-14, a method developed by Willard Libby and colleagues at the University of Chicago around 1949, drawing on nuclear physics techniques refined during the Second World War.

## Facts
- Libby's team established that living organisms maintain a roughly constant ratio of radioactive carbon-14 to stable carbon-12 while alive, and that this ratio decays at a known rate, carbon-14's half-life, after death.
- The first published tests, in 1949, dated organic samples of known historical age, such as ancient Egyptian artifacts, to confirm the method's accuracy against independent records.
- Radiocarbon dating is reliable for organic material up to roughly 50,000 years old, beyond which too little carbon-14 remains to measure precisely.
- Later refinement, accelerator mass spectrometry, developed from the late 1970s, allowed accurate dating from much smaller samples than the original counting method required.
- Libby received the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the method, which transformed archaeology, geology and climate science by giving researchers an independent, physics-based timeline instead of relying solely on stratigraphy or written records.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees radiocarbon dating as pattern-taming turned toward the species' own past: a technique built from the physics of nuclear decay let researchers assign approximate years to objects with no written record at all, extending memory-outsourcing backward to periods that left no texts. That the same postwar scientific infrastructure which produced nuclear weapons also produced a tool for reconstructing prehistory shows how one body of knowledge can serve opposite purposes within a single generation. This reading would weaken if the method's known calibration problems, caused by fluctuations in atmospheric carbon-14 over time, prove to have introduced systematic errors larger than currently corrected for.
