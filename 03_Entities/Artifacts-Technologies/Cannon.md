## Summary
A cannon is a metal-barreled weapon firing a projectile using gunpowder's explosive force. It is first attested in China and spread across Eurasia over the following decades.

## Facts
- The Heilongjiang (Acheng) hand cannon is dated by archaeological context to 1288 or earlier, tied to a Yuan campaign against a Mongol rebellion; it carries no inscription.
- The Xanadu Gun, from the ruined Yuan palace of Shangdu, bears a cast inscription corresponding to 1298, the oldest cannon with a manufacturer-recorded date.
- An undated Wuwei bronze cannon, attributed to the Western Xia period, may push cannon-type weapons in China back to the 1220s; others dispute any true gun predating 1259.
- The earliest firmly dated European evidence is a 1326 illustrated manuscript, with recorded use in Latin Europe following in the late 1320s-1330s; the first uncontested Islamic-world firearms are later still, placed by some assessments only in the 1360s.
- Cannon technology spread from Yuan China across Eurasia through the 14th century, reshaping siege warfare wherever it reached.

## Context & Connections
Cannon design built on [[Gunpowder]] weapons of the [[Song Dynasty]], including fire lances, and matured into cast-metal form under the [[Mongol Empire]] in [[East Asia]] in the [[Medieval Period]]. Its westward diffusion tracks routes the Mongol conquests had opened. Threads: [[Technology]] and [[War and Conflict]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer reads the cannon as tool-lineage made concrete: gunpowder, first developed for other ends, is redirected into a device built for sanctioned harm. Transmission across the exchange-web often outpaces the record, so an artifact's absence proves far less than its presence does. The reading would weaken if the Wuwei piece is confirmed to predate 1259. An alternative view treats only the inscribed Xanadu Gun as a safe anchor, since it alone carries a checkable date.
