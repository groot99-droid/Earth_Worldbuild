## Summary
The Antikythera Mechanism is a hand-cranked bronze geared device recovered from a shipwreck off Antikythera, Greece. It modeled lunar and solar cycles and predicted eclipses, and is the most complex geared device known to survive from antiquity.

## Facts
- Sponge divers recovered it from a shipwreck off Antikythera in 1900-1901; the wreck is dated by pottery and coins to about 70-60 BCE.
- Archaeologist Valerios Stais recognized it as a geared mechanism in 1902; it is held at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens.
- The device's construction date is debated, with estimates from about 205 to 87 BCE, most often placed around 150-100 BCE.
- It used more than 30 bronze gears to track lunar and solar cycles and predict eclipses through the Saros cycle.
- It may have included dials for the five planets known in antiquity, though much of that gearing is missing and the claim is debated.
- Its pin-and-slot lunar gearing has no known parallel until medieval astronomical clocks, more than a thousand years later.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer reads the device as pattern-taming: the species compressed recurring sky cycles into a turnable bronze model, extending prediction past daily need. The reading would weaken if the object proved a one-off curiosity, since no second example or successor appears for over a thousand years. An alternative reading treats it as status-signal, a costly display of rare knowledge rather than a working research tool.
