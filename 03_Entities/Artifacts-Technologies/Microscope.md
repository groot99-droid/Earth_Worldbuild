## Summary
The microscope is an optical instrument that magnifies objects too small to see unaided. Compound microscopes appeared in the Netherlands around 1620, and single-lens designs later reached even higher magnification.

## Facts
- Compound microscopes appeared in the Netherlands around 1620; the attribution to Zacharias Janssen, often dated to the 1590s, is now doubted, and priority is contested.
- Cornelis Drebbel demonstrated a compound microscope in London around 1621.
- [[Galileo Galilei]] adapted an existing design into his own instrument, presented in 1624 and called the occhiolino.
- Robert Hooke's Micrographia (1665) used compound-microscope observations of cork to coin the term "cell."
- Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using single-lens microscopes of his own making, was first to observe microorganisms in the 1670s; the Royal Society elected him a fellow in 1680.

## Context & Connections
The microscope emerged in [[Europe]] during the [[Early Modern Period]], alongside the telescope, part of a broader turn toward instrumented observation later systematized as [[Science]]. Its early history involves competing claims among Dutch lensmakers, not one clean inventor.

## Observer's Reading
The microscope reads as part of a pattern-taming project, a tool built to push observation past an inherited sensory limit. In several surveyed cases its earliest credit is unstable, fitting a tool-lineage pattern where a tidy single-inventor story replaces a messier network of lensmakers refining each other's work. This weakens if the disputed 1590s Janssen claim is ever confirmed. An alternative reading treats the instrument as a byproduct of an existing lens trade rather than a triumph of curiosity.
