## Summary
The Cyrus Cylinder is a baked clay cylinder inscribed in cuneiform after Cyrus the Great's conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE. It was found at Babylon in 1879 and is in the British Museum.

## Facts
- Hormuzd Rassam found the cylinder, about 23 centimeters long, during a British Museum excavation at Babylon in 1879. A fragment given to Yale in 1922 was later shown to belong to it.
- Its text presents Cyrus as chosen by the Babylonian god Marduk and describes a peaceful entry into the city.
- It records the return of divine images to their sanctuaries and of deported peoples to their homelands.
- It does not name Jerusalem or the Jewish exiles, though the Book of Ezra credits Cyrus with letting exiles return.
- The cylinder has been called the first charter of human rights. The British Museum and many historians of the ancient Near East reject this as anachronistic.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees the cylinder as story-glue written for a local audience: a conqueror told Babylonians that their own god had chosen him, easing his rule. A meaning-engine and a political claim are joined, as in earlier Mesopotamian royal inscriptions. Its later reading as a modern rights charter is a new story attached to an old object, which every culture does. The reading would weaken if the wording proved aimed mainly at a wider Persian audience.
