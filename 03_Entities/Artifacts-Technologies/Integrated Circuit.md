## Summary
The integrated circuit places multiple transistors and other components on a single piece of semiconductor material. Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments demonstrated the first working example in 1958, and Robert Noyce of Fairchild Semiconductor developed a more practical, manufacturable version in 1959.

## Facts
- Kilby built his prototype in September 1958 using germanium, proving that resistors, capacitors and transistors could function together on one chip rather than as separately wired components.
- Noyce's 1959 design used silicon and a planar manufacturing process that could connect components with printed metal traces, making mass production far more practical than Kilby's hand-wired prototype.
- The two men and their companies pursued separate patents, leading to years of legal dispute over priority before the companies eventually cross-licensed the technology.
- Integrated circuits allowed exponentially more components to fit in the same space at falling cost, a trend later described by Gordon Moore, a Fairchild and later Intel co-founder, as doubling roughly every two years.
- Kilby received the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics for the integrated circuit; Noyce had died in 1990 and so could not share the recognition, since the prize is not normally awarded posthumously.

## Context & Connections

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees near-simultaneous invention again shaping a tool-lineage's origin story, much as with earlier metallurgical and mechanical breakthroughs, when two researchers working separately reached compatible solutions within about a year of each other. That patent law, rather than any technical judgment, decided how credit and royalties were divided between Kilby's and Noyce's approaches shows how claim-lines drawn by courts can outlast the underlying scientific question of priority. This reading would weaken if it is shown that Noyce had direct knowledge of Kilby's specific design rather than developing his silicon-based approach independently.

## Open Questions
