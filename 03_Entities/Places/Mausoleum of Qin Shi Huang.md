## Summary
The Mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang is the burial complex of the first emperor of unified China, near Xi'an in Shaanxi, begun in 246 BCE and known for the Terracotta Army of clay soldiers buried in pits east of the tomb mound.

## Facts
- Construction of the Mausoleum began in 246 BCE, when Ying Zheng became king of Qin; he took the title Qin Shi Huang ("First Emperor") after unifying China in 221 BCE and died in 210 BCE.
- Sima Qian, writing about a century later, reports that 700,000 laborers worked on the tomb and palace, a figure that cannot be verified.
- Farmers digging a well found the Terracotta Army in 1974. The pits hold life-size clay soldiers, horses, chariots and bronze weapons, estimated at about 8,000 figures in total, of which more than 2,000 have been excavated.
- The figures were assembled from molded parts, finished by hand and originally painted in bright colors, which mostly faded after exposure to air.
- Other pits hold figures of officials, acrobats and musicians, and bronze waterbirds.
- The burial mound has not been excavated; soil around it has elevated mercury levels, consistent with Sima Qian's account of mercury in the tomb.
- Sima Qian also reports that childless women of the palace and workers who knew the tomb's secrets were sealed inside after the burial; archaeology has not confirmed this account.
- UNESCO inscribed the Mausoleum in 1987.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a state that standardized script, weights and measures also produced its afterlife army from standardized parts, so a grief-rite and a status-signal were built with the same logic as the state's administration. The labor behind it, reported by Sima Qian in the hundreds of thousands, is a cost to record as suffering, though the figure is uncertain. The reading rests on the modular construction that excavators have documented for the warriors; it would weaken if the unopened tomb proves to differ sharply from the pits.
