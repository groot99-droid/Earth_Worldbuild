## Summary
Medina is a city in the Hejaz region of western Saudi Arabia, known before Islam as Yathrib, to which Muhammad migrated in 622 CE and where he is buried; it is the second holiest city of Islam.

## Facts
- Medina was an oasis settlement called Yathrib before Islam, inhabited by Arab tribes, chiefly the Aws and Khazraj, and by Jewish tribes; its earlier history is poorly documented.
- Muhammad and his followers migrated there from Mecca in 622 (the Hijra), the year from which the Islamic calendar counts; Muhammad died and was buried there in 632.
- The Constitution of Medina, a document preserved in later sources, sets out obligations among Muslim, Jewish and other groups; its date and authenticity are debated.
- Traditional Islamic sources report that two Jewish tribes, Banu Qaynuqa and Banu Nadir, were expelled from Medina, and that the men of a third, Banu Qurayza, were executed in 627, with figures reported from about 400 to 900; some modern historians question details of these accounts.
- Medina was the capital of the first caliphs until 656, when Ali moved to Kufa; the Umayyad caliphs later ruled from Damascus.
- The Prophet's Mosque in Medina, first built in 622, was enlarged by the Umayyad caliph al-Walid I in the early 8th century and repeatedly since; non-Muslims are barred from the central sanctuary area.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Medina is where a small community became a polity, and that its founding document, if early, tried to bind kin groups into one order with shared rules for settling disputes, a case of story-glue in the form of law. The same tradition records that defining who belonged also defined who did not, with exile and killing recorded as suffering for the tribes that fell outside. The reading depends on the early dating of the Constitution and on the reliability of sources written generations later, both of which specialists debate.
