## Summary
Mexico City is the capital of Mexico, built by the Spanish after 1521 on the site of Tenochtitlan in the former Lake Texcoco, and today one of the largest cities in the Americas.

## Facts
- Mexico City was rebuilt by the Spanish on the ruins of Tenochtitlan after the conquest of 1521, with a municipality founded in 1524 as México Tenochtitlán, and it became the capital of the Viceroyalty of New Spain, established in 1535.
- The Metropolitan Cathedral, begun in the 1570s and completed in 1813, stands beside the main square, and the buried Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan was uncovered in excavations after workers found the Coyolxauhqui stone in 1978.
- The lakes were drained by canals and tunnels from the 17th century in response to floods, and the city now covers much of the old lakebed; parts of it have sunk as much as nine meters and some areas still sink by nearly 50 centimeters a year.
- Silver and goods moved through the capital in the Spanish trade system, and the Manila galleons linked New Spain to Asia through the Pacific port of Acapulco.
- Mexican independence was secured in September 1821, with Mexico City as the capital of the new state.
- The soft lakebed amplifies earthquakes: the quake of 19 September 1985 (magnitude about 8) killed about 10,000 people by official counts, with some estimates several times higher, and the quake of 19 September 2017 (magnitude 7.1) killed more than 300.
- The 2020 census counted 9,209,944 people in Mexico City and 21,804,515 in Greater Mexico City, and UNESCO inscribed the Historic Centre and Xochimilco in 1987.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Mexico City is a capital built directly on an earlier capital, with the Spanish cathedral and plaza placed near the old temple precinct, one claim-line drawn over another, and that the pull of the earlier city's site, its ancestor-weight, shaped the choice. The Mexica lived on the lake through causeways and chinampas, while the modern city sinks into the drained lakebed and is managed by pumping and drainage, so a site chosen for power carries its hazards into later centuries. The reading would weaken if records show that the Spanish chose the site mainly for practical reasons of labor and roads and not for its symbolic value.
