## Summary
Jamestown, on the James River in Virginia, USA, was founded in 1607 as the first permanent English settlement in North America and was the capital of the Virginia colony until 1699.

## Facts
- Jamestown was founded in May 1607 by about 105 English men and boys sent by the Virginia Company of London, on land within a paramount chiefdom of many Algonquian-speaking communities led by Wahunsenacawh, whom the English called Powhatan.
- In the winter of 1609 to 1610, known as the Starving Time, hunger and disease reduced the colony from a few hundred people (commonly given as about 500) to about 60, and accounts and archaeological evidence record cannibalism.
- John Rolfe's tobacco, first harvested successfully in 1614, gave the colony an export crop and increased the colonists' demand for land and labor.
- In July 1619 the first representative assembly in English America met at Jamestown; in late August an English privateer, the White Lion, landed about 20 Africans at Point Comfort, downriver, who had been taken from a Portuguese slave ship, and they were traded for food, and some were brought to Jamestown; their legal status is debated.
- A Powhatan attack on 22 March 1622 killed more than 300 colonists, about a third of the English population, and years of war followed; Powhatan deaths from war and introduced disease are poorly recorded.
- Jamestown was burned in 1676 during Bacon's Rebellion, and the capital moved to Middle Plantation, renamed Williamsburg, in 1699 after the statehouse burned in 1698.
- Archaeologists found the remains of the 1607 fort in 1996, after it had long been thought lost to river erosion.

## Context & Connections
Jamestown was an outpost of the [[Columbian Exchange]] in North America, sending tobacco to Europe and receiving people, animals and diseases across the Atlantic, and the arrival of 1619 belongs to the history of the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]]. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Jamestown is a place where two claims to the same land met, an English company's charter and a Powhatan chiefdom's territory, and that the settlement survived only after it found a crop that turned land and labor into exported wealth. The year 1619 is remembered for a representative assembly and for the arrival of enslaved Africans, and the Observer holds that both belong in one account, with the deaths of colonists, Powhatan people and Africans recorded as suffering. The reading would weaken if new records show that Virginia's system of hereditary slavery grew from later laws with little connection to the 1619 arrivals.
