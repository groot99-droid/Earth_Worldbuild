## Summary
Isfahan is a city on the Zayande River in central Iran, a Seljuk capital from 1051 and the capital of the Safavid Empire from 1598, when Shah Abbas I rebuilt its center around the great square Naqsh-e Jahan.

## Facts
- Isfahan stands on the Zayande River on the Iranian Plateau and was settled long before Islam; it became a Seljuk capital in 1051, and its Friday Mosque reflects more than a thousand years of mosque architecture (UNESCO inscribed it in 2012).
- After a revolt in 1387, Timur's forces killed a large part of Isfahan's population; chroniclers give figures of tens of thousands.
- Shah Abbas I moved the Safavid capital from Qazvin to Isfahan in 1598 and built the Naqsh-e Jahan (Imam) Square, the Shah and Sheikh Lotfollah mosques, the Ali Qapu palace and covered markets.
- Estimates by 17th-century European visitors put Isfahan's population at several hundred thousand, and a Persian saying called the city "half the world".
- In 1604 to 1605 Shah Abbas forcibly moved many Armenians from Julfa on the Aras River into Safavid territory, and many died on the way; the survivors founded New Julfa, an Armenian quarter of Isfahan, in about 1606.
- Afghan forces under Mahmud Hotak besieged Isfahan in 1722, and the resulting famine killed many tens of thousands of its inhabitants; the fall of the city was a decisive blow to Safavid rule.
- Isfahan ceased to be the capital in the 18th century, and UNESCO inscribed Meidan Emam, Esfahan in 1979.

## Context & Connections
Isfahan was the capital of the [[Safavid Empire]], a rival of the [[Ottoman Empire]], and it had earlier suffered the massacre ordered by Timur, founder of the [[Timurid Empire]]. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Isfahan under Shah Abbas is a capital designed as a display: a square where a palace, a royal mosque and a merchants' bazaar face one another, placing the ruler's authority, the religious establishment and commerce in one visible arrangement, a status-signal in urban form. The same ruler's forced movement of the Armenians, which the Observer records as suffering, produced a prosperous merchant community that traded silk across Eurasia, an exchange-web outcome of coercion. The reading would weaken if the wealth of New Julfa proves to have come mainly from trading networks that existed before Safavid patronage.
