## Summary
Newgrange is a large passage tomb in the Boyne Valley of County Meath, Ireland, built about 3200 to 3100 BCE by Neolithic farmers and aligned so that the winter solstice sunrise lights its inner chamber.

## Facts
- Newgrange is a mound about 12 meters high and about 85 meters across at its widest, with a passage about 19 meters long leading to a cross-shaped chamber, and radiocarbon dates place its construction at about 3200 to 3100 BCE, older than the Great Pyramid of Giza.
- A "roof box" above the entrance admits sunlight along the passage for about 17 minutes around sunrise at the winter solstice.
- Kerbstones around the base carry carved designs, including spirals, and cremated remains of a small number of people were found in the chamber.
- The white quartz wall at the front was reconstructed in the 1970s, and whether it originally looked like that is debated.
- Newgrange was used again in later periods, and Roman coins and other offerings have been found there; later Irish tradition associated the Boyne complex with legendary figures.
- Newgrange lies with the nearby passage tombs at Knowth and Dowth, and UNESCO inscribed the Brú na Bóinne complex in 1993.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Newgrange has a chamber built so that sunrise light at the winter solstice enters for a short time, which suggests careful, sustained observation of the sky by a farming society that had no mark-code to record what the alignment meant. The meaning is inferred: a calendar, a grief-rite for the dead, or both, and the evidence supports the alignment more strongly than any one explanation of its purpose. The reading would weaken if the roof box is shown to be a later addition and not part of the original design.
