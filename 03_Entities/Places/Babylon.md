## Summary
Babylon was a city on the Euphrates River in present-day Iraq, first mentioned in the late 3rd millennium BCE, the capital of Hammurabi's kingdom and later of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, and a center of astronomy, law and religion.

## Facts
- Babylon lies near Hillah, about 85 kilometers south of Baghdad, and is first mentioned in a tablet from the late 3rd millennium BCE.
- Babylon rose under Hammurabi (reigned about 1792 to 1750 BCE), whose law code was inscribed on a stele found at Susa and now held in the Louvre.
- The Hittite king Mursili I sacked Babylon about 1595 BCE, after which the Kassites ruled it for centuries; the Assyrian king Sennacherib destroyed it in 689 BCE, and his son Esarhaddon rebuilt it.
- Under Nebuchadnezzar II (reigned 605 to 562 BCE) Babylon had the Ishtar Gate, a large ziggurat called Etemenanki and palaces, and his armies destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BCE.
- Cyrus the Great of Persia took Babylon in 539 BCE; the Cyrus Cylinder, a clay text found there in 1879, presents him as chosen by the god Marduk, and claims that it is a charter of human rights are debated.
- Alexander the Great died at Babylon in 323 BCE; the city declined after the Seleucids founded Seleucia on the Tigris.
- Babylonian scribes kept the Astronomical Diaries from about 652 to 61 BCE, one of the longest continuous records of celestial observation.
- No Babylonian text or archaeological trace has confirmed the Hanging Gardens described by Greek writers; some scholars have proposed that they were at Nineveh.
- Military bases of US-led forces at the site in 2003 to 2004 damaged ancient remains, and UNESCO inscribed Babylon in 2019.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Babylon exists in memory in three forms that do not match: the excavated city, the scriptural symbol of exile and pride, and the source of a long astronomical record kept by scribes, an early case of memory-outsourcing in service of pattern-taming. The Hanging Gardens show a story outliving its evidence, since a wonder described by later writers leaves no trace in the city's own records. The reading would weaken if excavation at Babylon or Nineveh finds the gardens.
