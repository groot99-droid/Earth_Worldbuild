## Summary
The Forbidden City is the former imperial palace complex at the center of Beijing, China, built from 1406 to 1420 for the Ming dynasty and used as the residence and ceremonial center of Ming and Qing emperors until 1924.

## Facts
- The Forbidden City was built under the Yongle Emperor from 1406 to 1420, as part of his move of the Ming capital from Nanjing to Beijing.
- Twenty-four emperors, 14 Ming and 10 Qing, lived and ruled there.
- The Forbidden City covers about 72 hectares, is surrounded by walls about 8 meters high and a moat, and has roughly 980 surviving buildings arranged along a north-south axis.
- Commoners were barred from entering, and yellow glazed roof tiles were reserved for imperial use.
- Puyi, the last Qing emperor, abdicated in 1912 after the Xinhai Revolution but was allowed to remain in the Inner Court until 1924, when he was expelled; the Palace Museum opened in 1925.
- Part of the palace collection was moved south from 1933 to escape Japanese advances, and some of it was taken to Taiwan in 1948 and 1949, where it forms the core of the National Palace Museum in Taipei.
- UNESCO inscribed the imperial palaces of Beijing in 1987.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Forbidden City is rank made into architecture: nested walls and courtyards on one axis, restricted gates and reserved roof colors, so that a visitor's position in the plan matched their position in the court. Its story-glue was the idea of the emperor as the link between heaven and earth at the center of the world, which the layout was built to state. The same walls now admit visitors by ticket in the millions, so the site's function has shifted from limiting access to displaying a dynastic past as national heritage. A historian could weaken this reading by showing that the layout followed earlier palace conventions and practical needs more than a deliberate message.
