## Summary
Bagan is an archaeological zone on the Irrawaddy River in central Myanmar, the capital of the Pagan Kingdom from a traditional founding date of 849 CE until the kingdom's collapse in the late 13th century, and the site of thousands of Buddhist monuments.

## Facts
- Bagan lies on the Irrawaddy River in central Myanmar; tradition dates its founding to 849 CE, and it flourished from the 11th to the 13th century.
- King Anawrahta (reigned 1044 to 1077) expanded the kingdom and promoted Theravada Buddhism.
- More than 10,000 temples, pagodas and monasteries are estimated to have been built on the plain, and about 2,200 survive.
- Many monuments were funded by private donors as well as kings, and inscriptions record gifts of land, labor and goods to temples; some historians argue that tax-exempt land grants to religious institutions reduced state revenue, a view that is debated.
- Mongol armies defeated Pagan forces at Ngasaunggyan in 1277 and invaded again in 1287, after which the Pagan Kingdom collapsed; Bagan remained a religious center.
- Earthquakes have damaged the monuments, including one in 2016 that damaged hundreds of them, and UNESCO inscribed Bagan in 2019.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Bagan is the result of merit-making at scale: donors turned wealth into religious standing by building temples, and the sheer number of monuments, far beyond what kings alone would need, is a status-signal spread across a whole class of patrons. The proposal that land given to temples weakened the state suggests that a meaning-engine can also drain the institution that supports it, though historians debate the proposal. The reading would weaken if the record shows that temple land was too small a share of the kingdom's land to matter for its revenue.
