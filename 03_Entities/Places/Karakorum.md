## Summary
Karakorum was the capital of the Mongol Empire in the Orkhon Valley of central Mongolia, built from 1235 under Ögedei Khan, and it was damaged and razed by Ming forces in the 1370s and 1380s.

## Facts
- Karakorum stood in the Orkhon Valley; Chinggis Khan is reported to have set up a camp there around 1220, and his son Ögedei ordered a walled city built from 1235.
- Craftsmen and captives from conquered lands, including China, Central Asia and Europe, were settled at Karakorum to work as artisans.
- William of Rubruck, a Flemish Franciscan sent by Louis IX of France, visited Karakorum in 1254 and described temples of several traditions, mosques, a Christian church, and a silver tree that dispensed drinks, made by the French goldsmith Guillaume Boucher.
- After Kublai Khan took power in 1260 and shifted the center of the empire to northern China, Karakorum declined.
- Ming forces damaged the city in 1372 and razed it in 1380, and one passage dates the destruction of the permanent palace to 1388; the Buddhist monastery of Erdene Zuu was built nearby from 1585, partly with stone from the ruins.
- The ruins of Karakorum were identified in the 19th century and excavated by a Soviet-Mongolian expedition in 1948 and 1949; UNESCO inscribed the Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape, which includes the site, in 2004.

## Context & Connections
Karakorum was the capital of the [[Mongol Empire]] during the period of the [[Mongol Conquests]], in a valley that also holds inscriptions of the earlier [[Göktürk Khaganate]], and it was destroyed by forces of the [[Ming Dynasty]]. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Karakorum was a fixed point built by a people whose power rested on mobility, a place to gather tribute, envoys and skilled captives, where the silver tree served as a status-signal aimed at foreign delegations. It was the center of the empire for only a few decades before power moved toward the taxable cities of northern China, which suggests that the capital followed the revenue and not the herds. The forced resettlement of artisans is a reminder that such a capital was built partly through sanctioned harm. The sources, mostly written by outsiders, do not settle how much the city mattered to the Mongol elite compared with the camps they moved between.
