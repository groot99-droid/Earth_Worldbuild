## Summary
Chang'an was the capital of the Western Han dynasty from 202 BCE and, in a rebuilt form, of the Sui and Tang dynasties, on the site of present-day Xi'an in Shaanxi, China, until it was dismantled in 904 CE.

## Facts
- Chang'an was founded as the Han capital after 202 BCE, with the Changle Palace completed in 200 BCE; the census of 2 CE recorded about 250,000 residents.
- The Sui dynasty built a new planned city, Daxing, southeast of the Han site from 582, and the Tang, from 618, kept its plan and the name Chang'an.
- Tang Chang'an was a grid of walled wards, with gates closed at night, enclosing about 78 square kilometers, with a central avenue and two state-supervised markets. Estimates of its population, with suburbs, vary by source, from several hundred thousand up to about one million.
- Chang'an received envoys and merchants from Central Asia, Persia, Korea, Japan and Tibet. The monk Xuanzang returned there in 645 with Buddhist texts from India, and the Nestorian Stele, recording a Christian community, was erected there in 781.
- Chang'an fell to rebels in 756 during the An Lushan Rebellion and was briefly held by Tibetan forces in 763. In 904 the warlord Zhu Wen forced the court to move to Luoyang and had the city's palaces and houses pulled down.
- The plan of Chang'an influenced the layout of the Japanese capitals Nara and Kyoto.

## Context & Connections
Chang'an was founded just after the fall of the dynasty created by the [[Unification of China under Qin]], and it served as the capital of the [[Han Dynasty]] and the [[Tang Dynasty]]. It was the eastern end of the routes described in [[Opening of the Silk Roads]], where [[Sogdians]] were prominent traders, and its plan was echoed in [[Nara]] and [[Kyoto]]. Chang'an sits in [[Classical Antiquity]], with its peak in the [[Medieval Period]], in the region [[East Asia]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[Trade and Economy]], [[Religion and Belief]] and [[Art and Ritual]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Tang Chang'an turned social order into geometry: walled wards, gates closed at night, two supervised markets, and one avenue leading to the palace. A plan with claim-lines this exact was later copied at Nara and Kyoto, which suggests that a floor plan can carry story-glue about what a capital should look like. Its end in 904 shows the reverse, that a capital's meaning could be moved by moving the court and taking down the buildings. This reading would weaken if the Japanese copying turns out to have been driven mainly by practical drainage and defense rather than a shared idea of order.
