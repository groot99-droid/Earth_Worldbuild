## Summary
Nara is a city in Japan's Kansai region that was the capital, then called Heijō-kyō, from 710 to 784 CE, a period in which the Japanese court adopted Chinese models of city planning, writing and state Buddhism.

## Facts
- Heijō-kyō was established as the capital in 710 under Empress Genmei, on a grid plan modeled on the Tang capital Chang'an.
- Court scholars compiled the chronicles Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720) at Nara, written with Chinese characters.
- A smallpox epidemic in 735 to 737 is estimated by some historians to have killed a quarter to a third of Japan's population, though the figure is uncertain.
- In 743 Emperor Shōmu ordered a giant bronze Buddha, which was dedicated at Tōdai-ji in 752; the statue is about 15 meters tall.
- The Shōsōin storehouse at Tōdai-ji preserves objects from the 8th-century court, including items that reflect trade along the Silk Roads.
- The court moved to Nagaoka in 784 and to Heian-kyō (Kyoto) in 794, a move often attributed in part to the political influence of Buddhist institutions at Nara.
- UNESCO inscribed the Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara in 1998.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Nara shows a court importing a whole order at once: a Chinese city plan, Chinese characters, a state-sponsored Buddhism, and chronicles that used memory-outsourcing to fix the ruling house's origin story in writing. The Great Buddha was ordered a few years after a smallpox epidemic that killed a large part of the population, which suggests that a ruler facing mass death turned to a meaning-engine and to public works, though that link is an inference from the sources. The reading would weaken if the Buddha project proves to have been mainly a response to court rivalries.
