## Summary
Hattusa was the capital of the Hittite Empire, at Boğazkale in central Turkey, made the royal seat in about 1650 BCE and abandoned around 1200 BCE, and it preserved an archive of about 30,000 clay tablets.

## Facts
- Hattusa lies at Boğazkale in Çorum Province, Turkey; a Hattian settlement existed there earlier, and King Hattusili I made it the Hittite capital in about 1650 BCE.
- Hattusa was ringed by walls with monumental gates, including the Lion Gate and the Sphinx Gate, and had a Great Temple and a nearby rock sanctuary at Yazılıkaya.
- The capital moved briefly to Tarhuntassa under Muwatalli II and returned to Hattusa under Mursili III.
- Excavators, beginning with Hugo Winckler in 1906, found about 30,000 clay tablets in the royal archives, written in Hittite, Akkadian, Luwian, Hurrian and other languages and holding treaties, rituals and letters, including a version of the treaty with Egypt made after the Battle of Kadesh.
- Bedřich Hrozný identified Hittite as an Indo-European language in 1915.
- Hattusa was abandoned around 1200 BCE, during the Bronze Age Collapse; its central buildings burned, though whether the city was abandoned before or after the fire is debated.
- UNESCO inscribed Hattusha in 1986.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Hattusa is a case of memory-outsourcing at imperial scale: a state kept its treaties, rituals and letters on clay in several languages, and the archive survived in part because fire hardens clay, so the destruction of the city helped preserve its records. Treaties written in the diplomatic language of the day, Akkadian, show rulers of rival powers binding one another with story-glue in the form of oaths sworn before gods. The archive records what the palace chose to keep, and the reading would weaken if the tablets prove to reflect chance survival more than the state's actual priorities.
