## Summary
The Great Wall of China is a series of walls, ditches, towers and forts built across northern China by successive states and dynasties from about the 7th century BCE to the 17th century CE, mainly to control movement between farming societies and the steppe.

## Facts
- The Great Wall began as separate walls built by Warring States kingdoms such as Qi, Yan, Zhao and Qin from about the 7th century BCE (the dating of the earliest walls is uncertain); after unifying China in 221 BCE, Qin Shi Huang linked and extended walls in the north.
- The Han dynasty extended walls and signal towers far to the northwest along the Gansu corridor, to protect the routes westward and to counter the Xiongnu.
- The best-known standing sections, in brick and stone, date to the Ming dynasty (1368 to 1644), and building intensified after Mongol forces captured the Ming emperor Zhengtong at Tumu in 1449.
- A Chinese government survey published in 2012 reported about 21,000 kilometers of walls, trenches and natural barriers built across all dynasties, of which about 8,850 kilometers are Ming.
- Soldiers, conscripted laborers and convicts built the wall; how many died is unknown, and the large figures in later stories are not documented.
- UNESCO inscribed the Great Wall in 1987. The claim that it is visible to the naked eye from the Moon is false.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Great Wall of China is a claim-line built at very large scale, yet it worked less as a sealed barrier than as a filter, with towers, garrisons and gates that let a state watch and tax movement between two ways of living. The image of one unbroken wall is partly a later story-glue, and some historians argue it grew mainly in the 19th and 20th centuries as a national symbol. Specialists could weaken this reading by showing that the wall's military effect was larger than a filtering account allows.
