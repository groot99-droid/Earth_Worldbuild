## Summary
Havana is the capital of Cuba, on the island's northwest coast, established on its present harbor site in 1519 and long the assembly point for Spanish treasure fleets crossing the Atlantic.

## Facts
- Havana was founded as San Cristóbal de la Habana; the settlement moved twice and was established on its present harbor site in 1519.
- From the 1560s Spanish fleets carrying silver from Mexico and Peru assembled at Havana for the crossing to Spain, and the city was fortified with La Real Fuerza (built 1558 to 1577) and El Morro (built 1589 to 1630).
- Britain captured Havana after a siege in 1762 during the Seven Years' War and returned it to Spain in 1763 in exchange for Florida; Spain then built the La Cabaña fortress.
- Sugar and coffee plantations, worked by enslaved people, expanded Cuba's economy from the late 18th century; hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans arrived in Cuba, many through Havana, and slavery there was abolished in 1886.
- The US battleship Maine exploded in Havana harbor on 15 February 1898, killing about 260 crew, an event that helped lead to the Spanish-American War.
- Since 1959 Havana has been the capital of a socialist state, and the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 placed Cuba at the center of the Cold War.
- UNESCO inscribed Old Havana and its fortifications in 1982.

## Context & Connections
Havana was a key port for the silver flow from [[Potosi]] and the mainland, for the [[Sugar Plantation Complex]] and for the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]], and it changed hands during the [[Seven Years' War]]. It was a focal point of the [[Cold War]]. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Havana grew because a state needed a fixed place to gather and guard a moving stream of wealth: silver had to be assembled somewhere before the crossing, and fortifications followed the cargo. Its later wealth from sugar rested on enslaved labor, which the Observer records as suffering and not only as an economic input. The waterfront was claimed in turn by the leading naval power of each era, and a historian could challenge this by showing that local Creole elites and enslaved people shaped the city as much as outside powers did.
