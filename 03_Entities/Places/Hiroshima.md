## Summary
Hiroshima is a city on the delta of the Ōta River in western Honshu, Japan, founded as a castle town in 1589 and, on 6 August 1945, the first city struck by a nuclear weapon.

## Facts
- Hiroshima was founded as a castle town in 1589 by the daimyo Mōri Terumoto and grew into a military and port center; its population before the bombing was about 350,000.
- At 8:15 a.m. on 6 August 1945 the US B-29 Enola Gay dropped a uranium bomb, "Little Boy", which exploded about 600 meters above the city.
- About 70,000 to 80,000 people are estimated to have been killed immediately, and total deaths by the end of 1945 are estimated at 90,000 to 166,000, from burns, injuries and radiation. The dead included civilians, soldiers, Korean forced laborers and a small number of Allied prisoners of war.
- Survivors, called hibakusha, faced radiation illness, increased cancer risks and discrimination; the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission began long-term studies of them in 1947, continued by the Radiation Effects Research Foundation.
- A second atomic bomb was dropped on Nagasaki on 9 August, and Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945; whether the bombings were necessary to end the war remains debated among historians.
- The ruined Genbaku Dome was preserved as a memorial within the Peace Memorial Park, and UNESCO inscribed it in 1996.

## Context & Connections
Hiroshima was the target of the first nuclear attack of [[World War II]] and gave its name to the beginning of the [[Nuclear Age]]. The city of [[Kyoto]] had been on the list of possible targets and was removed from it. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Hiroshima marks the first use of a nuclear weapon against a city, in a single morning, and records the deaths as suffering that continued for decades among survivors. The city has since turned its ruined center into a memorial, keeping the shell of one building standing and collecting survivors' testimony, a grief-rite and an act of memory-outsourcing meant to reach people who were not there. Historians disagree about whether the bombing was necessary to end the war, and this reading takes no side in that dispute. It would weaken if the memorial's message proves to matter less to visitors than to the city that built it.
