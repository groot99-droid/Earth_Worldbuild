## Summary
The Nile River is a river of northeastern Africa, commonly cited as about 6,650 kilometers long though estimates vary by measurement method, flowing north to the Mediterranean, whose annual flood supported farming and the states of Egypt and Nubia from about the 6th millennium BCE.

## Facts
- The Nile flows north from the highlands of east and central Africa to the Mediterranean; its two main branches, the White Nile from the Lake Victoria region and the Blue Nile from Lake Tana in Ethiopia, meet at Khartoum, and the Ethiopian highlands supply most of the seasonal flood.
- Farming communities are documented in the Nile Valley and the Fayum from about the 6th millennium BCE, and the summer flood deposited silt that kept the valley fertile.
- Egyptian officials measured the flood on nilometers, and in later periods taxation was tied to its height.
- The states of Egypt, after the Unification of Egypt, and of Kush, upstream in Nubia, grew along the river.
- The Aswan High Dam, completed in 1970, ended the annual flood in Egypt and created Lake Nasser; about 100,000 Nubians were relocated, and monuments such as Abu Simbel were moved.
- Eleven countries share the Nile basin. A 1959 agreement between Egypt and Sudan allocated most of the river's measured flow to those two states, and Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, begun in 2011 and filled in stages from 2020, is a subject of dispute over water with Egypt and Sudan.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the Nile turned a predictable flood into a state: a river whose height could be measured, forecast and taxed rewarded record-keeping, an early case of pattern-taming tied to administration. The river's modern history shows that claim-lines drawn across water are harder to keep than lines on land, since a 1959 allocation between two states now faces the claims of upstream countries. The reading would weaken if the flood proves to have been too variable to forecast well enough to shape administration.
