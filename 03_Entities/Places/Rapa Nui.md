## Summary
Rapa Nui (Easter Island) is a remote Pacific island settled by Polynesians around 1200 CE, famous for nearly a thousand monumental stone statues called moai.

## Facts
- The island lies in the southeastern Pacific, thousands of kilometers from the nearest large land.
- Estimates for the arrival of the Polynesian settlers range from as early as about 300 CE, based on contested early radiocarbon dates, to about 1200 CE, the date favored by many recent studies.
- About 400 moai (397 in one count) lie in the quarry of Rano Raraku in various stages of completion, and about 95 percent of the island's monolithic statues were carved from its volcanic tuff. Published counts of the total number of moai differ.
- The popular story of self-inflicted ecological collapse is contested. Researchers point to introduced rats, which may have been the main cause of deforestation, and to disease and slave raids. In December 1862 Peruvian slavers captured about 1,000 islanders, and reports put the total taken in 1862 to 1864 at more than 1,400. Most of the captives died in Peru, and a smallpox epidemic that followed the return of a few survivors in 1863 reportedly killed about a thousand more. These figures come from popular summaries and vary between accounts.
- Rapa Nui National Park was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1995.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the island is often told as a parable of self-destruction, but the record suggests that outside pressures did much of the damage. Stories that fit a moral are chosen more readily than stories that fit the evidence.
