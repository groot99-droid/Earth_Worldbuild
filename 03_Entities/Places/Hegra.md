## Summary
Hegra, also called Mada'in Salih, is an archaeological site in the AlUla region of northwestern Saudi Arabia, the second major city of the Nabataean kingdom after Petra, known for 111 monumental rock-cut tombs.

## Facts
- Hegra lies north of the AlUla oasis on a caravan route between southern Arabia and the Mediterranean, and was part of the Nabataean kingdom from about the 1st century BCE.
- UNESCO records 111 monumental tombs cut into sandstone outcrops, 94 of them with decorated facades, along with dozens of wells; it inscribed Hegra in 2008, the first UNESCO site in Saudi Arabia.
- Many tombs bear Nabataean inscriptions naming the owner and family, forbidding sale or reuse, and threatening penalties or curses; dated inscriptions fall mostly in the 1st century CE.
- After Rome annexed the Nabataean kingdom in 106 CE, Hegra became part of the province of Arabia, and Roman inscriptions record a military presence there in the 2nd century.
- Islamic tradition associates the site with the people of Thamud mentioned in the Quran, and travelers traditionally avoided it.
- The Ottoman Hejaz Railway built a station at Mada'in Salih in 1907 to 1908.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that at Hegra a claim-line is engraved beside the grave: tomb inscriptions record who may be buried, who may not sell, and what penalty follows, so that property law reached past death in stone. A trading people put its most durable writing into grief-rites and inheritance, while most of its everyday commercial records have not survived. The reading rests on the surviving inscriptions being representative of Nabataean practice, which is uncertain because they come from a few unusually well-preserved sites.
