## Summary
Petra is a rock-cut city in the mountains of southern Jordan that served as the capital of the Nabataean kingdom from about the 2nd century BCE until Rome annexed the kingdom in 106 CE.

## Facts
- Petra lies in a basin among sandstone mountains, entered through a gorge about 1.2 kilometers long known as the Siq.
- Greek writers describe an expedition against the Nabataeans in 312 BCE and their rock refuge, which is sometimes identified with Petra, though the identification is doubtful.
- Petra's carved facades, including al-Khazneh ("the Treasury"), date mostly from about the 1st century BCE to the 1st century CE. Most facades are thought to be tombs, though the use of some, including the Treasury, is debated.
- Petra was supplied by dams, channels, ceramic pipes and cisterns in a desert climate, and its wealth is generally attributed to the caravan trade in incense and other goods. Estimates of its peak population are uncertain, with about 20,000 commonly cited and some figures as high as 30,000.
- After Roman annexation in 106 CE, Petra became part of the province of Arabia. An earthquake in 363 CE destroyed many buildings and the city declined afterward; the Swiss traveler Johann Ludwig Burckhardt brought it to European attention in 1812.
- UNESCO inscribed Petra in 1985.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Petra's most visible investment, the carved facades, is largely funerary, so a trading people's wealth became permanent chiefly as grief-rites and status-signals for the dead, while the caravans left little trace. A city that lived on stored water and control of a passage, not on farmland, shows how control of a route can stand in for control of land. The reading depends on the tomb attribution, which is well supported for many facades but debated for others such as the Treasury.
