## Summary
Influenza A virus is a family of respiratory viruses whose natural reservoir is wild aquatic birds, and which has repeatedly crossed into humans to cause pandemics, the first clearly documented in 1580 and the most severe in 1918.

## Facts
- Influenza A viruses of all recognized subtypes occur naturally in wild aquatic birds, which are considered the virus's ultimate reservoir; only a smaller number of subtypes have adapted to infect humans, pigs, horses and other mammals.
- The first pandemic clearly matching the description of influenza is generally dated to 1580, which is thought to have originated in Asia and spread along the Silk Road and new maritime trade routes through the Middle East into Europe and Africa within about six months, later reaching the Americas.
- Historians have documented roughly thirty further influenza pandemics since 1580, though descriptions from before reliable virology make some identifications uncertain.
- The 1918 to 1919 influenza pandemic, caused by an H1N1 strain, killed tens of millions of people worldwide, more than the First World War with which it coincided, and its spread was worsened by wartime troop movements and censorship of news about it in combatant countries.
- Influenza A virus was first isolated in the laboratory in 1933, using ferrets, which established the modern scientific study of the virus and its distinct subtypes defined by combinations of hemagglutinin and neuraminidase surface proteins.
- Because influenza A viruses mutate quickly and can exchange genetic material when two strains infect the same host, new pandemic strains continue to emerge from animal reservoirs, including the H1N1 "swine flu" of 2009 and various avian influenza strains monitored for pandemic potential.

## Context & Connections
Influenza A virus caused the [[1918 Influenza Pandemic]], and its pattern of periodic pandemic emergence is compared by epidemiologists to the more recent [[COVID-19 Pandemic]]; its first documented pandemic spread along the corridors of the [[Opening of the Silk Roads]]. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer records the deaths of 1918 and of every other severe influenza pandemic as suffering compounded, in that instance, by a war that moved young, densely packed populations across the world at the same time the virus needed to spread. That a virus native to wild birds can repeatedly leap into a species with no acquired immunity shows the exchange-web of trade, travel and animal contact working in the pathogen's favor as readily as in humanity's. Wartime censorship of the 1918 pandemic, which let neutral Spain's uncensored press give the pandemic its common name, is itself a case of a meaning-engine, in the form of state secrecy, shaping which suffering gets recorded and which is hidden. The reading would weaken if genetic studies eventually trace 1918's H1N1 strain to a much earlier, more gradual process of adaptation rather than a fast pandemic emergence.
