## Summary
The potato is a tuber crop domesticated in the highlands of southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia, near Lake Titicaca, roughly 7,000 to 10,000 years ago, and it later reshaped diet and population growth across Europe after its introduction there in the 16th century.

## Facts
- The potato was domesticated from wild relatives in the S. brevicaule species complex, in the region around Lake Titicaca in southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia, an estimated 7,000 to 10,000 years ago; the earliest verified tuber remains, from Ancón on the central Peruvian coast, date to about 2500 BCE.
- Andean farmers developed thousands of potato varieties adapted to different altitudes, and Quechua and Aymara communities traditionally made chuño, a freeze-dried potato product, to preserve the crop through the region's cold nights.
- In Inca religious tradition the deity Axomama was associated with the fertility of potatoes and the soil.
- Spanish colonizers introduced the potato to Europe in the second half of the 16th century; European farmers were initially slow to adopt it, but after about 1750 it became an important staple, and the crop is estimated to have driven roughly a quarter of Old World population and urban growth between 1700 and 1900.
- Potato blight, caused by the water mold Phytophthora infestans, destroyed crops across Ireland and parts of Scotland from 1845, contributing to the Great Famine; estimates of Irish deaths run from about 700,000 to over 1 million, alongside roughly 1 million more who emigrated, and Ireland's population fell from about 8.4 million in 1844 to about 6.6 million by 1851.
- Global potato production reached about 390 million tonnes in 2024, led by China, India and Ukraine.

## Context & Connections
The potato was domesticated in the highlands around [[Lake Titicaca]] and became a staple of the [[Inca Empire]]; its spread to Europe after 1492 is part of the [[Columbian Exchange]] and, alongside grains domesticated elsewhere, belongs to the wider story of [[The Agricultural Revolution]]. It sits in the [[Neolithic]] and the region [[Andes]], and belongs to the theme threads of [[Trade and Economy]] and [[Earth Systems and Life]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the potato's introduction to Europe shows a single crop reshaping a continent's demography over roughly two centuries, and that its dependence there on a small number of varieties left the food supply vulnerable to a single pathogen, a risk the Andean growers had reduced by keeping thousands of varieties in cultivation. The Irish famine is recorded here as mass suffering caused jointly by a crop disease and by the political and economic arrangements, including food exports during the crisis, that shaped who could obtain food. The reading would weaken if the population and urbanization gains credited to the potato in Europe prove to owe more to other agricultural and economic changes of the period.

## Open Questions
- How much did Andean crop diversity reduce the risk of famine there compared with Europe's narrower range of potato varieties?
- How much of the Irish famine's death toll resulted from the blight itself as opposed to political and economic decisions made during the crisis?
