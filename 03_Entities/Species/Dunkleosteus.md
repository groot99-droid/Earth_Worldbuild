## Summary
Dunkleosteus was a large armored placoderm fish of the Late Devonian with bony blades in place of teeth. Its best-known species, D. terrelli, comes from the Cleveland Shale of Ohio; its size has been revised downward and its bite force is under review.

## Facts
- The genus is reported from the Late Devonian, about 382 to 359 million years ago, but D. terrelli is best documented from about 372 to 360 million years ago, chiefly in the Cleveland Shale of Ohio. Ten species have been named, and about four are widely accepted.
- Older reconstructions gave lengths of 6 to 10 m. A 2023 study using the eye socket and cheek plate estimated about 3.4 m for typical adults and about 4.1 m for the largest. It has not been independently replicated.
- A 2007 study modeled the skull as a linked lever system and estimated a bite force of about 4,400 newtons at the tip and 5,400 at the blade edge, for an individual then thought to be 6 m long. A 2025 study argues that these figures need re-examination.
- The name was coined in 1956 by Jean-Pierre Lehman to honor David Dunkle. Placoderms died out at the end-Devonian Hangenberg event, about 359 million years ago.

## Context & Connections
Dunkleosteus lived in the [[Devonian Period]], the Age of Fishes, alongside lobe-finned relatives of [[Tiktaalik]] during the [[Fish-to-Tetrapod Transition]]. Placoderms declined in the [[Late Devonian Extinction]]. Compare the arthropod predators, [[Eurypterids]]. See [[Earth Systems and Life]] and [[Science]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the animal long shown as the great predator of its age shrank by about half on re-measurement, the ordinary condition of deep-time knowledge, in which a confident number is a reading of a few skulls. A whole class of armored fishes left no descendants while a lobe-finned relative went on to land, and nothing in the record marked which would. The reading would weaken if independent studies reproduced the older, larger sizes.
