## Summary
Pikaia is a small, eel-like swimming animal from the Burgess Shale of British Columbia, about 505 million years old. Reinterpreted in 1979 as an early chordate, it is often cited as a relative of vertebrate ancestors, though not thought to be one.

## Facts
- Pikaia gracilens is known only from the Walcott Quarry of the Burgess Shale, dated to about 505 million years ago by fossil zones, with no radiometric date and no later fossils.
- It is about 5 cm long and flattened from side to side, with about 100 muscle blocks, a bilobed head with short tentacles, a dorsal fin, and paired appendages.
- Charles Walcott described it in 1911 as a worm. In 1979 Simon Conway Morris and Harry Whittington read it as a chordate, and a 2012 redescription of 114 specimens read a narrow strand as a notochord. A 2024 study argued that earlier reconstructions were upside down.
- Older chordate-like animals from Chengjiang, about 518 million years old, include Haikouichthys, so Pikaia is not the earliest known chordate.
- In Wonderful Life (1989), Stephen Jay Gould used Pikaia to argue that history is contingent. Conway Morris later argued that evolution converges on similar solutions.

## Context & Connections
Pikaia lived in the [[Cambrian Period]] soon after the [[Cambrian Explosion]], sharing the Burgess Shale with [[Hallucigenia]] and [[Anomalocaris]]. It is a relative of the lineage leading to vertebrates, later including fishes such as [[Tiktaalik]]. See [[Earth Systems and Life]] and [[Science]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Pikaia became famous less for what it was than for the argument it served: a fossil small enough to lose in a tray of shale was used to ask whether the species' own lineage could have failed to exist. Contingency and convergence are competing readings of the same fossils, and Pikaia settles neither. The reading would weaken if the Cambrian record showed many independent chordate lineages, making chordate survival nearly certain.

## Open Questions
- Is Pikaia a stem-group chordate or a convergent form?
- How much did chordate survival depend on lineages like it?
