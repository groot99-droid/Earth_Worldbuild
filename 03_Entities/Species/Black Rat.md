## Summary
The black rat (Rattus rattus) is a rodent native to South Asia and Southeast Asia that became one of the most widespread commensals of humans, spreading with trade and shipping across Eurasia and later the world, and acting as a major vector of plague.

## Facts
- Genetic studies find well-differentiated black rat lineages native to South Asia, the Himalayan region and Indochina, with diversification beginning long before human involvement; exactly when black rats first became commensal with people in that homeland is not precisely dated.
- Black rats spread out of South Asia along trade routes to the Near East and Egypt in antiquity, and documented Roman-era shipping and trade carried them across the Mediterranean and into the wider Roman Empire, reaching as far as Britain by about the 1st century CE.
- As a commensal species, the black rat thrives in and around human granaries, ships and settlements, feeding on stored grain and waste, which let it travel wherever people and cargo moved.
- Fleas carried on black rats are considered a major vector of Yersinia pestis, the bacterium behind the Plague of Justinian and the Black Death, though the disease's spread beyond rat-infested areas suggests other means of transmission, including human fleas and lice, also mattered.
- The black rat was later displaced from much of Europe and North America by the larger brown rat (Rattus norvegicus), which spread from Asia in the 18th century, though black rats remain widespread in warmer regions and on many islands.
- As an invasive species on islands without native rodents, black rats have caused the extinction or decline of ground-nesting birds and other native species, a continuing ecological effect of their spread.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: the black rat shows that an exchange-web built for grain and cloth can carry unintended passengers, so that trade and sanctioned harm are connected through a rodent that neither side of any transaction intended to move. Wherever ships and granaries created new habitat, this one species followed without domestication, a hitchhiker riding humanity's own tool-lineage of storage and transport. The reading would weaken if genetic dating eventually shows the rat's major dispersals tracking climate and rodent competition more than human shipping.
