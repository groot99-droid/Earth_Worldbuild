## Summary
The tea plant is native to the borderlands of southwestern China, northeastern India and northern Myanmar, drunk in China by at least the 2nd century BCE, and its later global trade helped provoke both British cultivation in colonial India and the Opium Wars with China.

## Facts
- Chinese tradition credits the legendary ruler Shennong with discovering tea in 2737 BCE, but the earliest physical evidence, tea leaves found in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han near Xi'an, dates to the 2nd century BCE, and the earliest written reference to boiling tea appears in a text from 59 BCE.
- Tea drinking and its associated ceremonial culture spread from China to Korea, Japan and Vietnam during the Tang dynasty, taking root in Japan as the practice later known as chado.
- Portuguese priests and merchants brought tea to Europe in the 16th century, and the Dutch East India Company shipped tea from Macao to Java in 1607, the first recorded tea shipment by a European trading power; tea became fashionable in Britain after King Charles II married the tea-drinking Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza in 1662.
- Britain's growing demand for Chinese tea created a large trade imbalance paid for in silver, and British merchants turned to selling opium into China to offset the cost, a trade dispute that escalated into the Opium Wars.
- The British introduced Chinese tea plants to India in 1836, and the East India Company sent Robert Fortune to China in 1848 to obtain more; planters then found a distinct variety growing wild in Assam and hybridized it with the Chinese plant, the Alubari garden in Darjeeling opened in 1856, and tea reached Ceylon in 1867.
- Global tea production reached about 29.76 million tonnes in 2022, with China supplying about 49 percent and India about 20 percent; China, India, Kenya and Sri Lanka together account for roughly 81 percent of world output.

## Context & Connections
Tea's ceremonial culture was carried across East Asia during the [[Tang Dynasty]], following earlier consumption recorded under the [[Han Dynasty]], and the trade imbalance it created between Britain and China was a direct cause of the [[Opium Wars]]; British cultivation of tea in India became a pillar of the economy of the [[British Raj]]. ## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a plant first recorded as an elite and ceremonial drink later became the trigger for an armed conflict over a trade imbalance, showing how a taste adopted by one society can force hard choices on a distant government. Britain's substitution of opium for silver, and its later transplanting of tea cultivation to India, both read as attempts to escape dependence on a single exchange-web by force or by relocation. The reading would weaken if the Opium Wars are shown to have had causes more central than the tea trade imbalance.

## Open Questions
- How distinct were the Assam and Chinese tea varieties before their hybridization, and how much did the Assam discovery owe to prior local cultivation?
- How much of the decision to trade opium for tea reflects a considered strategy versus an improvised response to a worsening balance of payments?
