## Summary
The domestic cat descends from the African wildcat and was first tamed in the Near East by Neolithic farmers, with the earliest evidence of a tamed wildcat buried near a human grave on Cyprus about 7500 to 7200 BCE.

## Facts
- Domestic cats descend from the African wildcat (Felis lybica), and the earliest known sign of a tamed wildcat is a burial found close to a human Neolithic grave at Shillourokambos, southern Cyprus, dated to about 7500 to 7200 BCE.
- The leading explanation is that African wildcats were drawn to early farming settlements in the Fertile Crescent by rodents, especially the house mouse, and were tolerated and then tamed, a long relationship that lasted thousands of years.
- Cats were venerated in Egypt from about 3100 BCE, and Egyptian wildcats later contributed to the maternal gene pool of the domestic cat.
- As farming spread, so did cats: they reached Greece about 1200 BCE with traders, southern Europe by the 5th century BCE and northern Germany by the 5th century CE.
- In the United States alone domestic cats are estimated to kill 1.3 to 4.0 billion birds and 6.3 to 22.3 billion mammals a year, and abandoned pets add to a global feral cat population that has driven declines of bird, mammal and reptile species.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer offers a reading, not a fact: the cat is the strongest case for the idea that an animal may have chosen the arrangement more than humans did, since a hunter of grain-store rodents needed no training and asked only to be left alone, so the phrase "who domesticated whom" fits here better than for most species. The same animal was honored in Egyptian ritual and now, in large numbers outdoors, is a predator of wild birds and small mammals, a reminder that a meaning-engine's favorite creature can also be an ecological actor. The reading would weaken if the Cyprus burial proves to be an isolated taming with no descendants among modern cats.
