## Summary
Ban Zhao (about 45 to about 117 CE) was a Chinese historian and scholar of the Eastern Han dynasty who helped complete the Book of Han after her brother's death and wrote Lessons for Women. She was also a tutor and adviser at court.

## Facts
- Ban Zhao was born about 45 CE (some sources say about 49) into a scholarly family. Her father Ban Biao and her brother Ban Gu wrote much of the Book of Han (Hanshu), a history of the Western Han dynasty.
- Ban Gu died in prison in 92 CE with the history unfinished, and Ban Zhao helped complete it by supplying the missing part of the Eight Tables.
- She served at the imperial court as a tutor to the empress and her ladies and, from about 106 CE, as an adviser to the regent Empress Deng.
- She is believed to have written Lessons for Women (Nüjie), in seven chapters, about 106 CE, though the date is not firmly established. It argues that girls should be educated and sets out rules of conduct for wives.
- Later writers used Lessons for Women as a standard text for teaching girls obedience, and modern scholars disagree on whether it reinforced or challenged limits on women.
- She died about 117 CE; some sources say about 120.

## Context & Connections
Ban Zhao worked under the [[Han Dynasty]] in [[East Asia]] during [[Classical Antiquity]]. See [[Language and Writing]] and [[Kinship and Society]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that one woman helped complete the standard history of a dynasty and also wrote the text most used to shape women's conduct, so her legacy points in two directions at once. Because Lessons for Women was copied by later readers who valued its advice, the record may reflect what they wanted from her as much as what she meant. The reading would weaken if evidence showed the text was heavily edited after her lifetime.

## Open Questions
- Did she intend Lessons for Women as a defense of women's education, as a guide to conduct, or both?
