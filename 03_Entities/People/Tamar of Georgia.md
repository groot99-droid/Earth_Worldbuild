## Summary
Tamar (born about 1160 or 1166, died 1213) ruled the Kingdom of Georgia from 1184 to 1213, presiding over its political and cultural high point, later called the Georgian Golden Age.

## Facts
- She was crowned co-ruler with her father, George III, in 1178 to secure her succession, then became sole monarch on his death in 1184, ruling with the formal title "king of kings" rather than a feminine equivalent.
- Her armies won major victories against regional Muslim powers, including the Battle of Shamkor in 1195 and the Battle of Basian (dated to 1202, 1203 or 1204 depending on the source), expanding Georgian influence across much of the South Caucasus and into parts of Armenia and Anatolia.
- Her reign saw significant patronage of Georgian literature and architecture, including the era's most celebrated work, Shota Rustaveli's epic poem "The Knight in the Panther's Skin," traditionally associated with her as its inspiration.
- She was canonized as a saint by the Georgian Orthodox Church, and centuries of later Georgian tradition remember her reign as the kingdom's greatest period of security and cultural achievement.

## Context & Connections
Tamar ruled Georgia in the [[Levant and Anatolia]] region during the [[Medieval Period]], expanding Georgian power at the expense of neighboring Muslim states. See [[Religion and Belief]], [[War and Conflict]] and [[Kinship and Society]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Tamar's title, "king" rather than "queen," suggests Georgian tradition saw sovereignty itself as inherently masculine even while accepting a woman as its holder, a distinction that separates the office from the person occupying it more sharply than modern titles usually do.
