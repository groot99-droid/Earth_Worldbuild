## Summary
Catherine de' Medici (1519 to 1589) was queen of France and, as mother to three kings, one of the most powerful figures in France during the Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots.

## Facts
- Born into the powerful Medici banking family of Florence, she married the future Henry II of France in 1533 and became queen in 1547.
- After Henry's death in 1559 she was the chief political influence for her sons Francis II, Charles IX and Henry III, though she was not formally regent under Francis II or Henry III and held the regency only for Charles IX (as gouvernante de France from December 1560 until August 1563), during a period of escalating conflict between Catholics and Protestant Huguenots.
- She is widely held responsible, though the exact extent of her role is debated, for approving the assassination of Huguenot leaders, which began with the shooting of Admiral Coligny on 22 August 1572 (he survived, then was killed two days later as the massacre began) and triggered the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre in August 1572, in which about 2,000 to 3,000 Huguenots were killed in Paris and more in other cities.
- She pursued marriage alliances and diplomacy across Europe to secure the Valois dynasty's position, though her efforts did not prevent the eventual extinction of the Valois line with the death of her last son in 1589.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that Catherine's later reputation as a scheming, sinister figure was shaped heavily by hostile Huguenot pamphlets written during her lifetime and repeated by later historians; separating a mother managing three weak sons' survival in a genuinely lethal court from a caricature of poison and plotting is not straightforward from the surviving record.
