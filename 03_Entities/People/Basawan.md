## Summary
Basawan was a Hindu painter in the imperial workshop of the Mughal emperor Akbar, active from about 1560 to about 1600, though he is described as flourishing between 1580 and 1600 and the earlier date rests on his earliest mention, in a Tutinama of about 1556 to 1561. His birth and death dates are unknown, so the dates here mark his period of activity. Akbar's historian ranked him among the best of the workshop's more than a hundred painters.

## Facts
- Almost nothing is known of Basawan's life beyond his paintings and a brief praise in Abu'l-Fazl's Ain-i-Akbari. He was a Hindu and probably joined the imperial workshop as a young man in the 1560s. His earliest mention is in an illustrated Tutinama of about 1556 to 1561, and he is described as flourishing between 1580 and 1600.
- Akbar reigned from 1556 to 1605. The workshop's Persian masters, Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad, had trained in the tradition of Behzād and directed Indian artists in producing vast illustrated manuscripts, among them the Hamzanama, 1,400 large paintings.
- Abu'l-Fazl praised his skill in composition, faces, colour and portraiture, and wrote that many critics preferred him even to the celebrated Daswanth.
- His name is written in the margins of paintings in the Razmnama (a Persian translation of the Mahabharata), the Darabnama, the Akbarnama and small books of Persian poetry made in Lahore in the 1590s. In large history manuscripts he often designed the composition and another artist added the colour.
- From 1580 Jesuit missionaries brought European engravings and paintings to Akbar's court. He studied them closely, adapting their shading and deep space, and was among the first Indian painters to absorb European modelling and perspective.
- He was active until about 1600. His son Manohar became a leading painter under Jahangir. His paintings are held in the Victoria and Albert Museum, the British Library and the Chester Beatty Library.

## Context & Connections
Basawan worked in the [[Mughal Empire]] in [[South Asia]] during the [[Early Modern Period]]. His workshop's founders trained in the tradition of [[Behzad]]. See [[Art and Ritual]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes an imperial workshop that fused Persian masters, Hindu painters and European prints into one style, a deliberate joining of tool-lineages by a ruler who wanted his history illustrated. What survives of the individual is a name in a margin, written by librarians, and one passage of praise, so the personality that scholars trace is reconstructed from a labour that was often divided between designer and colourist. Any reading of him rests on attribution and should be held loosely.
