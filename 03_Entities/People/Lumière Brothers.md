## Summary
Auguste Lumière (1862 to 1954) and Louis Lumière (1864 to 1948) were French manufacturers of photographic plates who patented the Cinématographe in 1895 and staged the first paying public screening of projected motion pictures. The dates here run from Auguste's birth to his death.

## Facts
- The brothers were born in Besançon and grew up in Lyon, where their father Antoine ran a portrait studio and later a factory making dry photographic plates.
- After their father saw a demonstration of Edison's single-viewer Kinetoscope in 1894, they set out to build a device that could project moving images to a seated audience. Louis is generally credited with the key mechanical insight, adapting the sewing machine's intermittent claw movement to advance the film.
- They patented the Cinématographe in February 1895, one device that could shoot, print and project. Their first paying public screening was on 28 December 1895 in the Salon Indien of the Grand Café in Paris, traditionally described as a basement on the Boulevard des Capucines: ten short films for roughly thirty-five to forty people, though accounts differ.
- Their "actuality" films were usually a single static, unedited shot of under a minute. L'Arroseur arrosé is a rare staged gag, effectively cinema's first fiction film. Key works include Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory (1895) and The Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat (1896).
- They sent camera operators around the world, spreading projected cinema to India, Japan, Australia and the Americas within a year or two.
- By 1905, about a decade after the first screening, they had largely withdrawn from film production. In 1907 they introduced the Autochrome, an early commercially successful colour photography process.

## Context & Connections
The Lumières worked in Lyon and Paris, in [[Europe]] in the [[Industrial Age]]. [[Georges Méliès]] attended their first screening. See [[Art and Ritual]] and [[Technology]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the decisive step was social rather than mechanical: a device that let a seated crowd watch the same moving image at once is story-glue in miniature, strangers sharing a spectacle in a dark room for a fee. The brothers treated the medium as a product and stepped back from it by 1905, while a magician they had refused a camera made it an art. The attribution of the key mechanical idea to Louis alone is common but hard to check when two brothers shared credit.
