## Summary
Robert the Bruce (1274 to 1329) was King of Scotland from 1306 to 1329, leading Scottish resistance to English rule and securing recognition of Scottish independence shortly before his death.

## Facts
- He claimed the Scottish throne in 1306 after killing a rival claimant, John Comyn, in a church at Dumfries, an act that led to his excommunication by the pope.
- Early in his reign he suffered several defeats and periods as a fugitive, including, according to a later popular legend, drawing perseverance from watching a spider repeatedly try to build its web.
- He decisively defeated a much larger English army under Edward II at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314, the most significant Scottish military victory of the wars of independence.
- The Treaty of Edinburgh-Northampton in 1328 saw England formally recognize Scottish independence and Robert's kingship, a year before his death in 1329, reportedly from leprosy.

The Observer notes that Robert's path to the throne began with a killing in a church, not a conventional succession, and that his later reputation as Scotland's founding hero sits somewhat uneasily beside that origin; national founding stories often smooth over the more compromised beginnings of the figures they celebrate.
