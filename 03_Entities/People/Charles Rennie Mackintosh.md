## Summary
Charles Rennie Mackintosh (1868 to 1928) was a Glasgow architect-designer who led the "Glasgow Style" of Art Nouveau, treating buildings, furniture and interiors as one unified art. His Glasgow School of Art and total interior schemes made him a major influence on European design, though home recognition came only after his death.

## Facts
- Born in Glasgow in 1868, Mackintosh trained as an architect while attending evening classes at the Glasgow School of Art, where he met his future wife, artist Margaret Macdonald.
- With Macdonald, her sister Frances and Herbert MacNair, he formed "The Four," a symbolist group whose style became the Glasgow Style.
- At Honeyman and Keppie he won an anonymous 1896 competition for his own art school's new building; its 1909 completion made his reputation.
- He then designed houses, tearooms and a school around Glasgow, including Hill House (1902-1904), the Willow Tearooms (1903) and Scotland Street School (1903-1906), with Macdonald designing the furnishings throughout.
- His towering ladder-back chairs, shaped as much to define a room as to be sat in, became his best-known design.
- Commissions dried up after 1910, through changing taste and his own difficult manner with clients; by 1914 he had left Glasgow for England.
- In the 1920s he and Macdonald settled in southern France, where he turned to watercolours; he returned to London for cancer treatment and died there in 1928, in obscurity and hardship.
- Though nearly forgotten in Britain by his death, his work had already shaped Josef Hoffmann and the Wiener Werkstätte via the 1900 Vienna Secession, and now ranks among the era's most important architect-designers.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer sees a status-signal built for one city's cafes that became, without its maker's help, an export. Mackintosh treated a building, its chairs and its wall stencils as one problem, a case of making an environment cohere as a single statement of taste; his chairs traded comfort for silhouette. His ideas reached Vienna and shaped Hoffmann's workshop even as his home market lost interest, reputation and trade travelling along separate exchange-webs. His late turn to watercolours reads as withdrawal, not collapse, and the admiring frame around his career is largely later historians' work.
