## Summary
Hieronymus Bosch (about 1450 to 1516) was a painter from 's-Hertogenbosch in the Duchy of Brabant who filled his panels with hybrid monsters and crowds of tiny figures acting out human sin and folly. Only about 25 paintings are firmly attributed to him.

## Facts
- Bosch was born Jheronimus van Aken about 1450 in 's-Hertogenbosch. His grandfather, father and uncles were painters, and he later signed his works "Jheronimus Bosch" after the town.
- Sometime between 1479 and 1481 he married Aleid Goyaerts van den Meervenne, of a wealthy family. In 1486 or 1487 he became a sworn member of the Illustrious Brotherhood of Our Blessed Lady, and he died in 1516.
- He seems to have spent almost his whole life in and near 's-Hertogenbosch, and after marrying he and Aleid moved to nearby Oirschot, yet his fame spread. Philip the Handsome, Duke of Burgundy, commissioned a Last Judgment from him in 1504.
- He painted in oil on oak panels, often as triptychs. Key works include The Garden of Earthly Delights (about 1495 to 1505), The Ship of Fools (about 1500 to 1510), The Temptation of St Anthony (about 1501) and the Haywain Triptych (about 1510 to 1516).
- King Philip II of Spain acquired many Bosch paintings in the late 16th century, which is why the Prado in Madrid holds his greatest paintings.
- [[Pieter Bruegel the Elder]] built on his crowded moral landscapes. After being partly forgotten, Bosch was claimed in the 20th century by Surrealists such as Salvador Dalí and Max Ernst.
- In 2016, the 500th anniversary of his death, the Bosch Research and Conservation Project re-examined his works, and several attributions changed in both directions, with a Temptation of St Anthony credited to Bosch himself after being given to his workshop, and other works questioned as workshop pieces.

## Context & Connections

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes a painter who gave visible form to the fears of a meaning-engine, hell, folly and temptation, in crowded panels meant to be read slowly and closely. The same images were later read by Surrealists as dreams, so what the pictures meant changed with the audience. With only about 25 firmly attributed works and reattributions as recent as 2016, the artist himself is still being redrawn, and any account of what he intended should be held loosely.
