## Summary
Christopher Wren (1632 to 1723) was an English mathematician and astronomer, a founder of the Royal Society, who became an architect after the Great Fire of London in 1666. He designed more than fifty City churches and a new St Paul's Cathedral, topped out in 1708 and declared complete by Parliament in 1711.

## Facts
- Wren was born in East Knoyle, Wiltshire, the son of a clergyman. He was appointed professor of astronomy at Gresham College at 25, then Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford, and was a founding member of the Royal Society in 1662.
- His architectural career began with the Sheldonian Theatre in Oxford. The Great Fire of September 1666 destroyed old St Paul's and most of the City.
- He was appointed Surveyor of the King's Works in 1669 and spent the rest of his working life on the City's parish churches and on St Paul's. He died in 1723, aged 90, and is buried in the cathedral crypt.
- He combined the classical vocabulary of Inigo Jones and Palladio with French and Italian Baroque elements he studied on a research trip to Paris, the only time he travelled outside England.
- The dome of St Paul's is three independent shells nested inside one another, so that exterior profile, interior ceiling and structural core could each be optimised separately.
- Key works include the Royal Observatory, Greenwich (1675 to 1676), the Royal Hospital Chelsea (1682 to 1692) and the Monument to the Great Fire (1671 to 1677).
- Many City churches were later damaged or demolished, by Victorian redevelopment and by German bombing, but a substantial number still stand. Photographs of the dome above the smoke of the Blitz in 1940 circulated worldwide.

## Context & Connections
Wren worked in [[Europe]] during the [[Early Modern Period]] and the [[Scientific Revolution]], and drew on [[Andrea Palladio]]'s classical vocabulary. See [[Art and Ritual]], [[Science]] and [[Technology]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a fire which destroyed a city also gave a mathematician the chance to become its chief builder, so that catastrophe became the hinge of a career and a skyline. The triple-shell dome is pattern-taming applied to stone. St Paul's later became a story-glue for national endurance through the photographs of 1940, a meaning laid on the building by later events rather than one Wren intended.
