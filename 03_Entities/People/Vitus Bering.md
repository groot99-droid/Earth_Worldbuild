## Summary
Vitus Bering (1681 to 1741) was a Danish-born officer in the Russian navy who led two expeditions across Siberia to chart the waters between Asia and North America. The strait and sea between the continents carry his name.

## Facts
- Bering was baptized on 5 August 1681 in Horsens, Denmark, and joined the Russian navy in 1704 under Peter the Great. He died on 19 December 1741 on the island now named for him.
- On the First Kamchatka Expedition (1725 to 1730) he sailed through the strait between Siberia and Alaska in 1728 without sighting the American coast, though some historians think it was visible.
- The Great Northern Expedition (1733 to 1743) grew to about 3,000 people, one of the largest scientific undertakings of its time.
- In 1741 his ship, the St. Peter, and a second ship under Aleksei Chirikov, the St. Paul, sailed separately toward America. Chirikov's crew sighted land about a day earlier, on 15 July by the dates given in the sources; Bering's crew sighted Mount Saint Elias on 16 July.
- On the return voyage the St. Peter wrecked on an uninhabited island. Bering died there, a death traditionally put down to scurvy and exposure, though a 1991 analysis of his remains judged heart failure the likely cause; of 77 men aboard, 46 survived to reach Kamchatka the next year.
- The Cossack Semyon Dezhnev had already passed through the strait in 1648, but Bering's voyages brought it to European attention, and James Cook named it for him in 1778.

## Context & Connections
Bering's expeditions extended Russian reach across [[Siberia and the Arctic]] during the [[Early Modern Period]], following the earlier [[Russian Expansion into Siberia]] led by figures such as [[Yermak Timofeyevich]]. See [[Science]] and [[Trade and Economy]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a strait already known to a Cossack in 1648 became attached to a Danish officer's name because his voyage reached the meaning-engine of European science and cartography. The expedition's scale, thousands crossing a continent to build ships on its far shore, shows a state's reach as much as one man's skill. A specialist could argue that Chirikov's crew, not Bering's, first reached America in 1741, which would weaken a reading that credits the discovery to Bering alone.
