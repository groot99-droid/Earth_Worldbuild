## Summary
Hilma af Klint (26 October 1862 to 21 October 1944) was a Swedish painter who, from 1906, created hundreds of abstract paintings guided by her spiritual beliefs, several years before Kandinsky, Mondrian and Malevich. She kept them largely hidden, and they reached an international audience only from the 1980s.

## Facts
- Af Klint was born at Karlberg Palace in Solna, near Stockholm. She studied at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Stockholm from 1882 to 1887 and made a living painting landscapes, portraits and botanical illustrations.
- Her spiritual investigations, which sources date from 1879, developed around the time of the death of her younger sister Hermina in 1880 and drew her toward spiritualism and Theosophy. With four other women, who began as members of the Edelweiss Society, she made up "The Five", which held séances and experimented with automatic drawing, which the sources date to 1896.
- In 1906, believing she had received a commission from a spiritual guide, she began the Paintings for the Temple, a cycle of nearly 200 works completed in 1915. She considered herself a medium through whom the works were created.
- In 1908 she showed her work to Rudolf Steiner. His cool response contributed to a four-year break in the series. She later spent long periods at the Goetheanum, the Anthroposophical Society's headquarters, between 1921 and 1930, and filled more than 125 notebooks, with sources giving figures from 125 to more than 150.
- She believed the world was not ready for the paintings and directed that they not be exhibited until 20 years after her death. She died after a traffic accident, leaving more than 1,200 paintings and drawings to her nephew.
- Her abstract works combine geometric and organic forms with letters and symbols. In her colour code blue often stood for the female spirit, yellow for the male spirit and pink or red for physical and spiritual love. She drew on Theosophy, Christianity, botany and the new sciences of evolution and atomic physics.
- She exhibited only a handful of times in her lifetime, mostly at spiritual conferences. Her work reached an international audience in the 1980s, including in the 1986 exhibition The Spiritual in Art at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art. The Guggenheim's Paintings for the Future (2018 to 2019) drew more than 600,000 visitors, the most visited exhibition in its history.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes work made for a temple of a meaning-engine rather than for a market, and withheld by its maker because she thought the world was not ready, so that art history was written for about eight decades without it. She framed authorship as mediumship, which sits awkwardly with the modern habit of crediting an individual inventor. The claim that she "invented abstraction" before Kandinsky depends on dating the works and defining abstraction, and the question of who was first is a story-glue of art history.
