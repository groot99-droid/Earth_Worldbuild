## Summary
Enheduanna (flourishing around 2300 BCE; the span of about 2285 to 2250 BCE is only an estimate) was a high priestess of the moon god Nanna at the Sumerian city of Ur and a daughter of Sargon of Akkad. She is often called the earliest author known by name, though her dates and the attribution of her texts are debated.

## Facts
- Enheduanna served as en-priestess of Nanna at Ur, appointed by her father Sargon of Akkad. Her dates depend on the chronology used.
- A disc found at Ur in 1927 by Leonard Woolley's team shows a figure identified by inscription as Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon, in a ritual scene.
- Sumerian poems are attributed to her, including 42 temple hymns and hymns to the goddess Inanna such as "The Exaltation of Inanna."
- The surviving copies come from schools of the Old Babylonian period, about 1800 BCE, some five to six centuries after her lifetime, and scholars disagree about whether she wrote every attributed text.
- In "The Exaltation of Inanna" the speaker describes being driven from office by a usurper, Lugal-ane, and restored; the historicity of this is debated.

## Context & Connections
Enheduanna wrote in the Sumerian language of the [[Sumerians]] while serving a ruler from the Akkadian-speaking north, in [[Mesopotamia]] during the [[Bronze Age]]. Her work belongs to the story of the [[Invention of Writing]]. See [[Religion and Belief]], [[Language and Writing]] and [[Art and Ritual]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a conquering dynasty placed a royal daughter at the head of a conquered city's main cult, a way of joining a new claim-line to old sacred authority through the kin-lattice. The same priestess is the first author recorded by name, so mark-code served a political and a devotional purpose at once. Because the texts survive only in later copies, the reading would weaken if the attribution to her proved to be a later tradition.
