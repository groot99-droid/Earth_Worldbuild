## Summary
Giotto di Bondone (about 1267 to 1337) was a Florentine painter who gave medieval figures weight, emotion and space. Many call him the father of Western painting, and his frescoes in the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua are his firmest work.

## Facts
- Giotto was born about 1267, traditionally in the Mugello valley north of Florence, though his birthplace is disputed and some recent research points to Florence itself. Vasari's story of the shepherd boy discovered by Cimabue is legend, but he was almost certainly trained in Florentine workshops.
- He was traditionally credited with the Legend of St Francis frescoes at Assisi in the late 1290s, but it is now generally accepted that four different hands are identifiable there and that they came from Rome, so his authorship is doubtful; his early career is still debated.
- He painted the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua about 1303 to 1305 for the banker Enrico Scrovegni. It is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- He worked in Rome, in Santa Croce in Florence, and at the court of King Robert of Anjou in Naples from about 1328 to 1333.
- In 1334 Florence named him master of works for the cathedral, and he designed its bell tower. He died in January 1337 and was buried with public honours.
- He worked in buon fresco, painting into wet plaster one day's section at a time. His figures have volume and react to one another, and his spaces suggest depth more than 100 years before linear perspective was worked out.
- In the chapel's Adoration of the Magi the star is painted as a blazing comet, perhaps inspired by Halley's Comet of 1301. The European Space Agency named its 1986 Halley probe Giotto.
- Vasari opened his Lives of the Artists with Cimabue and Giotto. Masaccio and [[Michelangelo]] studied and copied Giotto's frescoes.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that the subject was fixed, the story of a meaning-engine repeated in every church, and that the change lay in how the story was made felt. Giotto's figures glance and grieve, so that sacred scenes read as human drama. The title "father of Western painting" comes largely from Vasari and later writers who needed a founder for a story of revival, which makes it partly a story-glue for art history rather than a plain finding. His early career remains debated, and the Assisi attribution is doubtful, since the St Francis cycle is now generally thought to be by Roman painters.
