## Summary
Kamāl ud-Dīn Behzād (also spelled Bihzad, born about 1455 to 1460 and died about 1535) is the most celebrated painter in the Persian tradition. He led the royal manuscript workshops at Herat and later Tabriz, and many works are attributed to him on grounds of style.

## Facts
- Behzād was probably born about 1455 to 1460 in Herat, capital of the Timurid dynasty founded by [[Timur]]. Orphaned young, he was brought up by the painter Mirak Naqqash, head of the royal library, and was protected by the poet and minister Mir Ali-Shir Nava'i.
- Under Sultan Husayn Bayqara, who ruled Herat from 1469 to 1506, he became the leading artist of the royal kitabkhana, the library workshop. His most famous surviving works date from this period, including paintings in a Bustan of Sa'di (1488) and a Khamsa of Nizami (1494 to 1495).
- After Herat fell to the Uzbeks in 1506 and then to the Safavid Shah Ismail I in about 1510, he moved to Tabriz, where in 1522 Ismail appointed him director of the royal library. He died about 1535.
- A legend says that during the Battle of Chaldiran in 1514 the shah hid Behzād in a cave, in some tellings together with a famous calligrapher, because he valued him more than his army.
- His figures have individual faces and gestures and are often busy with everyday tasks. He often hid his signature discreetly in the architecture of a painting.
- His followers Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad trained in his tradition and went on to found the Mughal imperial workshop under Humayun and Akbar.
- His name became a byword for excellence, which led to many false attributions. Scholars debate which works are by his own hand.

## Context & Connections
Behzād worked first in the [[Timurid Empire]] and then in the [[Safavid Empire]], on the [[Iranian Plateau]] during the [[Medieval Period]]. See [[Art and Ritual]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that a royal library workshop, where calligraphers, illuminators, painters and binders worked together, is an institution of memory-outsourcing: it made the books through which a court remembered its poems and histories. The individual name attached to such a collective product then became a standard of excellence, and standards attract false claims. Much of what is called Behzād's may be the work of others, and for many pictures style is the only guide.
