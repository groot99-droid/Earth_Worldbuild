## Summary
Georges Méliès (1861 to 1938) was a Paris stage magician who became a filmmaker and invented cinema's first special effects. Over roughly 500 films in under twenty years, including A Trip to the Moon (1902), he turned the new camera from a device for recording reality into a machine for staging the impossible.

## Facts
- Méliès was born in Paris to a prosperous shoe-manufacturing family and wanted to study painting, but his father would not support him as an artist and he supervised machinery at the family factory. In about 1888 he bought the Théâtre Robert-Houdin, a famous conjuring theatre.
- After attending the Lumière brothers' first public film screening in December 1895, he tried to buy a camera from them and was refused. He acquired one from a British inventor and built a studio at Montreuil with a glass-walled stage.
- About 1896 he discovered, by accident when his camera jammed mid-shot, that stopping and restarting filming could make objects vanish or transform. This became the "stop trick" behind his trick films.
- His techniques included stop-motion substitution, multiple exposure, dissolves, painted sets built to force perspective, and hand-tinted colour done frame by frame by a team of women colourists.
- American piracy of his films, the rise of narrative studio filmmaking and the disruption of the First World War ruined his business. He was reduced to running a toy stall in a Paris train station.
- He was rediscovered in his last decade, awarded the Légion d'honneur and, by some accounts, given a small pension. He died in Paris in 1938. Martin Scorsese's 2011 film Hugo dramatised his life.
- Key works include The Vanishing Lady (1896), A Trip to the Moon (1902), The Kingdom of the Fairies (1903) and The Impossible Voyage (1904).

## Context & Connections
Méliès worked in France, in [[Europe]] in the [[Industrial Age]], beside the [[Lumière Brothers]], and his business collapsed in the aftermath of [[World War I]]. See [[Art and Ritual]] and [[Technology]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes a magician who saw in a recording device a stage: the same machine that recorded ordinary life in the Lumières' hands made the impossible in his. A camera jam is the standard account of the origin of the stop trick, the sort of founding accident a tool-lineage likes to remember, and it should be treated as an account rather than a proven event. That piracy and the market for narrative film ruined him shows an early case of the copy outrunning the maker.
