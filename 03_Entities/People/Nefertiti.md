## Summary
Nefertiti (about 1370 to about 1330 BCE) was the Great Royal Wife of the Egyptian king Akhenaten and is shown with unusual prominence in the art of his reign. Her exact birth and death dates and her burial place are uncertain, and the dates given are approximate.

## Facts
- Nefertiti married Akhenaten (then Amenhotep IV) early in his reign; the exact dates of the marriage are uncertain.
- The couple promoted the worship of the sun disk Aten and moved the royal capital to a new city, Akhetaten (modern Amarna).
- Reliefs and statues show Nefertiti at a scale and in roles usually reserved for the king, including taking part in rites for the Aten.
- They had six daughters, including Meritaten and Ankhesenamun, who later married Tutankhamun.
- Scholarship before 2012 assumed she vanished from the record around year 12 of the reign, but an inscription dated to year 16 names Neferneferuaten-Nefertiti alongside Akhenaten. Whether she died, changed title, or ruled as co-regent under the name Neferneferuaten is debated.
- A painted limestone bust found in 1912 in the workshop of the sculptor Thutmose at Amarna is held in Berlin; Egypt has requested its return.
- Later king lists omitted Akhenaten and his immediate successors.

## Context & Connections
Nefertiti's life is tied to [[Akhenaten]], among the [[Ancient Egyptians]] of the [[Nile Valley]] in the [[Bronze Age]]. See [[Religion and Belief]], [[Art and Ritual]] and [[Kinship and Society]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that one sculpture made her among the best-known faces of antiquity, yet the surviving record covers a short period that later rulers tried to erase from the king lists. Her size in the reliefs works as a status-signal, and it is unclear whether it records real power or the couple's use of the royal family as a pair of divine images. The reading would weaken if evidence showed such scale was a convention for queens of the period.

## Open Questions
- Did she rule after Akhenaten's death, and under what name? Egyptologists still debate this, and a year 16 inscription shows she was still recorded late in the reign.
