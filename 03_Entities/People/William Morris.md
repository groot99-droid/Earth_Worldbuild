## Summary
William Morris (1834 to 1896) was an English designer, writer and socialist, the central figure of the Arts and Crafts movement. He rejected cheap mechanised ornament in favour of patterns drawn from nature and made by traditional craft methods.

## Facts
- Morris was born in Walthamstow in 1834 into a family that had grown wealthy through copper mining. At Oxford he met the painter Edward Burne-Jones and, through him, the circle around Dante Gabriel Rossetti.
- He built Red House (1859 to 1860) and, unable to find furnishings he considered well made, founded a decorative arts firm in 1861 with Burne-Jones, Rossetti, Philip Webb and others. It was reorganised in 1875 as Morris & Co.
- He founded the Socialist League in 1884 and wrote extensively on politics and utopian fiction. In 1891 he founded the Kelmscott Press; its illustrated Chaucer was completed months before his death in 1896.
- He designed from real plants, disciplining them into flattened repeating patterns. He revived hand block-printing and indigo-discharge dyeing and letterpress printing from hand-cut type over faster industrial methods.
- His principle was "have nothing in your house that you do not know to be useful or believe to be beautiful". His goods were luxuries that generally only the wealthy could afford.
- Morris & Co. wallpapers and textiles, including Strawberry Thief (1883) and Willow Bough (1887), remain in production. The Kelmscott Chaucer launched the private-press movement.

## Context & Connections
Morris worked in [[Europe]] in the [[Industrial Age]], in reaction to the [[Industrial Revolution]], and his machine-averse approach contrasts with that of [[Christopher Dresser]]. See [[Art and Ritual]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes a socialist who made luxury goods that mostly the wealthy could buy, an irony the record itself notes, so that an ethic of honest labour ran through a status-signal. His refusal of the machine was itself a claim about tool-lineage: that the hand-made line was the honest one. The patterns still in production after more than a century show a design outliving the arguments made for it.
