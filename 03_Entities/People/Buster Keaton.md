## Summary
Buster Keaton (1895 to 1966) was an American silent-film comedian, writer and director known as the deadpan "Great Stone Face," who built his comedy on engineered physical stunts he performed himself. Ranked with Charlie Chaplin among silent comedy's defining stars, he made *The General* (1926), now considered among the greatest films ever made.

## Facts
- Born in 1895 into a vaudeville family, Keaton performed from age three in the act The Three Keatons, taking falls that built his later physical control.
- He entered film in 1917 under Roscoe "Fatty" Arbuckle, and by 1920 was writing, directing and starring in his own comedies, moving into features with *Our Hospitality* (1923).
- *Sherlock Jr.* (1924) placed him inside a shifting film-dream using in-camera measurement rather than editing tricks, still studied for its daring.
- *The Navigator* (1924), about passengers adrift on an ocean liner, was his biggest commercial success of the decade.
- *The General* (1926), built around full-scale trains and a locomotive plunged off a burning bridge, flopped on release but is now ranked among cinema's greatest films.
- In *Steamboat Bill, Jr.* (1928) he performed one of the most dangerous shots ever filmed, standing where a falling house facade's open window would clear him.
- A 1928 MGM contract, taken under financial pressure, cost him creative control; the next decade brought decline, divorce and alcoholism.
- Rediscovered by critics from the 1950s, he received an honorary Academy Award in 1959 and died of lung cancer in 1966.
- His influence runs through later stunt performers such as Jackie Chan, who has cited Keaton's real, visible danger as a model.

## Context & Connections
Keaton worked in [[North America]] during the [[Industrial Age]], building comedies from the era's trains, boats and machinery. See [[Art and Ritual]] and [[Technology]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes a performer trained from infancy to fall safely, so his stunts read as a disciplined tool-lineage of bodily skill rather than improvised daring. His blank face amid engineered chaos worked as a status-signal, drawing attention by withholding the reaction expected of it. That *The General* failed on release while a later generation built the reputation it now holds shows a species revising its own memory-outsourcing of achievement well after the fact. The 1930s decline reads as plain record, unlike the reverent tone in which his stunts' zero margin for error is retold by admirers.
