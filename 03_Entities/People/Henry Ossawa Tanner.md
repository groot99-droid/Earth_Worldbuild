## Summary
Henry Ossawa Tanner (21 June 1859 to 25 May 1937) was an American painter, the first African American artist to win international fame. Frustrated by racism at home, he settled in Paris in 1891 and built his career there, painting dignified genre scenes and luminous biblical subjects.

## Facts
- Tanner was born in Pittsburgh, the eldest of nine children. His father, Benjamin Tucker Tanner, became a bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and his mother, Sarah, had escaped slavery via the Underground Railroad. At 13 he decided to become an artist.
- In 1879 he enrolled at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts and studied with Thomas Eakins. He was the only Black student and faced harassment. He left for Europe in 1891 and studied at the Académie Julian in Paris.
- On a return visit in 1893 he painted The Banjo Lesson, and he painted The Thankful Poor in 1894, both genre scenes of Black American life that challenged racist stereotypes.
- Back in France he turned to biblical subjects. The Resurrection of Lazarus won a medal at the 1897 Salon, and the French government bought it. His patron Rodman Wanamaker paid for trips to Egypt and Palestine.
- He married the singer Jessie Olsson in 1899. He was made a Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur in 1923 and died in Paris.
- He preferred a limited palette of deep blues and blue-greens with glowing gold highlights, built up in layers of glaze.
- He advised younger painters such as William Edouard Scott and Hale Woodruff, and was a hero to the artists of the Harlem Renaissance. During the Clinton administration, reportedly in 1996, his Sand Dunes at Sunset, Atlantic City became the first painting by an African American artist in the White House collection.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes a painter who left a country whose art institutions harassed him and built a career in another, where the salon judged the paintings. His two bodies of work, genre scenes of Black life and biblical scenes using ordinary people as models, both aimed at truth over caricature, and both answered a stereotype without naming it. That the first painting by an African American artist entered the White House collection only under the Clinton administration shows how slowly an institution's memory catches up with a reputation.
