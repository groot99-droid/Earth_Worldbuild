## Summary
Joseph Mallord William Turner (April 1775 to 19 December 1851) was the greatest British landscape painter and a bold colourist. He began as a precise topographical watercolourist, and his late paintings dissolve ships, trains and cities into light, mist and weather.

## Facts
- Turner was born in Covent Garden, London, the son of a barber and wigmaker. At 14 he entered the Royal Academy Schools and at 15 exhibited his first watercolour. He was elected a full Royal Academician in 1802, at 26 or 27 depending on the source.
- He travelled tirelessly in Britain, the Alps, France, Germany and Italy, especially Venice, filling hundreds of sketchbooks.
- He never married but had two daughters with Sarah Danby. John Ruskin championed him in Modern Painters (1843). He died in Chelsea and was buried in St Paul's Cathedral.
- By the 1830s he painted natural forces themselves, storms, fire, sunlight and steam. He worked thin washes over white grounds, scraped and rubbed his canvases, and applied paint thickly with knives.
- He painted steamships and railways alongside Venetian lagoons. Rain, Steam and Speed (1844) is one of the first great paintings of modern technology.
- The Slave Ship (1840) was possibly inspired by the Zong massacre of 1781, when enslaved Africans were thrown overboard so owners could claim insurance. He showed it during the 1840 campaign to end slavery worldwide.
- He left around 300 oil paintings and over 19,000 sketches, drawings and watercolours and some 200 sketchbooks to the nation, held mainly at Tate Britain. Monet and Pissarro studied his work in London in 1870 to 1871, and the Turner Prize has been awarded since 1984.

## Context & Connections
Turner painted the [[Industrial Revolution]] and its [[Railways]] in [[Europe]] during the [[Industrial Age]], and in The Slave Ship confronted the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]]. See [[Art and Ritual]] and [[Technology]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes a painter who put a steam train and a slave ship into the same body of work, the two great tool-lineages of his century, one celebrated and one condemned, both moving people and goods across water and land. Critics who mocked his late style were reading a change in how the species pictures weather and speed. That he left his work to the nation and gave a prize its name shows a personal reputation converted into an institution, a story-glue for national art.
