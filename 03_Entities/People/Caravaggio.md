## Summary
Michelangelo Merisi, called Caravaggio (1571 to 1610), was an Italian painter who transformed European painting around 1600 with harsh theatrical light and street-level realism. His turbulent life ended at 38, but his influence spread across Europe within a generation.

## Facts
- Caravaggio was born in Milan in September 1571. His name comes from the nearby town his family came from. He trained in Milan with Simone Peterzano and arrived in Rome about 1592.
- Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte took him into his household. His first public commission, two large paintings of St Matthew for the Contarelli Chapel in San Luigi dei Francesi (1599 to 1600), caused a sensation. Several later altarpieces were rejected as indecorous.
- Police records list brawls, assaults and insults. In 1606 he killed a man, Ranuccio Tomassoni, and fled Rome under sentence of death. He then lived in Naples, Malta and Sicily.
- He died in July 1610 at Porto Ercole in Tuscany, on his way to Rome hoping for a papal pardon.
- He painted directly on the canvas, apparently without preparatory drawings, from live models posed under a single strong light. His backgrounds are dark and bare (tenebrism).
- Key works include Judith Beheading Holofernes (about 1599 to 1602, sources differ), The Calling of Saint Matthew (1599 to 1600) and Supper at Emmaus (1601).
- The "Caravaggisti" included Orazio and Artemisia Gentileschi, Jusepe de Ribera, Georges de La Tour and the Utrecht painters, and through them his influence reached [[Diego Velázquez]] and [[Rembrandt van Rijn]]. A 1951 exhibition in Milan came within the twentieth-century revival of a reputation that had faded, though sources do not name it as the cause.

## Context & Connections
Caravaggio worked in Rome, Naples, Malta and Sicily in [[Europe]] during the [[Early Modern Period]]. His followers included [[Artemisia Gentileschi]]. See [[Art and Ritual]] and [[Religion and Belief]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer notes that placing sacred events in the streets of contemporary Rome, with dirty feet and hired models, was read by some patrons as truth and by others as indecorum, which shows a meaning-engine policing its own images. The violent biography is well documented in police records, which makes it easy to read the life into the pictures. His reputation faded and returned in the twentieth century, with a 1951 exhibition as one landmark of that return, so the present esteem is a recent judgment.
