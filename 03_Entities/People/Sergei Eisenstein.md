## Summary
Sergei Eisenstein (1898 to 1948) was a Soviet film director and theorist, born in Riga, who argued that meaning came from the collision between shots rather than any single image. His Odessa Steps massacre sequence in *Battleship Potemkin* (1925) remains one of cinema's most studied passages.

## Facts
- Eisenstein was born in Riga in 1898, son of an architect and civil engineer, and trained in engineering and architecture before turning to the arts.
- The Russian Revolution pushed him into theatre; he joined Meyerhold's Proletkult Theatre before moving into film in the early 1920s.
- His second feature, *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), made for the twentieth anniversary of the 1905 Revolution, brought him international fame.
- In *Strike* (1925) he pioneered "intellectual montage," cutting colliding images together and favoring crowds and non-professional "types" over trained stars.
- Invited to Hollywood then Mexico in the early 1930s, both projects went unfinished, and his 1937 Soviet film *Bezhin Meadow* was suppressed and reportedly destroyed.
- He regained official favor with *Alexander Nevsky* (1938), scored by Sergei Prokofiev, extending his editing style to sound and mass combat.
- He spent his final years on *Ivan the Terrible*; Part I won a Stalin Prize, but Part II, completed in 1946, was criticized by various authorities and went unreleased until 1958; Eisenstein died of a heart attack in Moscow in 1948.
- His writings, including *Film Form* and *The Film Sense*, became foundational film-studies texts, and he is ranked among cinema's most important directors and theorists.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer finds a subject who treated the edit itself as an argument: meaning, he held, was made not inside a single image but in the collision between two, an engineered story-glue built into a film's machinery. His preference for crowds and "type"-cast non-professionals over trained performers made the collective, not the individual, the vehicle for a revolutionary meaning-engine. The staged violence recurring through his films, a factory massacre, a staircase shooting, a battle on ice, reads as a fascination with sanctioned harm as spectacle. That the same state whose myths he helped build also suppressed one film and held back another shows how uneasily a master of persuasion sat within the system he served.

## Open Questions
- Since the negative of *Bezhin Meadow* (1937) is reported to have been destroyed after its suppression, what the finished film actually contained is now unknown.
