## Summary
The Ordovician Period, from 485.4 to 443.8 million years ago, is the second period of the Paleozoic Era. It is the interval of a great rise in marine biodiversity, the first evidence of plants on land, and, at its close, the first of the "Big Five" mass extinctions.

## Facts
- The Ordovician Period spans 485.4 to 443.8 million years ago. Its base is defined at Green Point, Newfoundland, by the conodont Iapetognathus fluctivagus, and its top at Dob's Linn in Scotland by a graptolite.
- The Great Ordovician Biodiversification, running for tens of millions of years from the early period, greatly increased the diversity of marine animals.
- Sea level was very high, and warm shallow seas held reefs, brachiopods, trilobites, crinoids and graptolites. Straight-shelled nautiloid cephalopods, some several metres long, were large predators.
- The earliest evidence of land plants, spores about 470 million years old, comes from this period; see [[Colonization of Land]].
- The climate cooled late in the period, and an ice age centred on Gondwana set in during the Hirnantian. The [[Late Ordovician Extinction]], about 445 to 443 million years ago, removed an estimated 85 percent of species, mostly marine.

## Observer's Reading
The Observer records that the Ordovician holds both a great rise in marine diversity and, near its end, a mass extinction linked to glaciation. Both arose from shifting planetary conditions, and neither was directed at any outcome. A demonstration that diversity rose steadily whatever the climate did would weaken the reading that a biosphere's history is the sum of many pushes, not a trend.

## Open Questions
- What drove the Great Ordovician Biodiversification: climate, ocean chemistry, ecological change, or some combination?
- Why did glaciation set in when carbon dioxide is estimated to have been so high?
