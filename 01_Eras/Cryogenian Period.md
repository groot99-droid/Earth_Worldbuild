## Summary
The Cryogenian Period, from 720 to 635 million years ago, is the middle period of the Neoproterozoic Era. It is the interval of the two global-scale glaciations that gave rise to the Snowball Earth hypothesis.

## Facts
- The Cryogenian Period spans 720 to 635 million years ago. Its base is set by a numerical age near the start of the first glaciation, and its top, the base of the [[Ediacaran Period]], is defined in the Flinders Ranges of South Australia.
- Two major glaciations are recorded: the Sturtian (about 717 to 660 million years ago) and the Marinoan (about 650 to 635 million years ago). Glacial deposits formed at low paleolatitudes on several continents, which supports the hypothesis that ice reached the tropics; see [[Snowball Earth]].
- Each glaciation is capped by unusual carbonate rocks. The usual explanation for the end of each is a build-up of volcanic carbon dioxide strong enough to melt the ice; this is a hypothesis.
- Banded iron formations, largely absent for well over a billion years, reappear in Sturtian-age deposits, interpreted as evidence of iron-rich seawater under the ice.
- Eukaryotes survived. Molecular fossils that some researchers attribute to sponges appear in Cryogenian rocks, but that attribution is disputed and the oldest firmly accepted animal fossils are younger.

## Context & Connections
The Cryogenian Period follows the [[Tonian Period]] and precedes the [[Ediacaran Period]] within the [[Neoproterozoic Era]]. It is the period of [[Snowball Earth]] and belongs to the theme thread of [[Earth Systems and Life]].

## Observer's Reading
The Observer treats the Cryogenian as a test of how much a living planet can lose and still continue: life survived at least one ice age lasting tens of millions of years. The Observer cautions against reading this as design, since many lineages may have ended in the ice and the survivors are the only ones the record shows. The reading would lose force if the ice proved much less extensive than the Snowball hypothesis proposes.

## Open Questions
- Was the ocean fully frozen, or did open water and refuges remain?
- What part did the glaciations play in the later rise of large complex organisms?
