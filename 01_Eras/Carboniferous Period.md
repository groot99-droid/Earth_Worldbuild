## Summary
The Carboniferous Period, from 358.9 to 298.9 million years ago, is the fifth period of the Paleozoic Era. It is named for the coal its swamp forests laid down, and it is the interval of the first amniote vertebrates and of the assembly of the supercontinent Pangaea.

## Facts
- The Carboniferous Period spans 358.9 to 298.9 million years ago. Its name means coal-bearing, and in North America it is divided into the Mississippian and Pennsylvanian.
- Swamp forests on tropical lowlands, dominated by tree-sized club-mosses such as Lepidodendron, buried the plant matter that became coal. Why so much coal formed in this period is debated. Britain's coalfields belong to it, and their coal was a major fuel of the [[Industrial Revolution]].
- Estimates for late Carboniferous atmospheric oxygen run as high as about 30 percent, against about 21 percent today, and their reliability is debated. Giant arthropods lived then, including the dragonfly relative Meganeura, with a wingspan of about 70 centimetres.
- The earliest known amniotes, animals with eggs able to develop on land, appear, including Hylonomus from Nova Scotia, about 312 million years old.
- The Late Paleozoic Ice Age, centred on Gondwana, began in this period. The coal forests collapsed about 305 million years ago as the climate dried, and the collision of Gondwana and Euramerica built much of Pangaea.
